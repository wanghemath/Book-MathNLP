27  Optimization for NLP

Gradients, stochasticity, and large-scale training

Modern NLP models are trained by solving very large optimization problems. The model may be a linear classifier, a recurrent network, a Transformer, or a retrieval-augmented system with trainable components, but the mathematical core is the same: choose parameters, define a loss, estimate a gradient from data, and update the parameters. This chapter develops optimization for NLP from a clean mathematical point of view.

27.1 22.1 Learning goals

After reading this chapter, students should be able to:

  • formulate NLP training as empirical risk minimization;
  • derive gradients for softmax cross-entropy losses;
  • distinguish full-batch gradients from stochastic mini-batch gradients;
  • explain how SGD, momentum, RMSProp, Adam, and AdamW update parameters;
  • interpret learning rate, batch size, gradient noise, clipping, and regularization mathematically;
  • understand why optimization in neural NLP is nonconvex but still useful in practice;
  • implement small optimization algorithms from scratch in Python;
  • use AI tools to audit an optimization setup without treating them as black boxes.
NoteGuiding question

When an NLP model improves during training, what mathematical object is changing?

The answer is not simply “the model learns language.” More precisely, the parameter vector \(\theta\) changes so that a loss function, usually an empirical estimate of an expected negative log-likelihood, becomes smaller on the training distribution.

27.2 22.2 The optimization problem behind NLP

Let \(z\) denote an NLP input. Depending on the task, \(z\) may be a token, a document, a sentence pair, a prompt, or a sequence prefix. Let \(y\) denote the target. A model with parameters \(\theta \in R^p\) defines either a prediction function

\[ f_\theta(z) \]

or a conditional probability distribution

\[ p_\theta(y \mid z). \]

A loss function measures the cost of a prediction. In supervised text classification, a common loss is

\[ \ell(\theta;z,y) = -\log p_\theta(y\mid z). \]

Given training data

\[ D_n = \{(z_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^n, \]

the empirical risk is

\[ L_n(\theta)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \ell(\theta;z_i,y_i). \]

Training means solving, approximately,

\[ \hat \theta \in \arg\min_{\theta \in R^p} L_n(\theta). \]

The population risk is

\[ L(\theta)=E_{(Z,Y)\sim P}\left[\ell(\theta;Z,Y)\right], \]

where \(P\) is the data-generating distribution. Since \(P\) is unknown, the empirical risk \(L_n\) is used as an estimate.

Optimization viewpoint. NLP training is the numerical problem of minimizing \(L_n(\theta)\), but statistical learning is about whether small \(L_n(\theta)\) also implies small \(L(\theta)\).

27.3 22.3 Token-level losses for language models

For autoregressive language modeling, a training sequence is

\[ w_{1:T}=(w_1,\ldots,w_T). \]

The model defines next-token probabilities

\[ p_\theta(w_t\mid w_{<t}). \]

The negative log-likelihood for one sequence is

\[ \ell(\theta;w_{1:T}) = -\sum_{t=1}^T \log p_\theta(w_t\mid w_{<t}). \]

For a corpus with sequences \(w^{(1)},\ldots,w^{(n)}\), the average token-level loss is

\[ L_n(\theta) = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{t=1}^{T_i} \log p_\theta\left(w_t^{(i)}\mid w_{<t}^{(i)}\right), \]

where

\[ N = \sum_{i=1}^n T_i. \]

The corresponding perplexity is

\[ \operatorname{PPL}(\theta)=\exp(L_n(\theta)) \]

when the loss uses natural logarithms.

This gives a direct connection between optimization and language modeling:

Lower training loss means the model assigns higher probability to the observed tokens under teacher forcing.

27.4 22.4 Softmax cross-entropy and its gradient

Many NLP objectives end with a softmax. Suppose a model produces logits

\[ z = (z_1,\ldots,z_K) \in R^K. \]

The softmax probabilities are

\[ p_j = \frac{\exp(z_j)}{\sum_{k=1}^K \exp(z_k)}. \]

For a one-hot target vector \(e_y\), the cross-entropy loss is

\[ \ell(z,y)=-\log p_y. \]

Equivalently,

\[ \ell(z,y)= -z_y + \log\left(\sum_{k=1}^K \exp(z_k)\right). \]

Differentiating gives the fundamental gradient identity

\[ \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial z_j}=p_j-1_{j=y}. \]

In vector form,

\[ \nabla_z \ell = p-e_y. \]

This identity appears throughout NLP:

  • in text classifiers;
  • in masked language models;
  • in next-token prediction;
  • in instruction tuning;
  • in preference losses after reduction to logits.
TipInterpretation

The gradient \(p-e_y\) says: decrease the logit of classes assigned too much probability and increase the logit of the correct class. The size of the correction depends on the model’s current probability error.

27.5 22.5 From logits to parameter gradients

Let the logits depend on parameters \(\theta\) through a differentiable map

\[ z=f_\theta(x). \]

The chain rule gives

\[ \nabla_\theta \ell(\theta;x,y) = J_\theta f_\theta(x)^T (p-e_y), \]

where \(J_\theta f_\theta(x)\) is the Jacobian of the logits with respect to the parameters.

For a linear softmax classifier,

\[ z = Wh+b, \]

where \(h \in R^d\), \(W \in R^{K\times d}\), and \(b \in R^K\). Then

\[ \nabla_W \ell = (p-e_y)h^T, \]

\[ \nabla_b \ell = p-e_y, \]

and

\[ \nabla_h \ell = W^T(p-e_y). \]

This small calculation is the local building block of backpropagation.

27.6 22.6 Gradient descent

Gradient descent updates parameters by moving in the direction of steepest local decrease:

\[ \theta_{k+1}=\theta_k - \eta_k \nabla L_n(\theta_k), \]

where \(\eta_k>0\) is the learning rate.

For a quadratic loss

\[ L(\theta)=\frac{1}{2}\theta^T A \theta - b^T \theta, \]

with \(A\) symmetric positive definite, the gradient is

\[ \nabla L(\theta)=A \theta-b. \]

Gradient descent converges when the learning rate is small enough. If \(\lambda_{\max}(A)\) is the largest eigenvalue of \(A\), then a constant learning rate satisfying

\[ 0<\eta<\frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}(A)} \]

ensures convergence for this quadratic problem.

This idealized result matters because neural-network losses are locally approximated by quadratic functions through Taylor expansion:

\[ L(\theta+u)\approx L(\theta)+\nabla L(\theta)^T u + \frac{1}{2}u^T H(\theta)u. \]

Large curvature directions require smaller steps.

27.7 22.7 Stochastic gradients

For large NLP corpora, the full gradient

\[ \nabla L_n(\theta)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \nabla \ell(\theta;z_i,y_i) \]

is expensive. Mini-batch stochastic gradient descent uses a random subset \(B_k\subset \{1,\ldots,n\}\):

\[ g_k=\frac{1}{|B_k|}\sum_{i\in B_k}\nabla \ell(\theta_k;z_i,y_i). \]

If the mini-batch is sampled uniformly, then

\[ E[g_k\mid \theta_k]=\nabla L_n(\theta_k). \]

Thus \(g_k\) is an unbiased estimator of the full gradient. Its variance decreases as the batch size increases. Under idealized independent sampling,

\[ \operatorname{Var}(g_k)\approx \frac{1}{B}\Sigma(\theta_k), \]

where \(B=|B_k|\) and \(\Sigma(\theta_k)\) is the covariance matrix of per-example gradients.

The SGD update is

\[ \theta_{k+1}=\theta_k-\eta_k g_k. \]

Statistical view of SGD. SGD replaces an exact derivative by a noisy but cheaper estimator. The learning rate controls how much of this noise is converted into parameter motion.

27.8 22.8 Learning rate as a scale parameter

The learning rate is not merely a software option. It determines the scale of parameter movement:

\[ \|\theta_{k+1}-\theta_k\| = \eta_k \|g_k\|. \]

If \(\eta_k\) is too small, learning is slow. If it is too large, the iterates may oscillate or diverge. In neural NLP, useful learning-rate schedules often include:

  1. warmup, where the learning rate increases gradually at the beginning;
  2. constant phase, where the learning rate remains approximately stable;
  3. decay, where the learning rate decreases to refine the solution.

A simple inverse-square-root schedule is

\[ \eta_k = c\min\{k^{-1/2}, k w^{-3/2}\}, \]

where \(w\) is the warmup length. A cosine decay schedule is

\[ \eta_k = \eta_{\min} +\frac{1}{2}(\eta_{\max}-\eta_{\min}) \left(1+\cos\left(\frac{\pi k}{K}\right)\right). \]

The mathematical purpose of a schedule is to balance exploration and stabilization.

27.9 22.9 Momentum

Momentum keeps a running average of past gradients:

\[ v_{k+1}=\mu v_k + g_k, \]

\[ \theta_{k+1}=\theta_k-\eta v_{k+1}, \]

where \(0\leq \mu <1\). When gradients point in a consistent direction, momentum accelerates movement. When gradients oscillate, momentum can average out some oscillation.

For a long sequence of gradients, the velocity is

\[ v_{k+1}=g_k+\mu g_{k-1}+\mu^2 g_{k-2}+\cdots. \]

Thus momentum is an exponentially weighted history of gradients.

27.10 22.10 Adaptive methods: AdaGrad, RMSProp, and Adam

NLP models often have sparse or highly uneven gradients. Some parameters update frequently; others update rarely. Adaptive methods rescale coordinates based on historical gradient magnitudes.

27.10.1 AdaGrad

AdaGrad accumulates squared gradients coordinatewise:

\[ s_{k+1}=s_k+g_k\odot g_k, \]

\[ \theta_{k+1}=\theta_k-\eta \frac{g_k}{\sqrt{s_{k+1}}+\epsilon}. \]

Coordinates with large accumulated gradients receive smaller future steps.

27.10.2 RMSProp

RMSProp uses an exponential moving average instead of a cumulative sum:

\[ s_{k+1}=\rho s_k+(1-\rho)(g_k\odot g_k), \]

\[ \theta_{k+1}=\theta_k-\eta \frac{g_k}{\sqrt{s_{k+1}}+\epsilon}. \]

27.10.3 Adam

Adam combines momentum and coordinatewise scaling:

\[ m_{k+1}=\beta_1 m_k+(1-\beta_1)g_k, \]

\[ v_{k+1}=\beta_2 v_k+(1-\beta_2)(g_k\odot g_k). \]

Bias-corrected estimates are

\[ \hat m_{k+1}=\frac{m_{k+1}}{1-\beta_1^{k+1}}, \qquad \hat v_{k+1}=\frac{v_{k+1}}{1-\beta_2^{k+1}}. \]

The update is

\[ \theta_{k+1}=\theta_k-\eta\frac{\hat m_{k+1}}{\sqrt{\hat v_{k+1}}+\epsilon}. \]

Adam is popular in neural NLP because it handles noisy, high-dimensional, and unevenly scaled gradients well.

27.11 22.11 Weight decay and AdamW

A common regularized objective is

\[ L_{\lambda}(\theta)=L_n(\theta)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\theta\|_2^2. \]

For ordinary gradient descent, this gives

\[ \theta_{k+1} =\theta_k-\eta \left(\nabla L_n(\theta_k)+\lambda \theta_k\right) =(1-\eta\lambda)\theta_k-\eta\nabla L_n(\theta_k). \]

The regularizer shrinks parameters toward zero.

For adaptive optimizers, directly adding \(\lambda \theta\) to the gradient interacts with coordinatewise scaling. AdamW decouples weight decay from the adaptive gradient step:

\[ \theta_{k+1} =(1-\eta\lambda)\theta_k -\eta\frac{\hat m_{k+1}}{\sqrt{\hat v_{k+1}}+\epsilon}. \]

This is often a cleaner implementation of shrinkage for Transformer models.

27.12 22.12 Gradient clipping

RNNs and Transformers can produce very large gradients. Gradient clipping limits the step size by replacing \(g\) with

\[ \tilde g = g\min\left\{1,\frac{c}{\|g\|}\right\}. \]

If \(\|g\|\leq c\), then \(\tilde g=g\). If \(\|g\|>c\), then \(\|\tilde g\|=c\).

Clipping is not the same as fixing the model. It is a numerical stabilization method. It prevents one unusually large mini-batch gradient from causing a destructive update.

27.13 22.13 Optimization and generalization

Optimization minimizes training loss, but the goal is usually good performance on new data. A model with very low empirical risk can still have high population risk if it overfits.

Important sources of implicit or explicit regularization include:

  • weight decay;
  • dropout;
  • early stopping;
  • data augmentation;
  • label smoothing;
  • small-batch gradient noise;
  • learning-rate schedules;
  • architecture constraints;
  • parameter-efficient fine-tuning.

From a statistical point of view, the optimizer is part of the learning algorithm. Two optimizers can reach different solutions with similar training losses but different generalization behavior.

27.14 22.14 Nonconvexity in neural NLP

Linear regression with squared loss is convex. Logistic regression is convex in its parameters. Neural NLP models are typically nonconvex because they are compositions of many nonlinear maps:

\[ f_\theta = f_L\circ f_{L-1}\circ \cdots \circ f_1. \]

The loss surface may contain many critical points where

\[ \nabla L_n(\theta)=0. \]

These can be local minima, saddle points, or flat regions. In high-dimensional neural networks, saddle points and nearly flat valleys are especially important.

A second-order Taylor approximation near \(\theta\) is

\[ L_n(\theta+u) \approx L_n(\theta)+\nabla L_n(\theta)^Tu+\frac{1}{2}u^T H(\theta)u. \]

The Hessian eigenvalues describe local curvature. Large positive eigenvalues indicate sharp directions. Small eigenvalues indicate flat directions. Negative eigenvalues indicate directions of local decrease.

In modern NLP, the parameter dimension \(p\) is often much larger than the number of human-interpretable modeling choices. Therefore optimization analysis focuses less on finding the unique global minimizer and more on stability, scaling, gradient quality, and generalization.

27.15 22.15 Numerical stability

Optimization can fail because of numerical issues, not because the mathematical model is conceptually wrong. Common stability tricks include:

27.15.1 Stable log-sum-exp

Instead of computing

\[ \log\sum_j \exp(z_j), \]

use

\[ m + \log\sum_j \exp(z_j-m), \qquad m=\max_j z_j. \]

This avoids overflow when logits are large.

27.15.2 Loss masks

For padded sequences, the loss should ignore padding tokens. If \(M_{i,t}\in\{0,1\}\) is a mask, then

\[ L(\theta)= -\frac{1}{\sum_{i,t} M_{i,t}} \sum_{i,t} M_{i,t} \log p_\theta(w_{i,t}\mid w_{i,<t}). \]

27.15.3 Mixed precision

Large models often use lower precision arithmetic to save memory and increase speed. This introduces a tradeoff between numerical efficiency and stability. Loss scaling and careful normalization help prevent underflow and overflow.

27.17 22.17 Python example: softmax regression gradient


This example derives and checks the gradient for a small text classifier. The model is


\[
z_i = W x_i + b,
\qquad
p_i=\operatorname{softmax}(z_i),
\]


with average cross-entropy loss.




import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(7243)

X = np.array([
    [2, 1, 0, 0],
    [1, 2, 0, 0],
    [0, 0, 2, 1],
    [0, 0, 1, 2],
    [1, 0, 1, 0],
    [0, 1, 0, 1]
], dtype=float)
y = np.array([0, 0, 1, 1, 0, 1])
K = 2
n, d = X.shape

W = rng.normal(scale=0.1, size=(K, d))
b = np.zeros(K)

def softmax_rows(Z):
    Z_shift = Z - Z.max(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    expZ = np.exp(Z_shift)
    return expZ / expZ.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)

def loss_and_grad(W, b, X, y):
    n = X.shape[0]
    Z = X @ W.T + b
    P = softmax_rows(Z)
    loss = -np.mean(np.log(P[np.arange(n), y]))
    G = P.copy()
    G[np.arange(n), y] -= 1.0
    G /= n
    grad_W = G.T @ X
    grad_b = G.sum(axis=0)
    return loss, grad_W, grad_b

loss, grad_W, grad_b = loss_and_grad(W, b, X, y)
print("loss:", round(loss, 6))
print("grad_W shape:", grad_W.shape)
print("grad_b:", np.round(grad_b, 6))

27.18 22.18 Python example: finite-difference gradient check


A gradient check compares an analytic derivative with a numerical approximation:


\[
\frac{\partial L}{\partial \theta_j}
\approx
\frac{L(\theta+\epsilon e_j)-L(\theta-\epsilon e_j)}{2\epsilon}.
\]




eps = 1e-5
j, k = 0, 2
W_plus = W.copy()
W_minus = W.copy()
W_plus[j, k] += eps
W_minus[j, k] -= eps
loss_plus, _, _ = loss_and_grad(W_plus, b, X, y)
loss_minus, _, _ = loss_and_grad(W_minus, b, X, y)
finite_difference = (loss_plus - loss_minus) / (2 * eps)
analytic = grad_W[j, k]

print("finite difference:", round(finite_difference, 8))
print("analytic gradient:", round(analytic, 8))
print("absolute error:", abs(finite_difference - analytic))

27.19 22.19 Python example: GD, momentum, and Adam on a quadratic


The next example mirrors the interactive figure.




import numpy as np

A = np.diag([1.0, 20.0])

def quad_loss(theta):
    return 0.5 * theta @ A @ theta

def quad_grad(theta):
    return A @ theta

def optimize(method="gd", lr=0.08, steps=60, beta=0.9):
    theta = np.array([2.8, 2.2], dtype=float)
    path = [theta.copy()]
    losses = [quad_loss(theta)]
    m = np.zeros_like(theta)
    v = np.zeros_like(theta)
    beta1, beta2 = 0.9, 0.99
    eps = 1e-8
    for t in range(1, steps + 1):
        g = quad_grad(theta)
        if method == "gd":
            theta = theta - lr * g
        elif method == "momentum":
            m = beta * m + g
            theta = theta - lr * m
        elif method == "adam":
            m = beta1 * m + (1 - beta1) * g
            v = beta2 * v + (1 - beta2) * (g * g)
            m_hat = m / (1 - beta1 ** t)
            v_hat = v / (1 - beta2 ** t)
            theta = theta - lr * m_hat / (np.sqrt(v_hat) + eps)
        path.append(theta.copy())
        losses.append(quad_loss(theta))
    return np.array(path), np.array(losses)

for method in ["gd", "momentum", "adam"]:
    path, losses = optimize(method=method)
    print(f"{method:8s} final theta = {np.round(path[-1], 4)}, final loss = {losses[-1]:.6f}")

27.20 22.20 Python example: mini-batch gradient noise


Mini-batch gradients are random estimates of the full gradient. The variance decreases with batch size.




rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)
N = 1000
true_grad = np.array([1.0, -0.5, 0.25])
per_example_grads = true_grad + rng.normal(scale=[2.0, 1.0, 0.5], size=(N, 3))
full_grad = per_example_grads.mean(axis=0)

for B in [1, 4, 16, 64, 256]:
    estimates = []
    for _ in range(500):
        idx = rng.choice(N, size=B, replace=False)
        estimates.append(per_example_grads[idx].mean(axis=0))
    estimates = np.array(estimates)
    avg_error_norm = np.linalg.norm(estimates - full_grad, axis=1).mean()
    print(f"batch size {B:3d}: mean gradient-estimation error norm = {avg_error_norm:.4f}")

27.21 22.21 Python example: gradient clipping




def clip_gradient(g, threshold):
    norm = np.linalg.norm(g)
    if norm <= threshold:
        return g.copy()
    return g * (threshold / norm)

gradients = [
    np.array([0.2, -0.1, 0.3]),
    np.array([5.0, -4.0, 3.0]),
    np.array([40.0, -20.0, 10.0])
]
threshold = 2.0

for g in gradients:
    clipped = clip_gradient(g, threshold)
    print("original norm:", round(np.linalg.norm(g), 3),
          " clipped norm:", round(np.linalg.norm(clipped), 3),
          " clipped vector:", np.round(clipped, 3))

27.22 22.22 Python example: training a tiny text classifier


The following code trains a softmax classifier using averaged token-count features. This is not a state-of-the-art NLP model. Its purpose is to show the complete optimization loop in a transparent way.




from collections import Counter
import numpy as np

train_texts = [
    "excellent clear helpful explanation",
    "useful clear lecture helpful",
    "confusing unclear poor explanation",
    "poor confusing lecture unclear",
    "clear useful examples",
    "unclear poor examples"
]
train_y = np.array([1, 1, 0, 0, 1, 0])

vocab = sorted(set(word for text in train_texts for word in text.split()))
word_to_id = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(vocab)}

X = np.zeros((len(train_texts), len(vocab)))
for i, text in enumerate(train_texts):
    counts = Counter(text.split())
    for word, count in counts.items():
        X[i, word_to_id[word]] = count
    X[i] /= max(1, X[i].sum())

rng = np.random.default_rng(11)

::: {.chapter-opening}
Modern NLP models are trained by solving very large optimization problems.  The model may be a linear classifier, a recurrent network, a Transformer, or a retrieval-augmented system with trainable components, but the mathematical core is the same: choose parameters, define a loss, estimate a gradient from data, and update the parameters.  This chapter develops optimization for NLP from a clean mathematical point of view.
:::

## 22.1 Learning goals

After reading this chapter, students should be able to:

- formulate NLP training as empirical risk minimization;
- derive gradients for softmax cross-entropy losses;
- distinguish full-batch gradients from stochastic mini-batch gradients;
- explain how SGD, momentum, RMSProp, Adam, and AdamW update parameters;
- interpret learning rate, batch size, gradient noise, clipping, and regularization mathematically;
- understand why optimization in neural NLP is nonconvex but still useful in practice;
- implement small optimization algorithms from scratch in Python;
- use AI tools to audit an optimization setup without treating them as black boxes.

::: {.callout-note}
## Guiding question
When an NLP model improves during training, what mathematical object is changing?

The answer is not simply "the model learns language."  More precisely, the parameter vector $\theta$ changes so that a loss function, usually an empirical estimate of an expected negative log-likelihood, becomes smaller on the training distribution.
:::

## 22.2 The optimization problem behind NLP

Let $z$ denote an NLP input.  Depending on the task, $z$ may be a token, a document, a sentence pair, a prompt, or a sequence prefix.  Let $y$ denote the target.  A model with parameters $\theta \in R^p$ defines either a prediction function

$$
f_\theta(z)
$$

or a conditional probability distribution

$$
p_\theta(y \mid z).
$$

A loss function measures the cost of a prediction.  In supervised text classification, a common loss is

$$
\ell(\theta;z,y) = -\log p_\theta(y\mid z).
$$

Given training data

$$
D_n = \{(z_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^n,
$$

the empirical risk is

$$
L_n(\theta)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \ell(\theta;z_i,y_i).
$$

Training means solving, approximately,

$$
\hat \theta \in \arg\min_{\theta \in R^p} L_n(\theta).
$$

The population risk is

$$
L(\theta)=E_{(Z,Y)\sim P}\left[\ell(\theta;Z,Y)\right],
$$

where $P$ is the data-generating distribution.  Since $P$ is unknown, the empirical risk $L_n$ is used as an estimate.

::: {.math-card}
**Optimization viewpoint.**  NLP training is the numerical problem of minimizing $L_n(\theta)$, but statistical learning is about whether small $L_n(\theta)$ also implies small $L(\theta)$.
:::

## 22.3 Token-level losses for language models

For autoregressive language modeling, a training sequence is

$$
w_{1:T}=(w_1,\ldots,w_T).
$$

The model defines next-token probabilities

$$
p_\theta(w_t\mid w_{<t}).
$$

The negative log-likelihood for one sequence is

$$
\ell(\theta;w_{1:T})
= -\sum_{t=1}^T \log p_\theta(w_t\mid w_{<t}).
$$

For a corpus with sequences $w^{(1)},\ldots,w^{(n)}$, the average token-level loss is

$$
L_n(\theta)
= -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{t=1}^{T_i}
\log p_\theta\left(w_t^{(i)}\mid w_{<t}^{(i)}\right),
$$

where

$$
N = \sum_{i=1}^n T_i.
$$

The corresponding perplexity is

$$
\operatorname{PPL}(\theta)=\exp(L_n(\theta))
$$

when the loss uses natural logarithms.

This gives a direct connection between optimization and language modeling:

> Lower training loss means the model assigns higher probability to the observed tokens under teacher forcing.

## 22.4 Softmax cross-entropy and its gradient

Many NLP objectives end with a softmax.  Suppose a model produces logits

$$
z = (z_1,\ldots,z_K) \in R^K.
$$

The softmax probabilities are

$$
p_j = \frac{\exp(z_j)}{\sum_{k=1}^K \exp(z_k)}.
$$

For a one-hot target vector $e_y$, the cross-entropy loss is

$$
\ell(z,y)=-\log p_y.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
\ell(z,y)= -z_y + \log\left(\sum_{k=1}^K \exp(z_k)\right).
$$

Differentiating gives the fundamental gradient identity

$$
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial z_j}=p_j-1_{j=y}.
$$

In vector form,

$$
\nabla_z \ell = p-e_y.
$$

This identity appears throughout NLP:

- in text classifiers;
- in masked language models;
- in next-token prediction;
- in instruction tuning;
- in preference losses after reduction to logits.

::: {.callout-tip}
## Interpretation
The gradient $p-e_y$ says: decrease the logit of classes assigned too much probability and increase the logit of the correct class.  The size of the correction depends on the model's current probability error.
:::

## 22.5 From logits to parameter gradients

Let the logits depend on parameters $\theta$ through a differentiable map

$$
z=f_\theta(x).
$$

The chain rule gives

$$
\nabla_\theta \ell(\theta;x,y)
= J_\theta f_\theta(x)^T (p-e_y),
$$

where $J_\theta f_\theta(x)$ is the Jacobian of the logits with respect to the parameters.

For a linear softmax classifier,

$$
z = Wh+b,
$$

where $h \in R^d$, $W \in R^{K\times d}$, and $b \in R^K$.  Then

$$
\nabla_W \ell = (p-e_y)h^T,
$$

$$
\nabla_b \ell = p-e_y,
$$

and

$$
\nabla_h \ell = W^T(p-e_y).
$$

This small calculation is the local building block of backpropagation.

## 22.6 Gradient descent

Gradient descent updates parameters by moving in the direction of steepest local decrease:

$$
\theta_{k+1}=\theta_k - \eta_k \nabla L_n(\theta_k),
$$

where $\eta_k>0$ is the learning rate.

For a quadratic loss

$$
L(\theta)=\frac{1}{2}\theta^T A \theta - b^T \theta,
$$

with $A$ symmetric positive definite, the gradient is

$$
\nabla L(\theta)=A \theta-b.
$$

Gradient descent converges when the learning rate is small enough.  If $\lambda_{\max}(A)$ is the largest eigenvalue of $A$, then a constant learning rate satisfying

$$
0<\eta<\frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}(A)}
$$

ensures convergence for this quadratic problem.

This idealized result matters because neural-network losses are locally approximated by quadratic functions through Taylor expansion:

$$
L(\theta+u)\approx L(\theta)+\nabla L(\theta)^T u + \frac{1}{2}u^T H(\theta)u.
$$

Large curvature directions require smaller steps.

## 22.7 Stochastic gradients

For large NLP corpora, the full gradient

$$
\nabla L_n(\theta)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \nabla \ell(\theta;z_i,y_i)
$$

is expensive.  Mini-batch stochastic gradient descent uses a random subset $B_k\subset \{1,\ldots,n\}$:

$$
g_k=\frac{1}{|B_k|}\sum_{i\in B_k}\nabla \ell(\theta_k;z_i,y_i).
$$

If the mini-batch is sampled uniformly, then

$$
E[g_k\mid \theta_k]=\nabla L_n(\theta_k).
$$

Thus $g_k$ is an unbiased estimator of the full gradient.  Its variance decreases as the batch size increases.  Under idealized independent sampling,

$$
\operatorname{Var}(g_k)\approx \frac{1}{B}\Sigma(\theta_k),
$$

where $B=|B_k|$ and $\Sigma(\theta_k)$ is the covariance matrix of per-example gradients.

The SGD update is

$$
\theta_{k+1}=\theta_k-\eta_k g_k.
$$

::: {.math-card}
**Statistical view of SGD.** SGD replaces an exact derivative by a noisy but cheaper estimator.  The learning rate controls how much of this noise is converted into parameter motion.
:::

## 22.8 Learning rate as a scale parameter

The learning rate is not merely a software option.  It determines the scale of parameter movement:

$$
\|\theta_{k+1}-\theta_k\| = \eta_k \|g_k\|.
$$

If $\eta_k$ is too small, learning is slow.  If it is too large, the iterates may oscillate or diverge.  In neural NLP, useful learning-rate schedules often include:

1. **warmup**, where the learning rate increases gradually at the beginning;
2. **constant phase**, where the learning rate remains approximately stable;
3. **decay**, where the learning rate decreases to refine the solution.

A simple inverse-square-root schedule is

$$
\eta_k = c\min\{k^{-1/2}, k w^{-3/2}\},
$$

where $w$ is the warmup length.  A cosine decay schedule is

$$
\eta_k = \eta_{\min}
+\frac{1}{2}(\eta_{\max}-\eta_{\min})
\left(1+\cos\left(\frac{\pi k}{K}\right)\right).
$$

The mathematical purpose of a schedule is to balance exploration and stabilization.

## 22.9 Momentum

Momentum keeps a running average of past gradients:

$$
v_{k+1}=\mu v_k + g_k,
$$

$$
\theta_{k+1}=\theta_k-\eta v_{k+1},
$$

where $0\leq \mu <1$.  When gradients point in a consistent direction, momentum accelerates movement.  When gradients oscillate, momentum can average out some oscillation.

For a long sequence of gradients, the velocity is

$$
v_{k+1}=g_k+\mu g_{k-1}+\mu^2 g_{k-2}+\cdots.
$$

Thus momentum is an exponentially weighted history of gradients.

## 22.10 Adaptive methods: AdaGrad, RMSProp, and Adam

NLP models often have sparse or highly uneven gradients.  Some parameters update frequently; others update rarely.  Adaptive methods rescale coordinates based on historical gradient magnitudes.

### AdaGrad

AdaGrad accumulates squared gradients coordinatewise:

$$
s_{k+1}=s_k+g_k\odot g_k,
$$

$$
\theta_{k+1}=\theta_k-\eta \frac{g_k}{\sqrt{s_{k+1}}+\epsilon}.
$$

Coordinates with large accumulated gradients receive smaller future steps.

### RMSProp

RMSProp uses an exponential moving average instead of a cumulative sum:

$$
s_{k+1}=\rho s_k+(1-\rho)(g_k\odot g_k),
$$

$$
\theta_{k+1}=\theta_k-\eta \frac{g_k}{\sqrt{s_{k+1}}+\epsilon}.
$$

### Adam

Adam combines momentum and coordinatewise scaling:

$$
m_{k+1}=\beta_1 m_k+(1-\beta_1)g_k,
$$

$$
v_{k+1}=\beta_2 v_k+(1-\beta_2)(g_k\odot g_k).
$$

Bias-corrected estimates are

$$
\hat m_{k+1}=\frac{m_{k+1}}{1-\beta_1^{k+1}},
\qquad
\hat v_{k+1}=\frac{v_{k+1}}{1-\beta_2^{k+1}}.
$$

The update is

$$
\theta_{k+1}=\theta_k-\eta\frac{\hat m_{k+1}}{\sqrt{\hat v_{k+1}}+\epsilon}.
$$

Adam is popular in neural NLP because it handles noisy, high-dimensional, and unevenly scaled gradients well.

## 22.11 Weight decay and AdamW

A common regularized objective is

$$
L_{\lambda}(\theta)=L_n(\theta)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\theta\|_2^2.
$$

For ordinary gradient descent, this gives

$$
\theta_{k+1}
=\theta_k-\eta \left(\nabla L_n(\theta_k)+\lambda \theta_k\right)
=(1-\eta\lambda)\theta_k-\eta\nabla L_n(\theta_k).
$$

The regularizer shrinks parameters toward zero.

For adaptive optimizers, directly adding $\lambda \theta$ to the gradient interacts with coordinatewise scaling.  AdamW decouples weight decay from the adaptive gradient step:

$$
\theta_{k+1}
=(1-\eta\lambda)\theta_k
-\eta\frac{\hat m_{k+1}}{\sqrt{\hat v_{k+1}}+\epsilon}.
$$

This is often a cleaner implementation of shrinkage for Transformer models.

## 22.12 Gradient clipping

RNNs and Transformers can produce very large gradients.  Gradient clipping limits the step size by replacing $g$ with

$$
\tilde g = g\min\left\{1,\frac{c}{\|g\|}\right\}.
$$

If $\|g\|\leq c$, then $\tilde g=g$.  If $\|g\|>c$, then $\|\tilde g\|=c$.

Clipping is not the same as fixing the model.  It is a numerical stabilization method.  It prevents one unusually large mini-batch gradient from causing a destructive update.

## 22.13 Optimization and generalization

Optimization minimizes training loss, but the goal is usually good performance on new data.  A model with very low empirical risk can still have high population risk if it overfits.

Important sources of implicit or explicit regularization include:

- weight decay;
- dropout;
- early stopping;
- data augmentation;
- label smoothing;
- small-batch gradient noise;
- learning-rate schedules;
- architecture constraints;
- parameter-efficient fine-tuning.

From a statistical point of view, the optimizer is part of the learning algorithm.  Two optimizers can reach different solutions with similar training losses but different generalization behavior.

## 22.14 Nonconvexity in neural NLP

Linear regression with squared loss is convex.  Logistic regression is convex in its parameters.  Neural NLP models are typically nonconvex because they are compositions of many nonlinear maps:

$$
f_\theta = f_L\circ f_{L-1}\circ \cdots \circ f_1.
$$

The loss surface may contain many critical points where

$$
\nabla L_n(\theta)=0.
$$

These can be local minima, saddle points, or flat regions.  In high-dimensional neural networks, saddle points and nearly flat valleys are especially important.

A second-order Taylor approximation near $\theta$ is

$$
L_n(\theta+u)
\approx L_n(\theta)+\nabla L_n(\theta)^Tu+\frac{1}{2}u^T H(\theta)u.
$$

The Hessian eigenvalues describe local curvature.  Large positive eigenvalues indicate sharp directions.  Small eigenvalues indicate flat directions.  Negative eigenvalues indicate directions of local decrease.

In modern NLP, the parameter dimension $p$ is often much larger than the number of human-interpretable modeling choices.  Therefore optimization analysis focuses less on finding the unique global minimizer and more on stability, scaling, gradient quality, and generalization.

## 22.15 Numerical stability

Optimization can fail because of numerical issues, not because the mathematical model is conceptually wrong.  Common stability tricks include:

### Stable log-sum-exp

Instead of computing

$$
\log\sum_j \exp(z_j),
$$

use

$$
m + \log\sum_j \exp(z_j-m),
\qquad m=\max_j z_j.
$$

This avoids overflow when logits are large.

### Loss masks

For padded sequences, the loss should ignore padding tokens.  If $M_{i,t}\in\{0,1\}$ is a mask, then

$$
L(\theta)=
-\frac{1}{\sum_{i,t} M_{i,t}}
\sum_{i,t} M_{i,t}
\log p_\theta(w_{i,t}\mid w_{i,<t}).
$$

### Mixed precision

Large models often use lower precision arithmetic to save memory and increase speed.  This introduces a tradeoff between numerical efficiency and stability.  Loss scaling and careful normalization help prevent underflow and overflow.

    return 0.5 * (a * point[0] * point[0] + b * point[1] * point[1]);
  }

  function grad(point, a, b) {
    return [a * point[0], b * point[1]];
  }

  function runGD(lr, steps, a, b) {
    let p = [2.8, 2.2];
    const path = [[p[0], p[1]]];
    const losses = [loss(p, a, b)];
    for (let k = 0; k < steps; k++) {
      const g = grad(p, a, b);
      p = [p[0] - lr * g[0], p[1] - lr * g[1]];
      path.push([p[0], p[1]]);
      losses.push(loss(p, a, b));
      if (!Number.isFinite(losses[losses.length - 1]) || losses[losses.length - 1] > 1e8) break;
    }
    return {path: path, losses: losses};
  }

  function runMomentum(lr, beta, steps, a, b) {
    let p = [2.8, 2.2];
    let v = [0, 0];
    const path = [[p[0], p[1]]];
    const losses = [loss(p, a, b)];
    for (let k = 0; k < steps; k++) {
      const g = grad(p, a, b);
      v = [beta * v[0] + g[0], beta * v[1] + g[1]];
      p = [p[0] - lr * v[0], p[1] - lr * v[1]];
      path.push([p[0], p[1]]);
      losses.push(loss(p, a, b));
      if (!Number.isFinite(losses[losses.length - 1]) || losses[losses.length - 1] > 1e8) break;
    }
    return {path: path, losses: losses};
  }

  function runAdam(lr, steps, a, b) {
    let p = [2.8, 2.2];
    let m = [0, 0];
    let v = [0, 0];
    const beta1 = 0.9;
    const beta2 = 0.99;
    const eps = 1e-8;
    const path = [[p[0], p[1]]];
    const losses = [loss(p, a, b)];
    for (let k = 0; k < steps; k++) {
      const g = grad(p, a, b);
      m = [beta1 * m[0] + (1 - beta1) * g[0], beta1 * m[1] + (1 - beta1) * g[1]];
      v = [beta2 * v[0] + (1 - beta2) * g[0] * g[0], beta2 * v[1] + (1 - beta2) * g[1] * g[1]];
      const mh = [m[0] / (1 - Math.pow(beta1, k + 1)), m[1] / (1 - Math.pow(beta1, k + 1))];
      const vh = [v[0] / (1 - Math.pow(beta2, k + 1)), v[1] / (1 - Math.pow(beta2, k + 1))];
      p = [p[0] - lr * mh[0] / (Math.sqrt(vh[0]) + eps), p[1] - lr * mh[1] / (Math.sqrt(vh[1]) + eps)];
      path.push([p[0], p[1]]);
      losses.push(loss(p, a, b));
      if (!Number.isFinite(losses[losses.length - 1]) || losses[losses.length - 1] > 1e8) break;
    }
    return {path: path, losses: losses};
  }

## 22.17 Python example: softmax regression gradient

This example derives and checks the gradient for a small text classifier.  The model is

$$
z_i = W x_i + b,
\qquad
p_i=\operatorname{softmax}(z_i),
$$

with average cross-entropy loss.

```{python}
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(7243)

X = np.array([
    [2, 1, 0, 0],
    [1, 2, 0, 0],
    [0, 0, 2, 1],
    [0, 0, 1, 2],
    [1, 0, 1, 0],
    [0, 1, 0, 1]
], dtype=float)
y = np.array([0, 0, 1, 1, 0, 1])
K = 2
n, d = X.shape

W = rng.normal(scale=0.1, size=(K, d))
b = np.zeros(K)

def softmax_rows(Z):
    Z_shift = Z - Z.max(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    expZ = np.exp(Z_shift)
    return expZ / expZ.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)

def loss_and_grad(W, b, X, y):
    n = X.shape[0]
    Z = X @ W.T + b
    P = softmax_rows(Z)
    loss = -np.mean(np.log(P[np.arange(n), y]))
    G = P.copy()
    G[np.arange(n), y] -= 1.0
    G /= n
    grad_W = G.T @ X
    grad_b = G.sum(axis=0)
    return loss, grad_W, grad_b

loss, grad_W, grad_b = loss_and_grad(W, b, X, y)
print("loss:", round(loss, 6))
print("grad_W shape:", grad_W.shape)
print("grad_b:", np.round(grad_b, 6))
```

The shapes are important:

$$
X\in R^{n\times d},
\qquad
W\in R^{K\times d},
\qquad
\nabla_W L\in R^{K\times d}.
$$

## 22.18 Python example: finite-difference gradient check

A gradient check compares an analytic derivative with a numerical approximation:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial \theta_j}
\approx
\frac{L(\theta+\epsilon e_j)-L(\theta-\epsilon e_j)}{2\epsilon}.
$$

```{python}
eps = 1e-5
j, k = 0, 2
W_plus = W.copy()
W_minus = W.copy()
W_plus[j, k] += eps
W_minus[j, k] -= eps
loss_plus, _, _ = loss_and_grad(W_plus, b, X, y)
loss_minus, _, _ = loss_and_grad(W_minus, b, X, y)
finite_difference = (loss_plus - loss_minus) / (2 * eps)
analytic = grad_W[j, k]

print("finite difference:", round(finite_difference, 8))
print("analytic gradient:", round(analytic, 8))
print("absolute error:", abs(finite_difference - analytic))
```

Gradient checking is useful when writing custom training code.  It is too expensive for normal training, but it is excellent for debugging.

## 22.19 Python example: GD, momentum, and Adam on a quadratic

The next example mirrors the interactive figure.

```{python}
import numpy as np

A = np.diag([1.0, 20.0])

def quad_loss(theta):
    return 0.5 * theta @ A @ theta

def quad_grad(theta):
    return A @ theta

def optimize(method="gd", lr=0.08, steps=60, beta=0.9):
    theta = np.array([2.8, 2.2], dtype=float)
    path = [theta.copy()]
    losses = [quad_loss(theta)]
    m = np.zeros_like(theta)
    v = np.zeros_like(theta)
    beta1, beta2 = 0.9, 0.99
    eps = 1e-8
    for t in range(1, steps + 1):
        g = quad_grad(theta)
        if method == "gd":
            theta = theta - lr * g
        elif method == "momentum":
            m = beta * m + g
            theta = theta - lr * m
        elif method == "adam":
            m = beta1 * m + (1 - beta1) * g
            v = beta2 * v + (1 - beta2) * (g * g)
            m_hat = m / (1 - beta1 ** t)
            v_hat = v / (1 - beta2 ** t)
            theta = theta - lr * m_hat / (np.sqrt(v_hat) + eps)
        path.append(theta.copy())
        losses.append(quad_loss(theta))
    return np.array(path), np.array(losses)

for method in ["gd", "momentum", "adam"]:
    path, losses = optimize(method=method)
    print(f"{method:8s} final theta = {np.round(path[-1], 4)}, final loss = {losses[-1]:.6f}")
```

A quadratic example is simple, but it teaches three practical lessons:

1. curvature affects stable step sizes;
2. momentum can reduce zigzagging;
3. adaptive scaling can help when coordinates have very different curvature.

## 22.20 Python example: mini-batch gradient noise

Mini-batch gradients are random estimates of the full gradient.  The variance decreases with batch size.

```{python}
rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)
N = 1000
true_grad = np.array([1.0, -0.5, 0.25])
per_example_grads = true_grad + rng.normal(scale=[2.0, 1.0, 0.5], size=(N, 3))
full_grad = per_example_grads.mean(axis=0)

for B in [1, 4, 16, 64, 256]:
    estimates = []
    for _ in range(500):
        idx = rng.choice(N, size=B, replace=False)
        estimates.append(per_example_grads[idx].mean(axis=0))
    estimates = np.array(estimates)
    avg_error_norm = np.linalg.norm(estimates - full_grad, axis=1).mean()
    print(f"batch size {B:3d}: mean gradient-estimation error norm = {avg_error_norm:.4f}")
```

Larger batches give more accurate gradient estimates, but they are more expensive and may reduce the useful exploratory noise of SGD.

## 22.21 Python example: gradient clipping

```{python}
def clip_gradient(g, threshold):
    norm = np.linalg.norm(g)
    if norm <= threshold:
        return g.copy()
    return g * (threshold / norm)

gradients = [
    np.array([0.2, -0.1, 0.3]),
    np.array([5.0, -4.0, 3.0]),
    np.array([40.0, -20.0, 10.0])
]
threshold = 2.0

for g in gradients:
    clipped = clip_gradient(g, threshold)
    print("original norm:", round(np.linalg.norm(g), 3),
          " clipped norm:", round(np.linalg.norm(clipped), 3),
          " clipped vector:", np.round(clipped, 3))
```

Clipping preserves direction but changes magnitude when the gradient is too large.

## 22.22 Python example: training a tiny text classifier

The following code trains a softmax classifier using averaged token-count features.  This is not a state-of-the-art NLP model.  Its purpose is to show the complete optimization loop in a transparent way.

```{python}
from collections import Counter
import numpy as np

train_texts = [
    "excellent clear helpful explanation",
    "useful clear lecture helpful",
    "confusing unclear poor explanation",
    "poor confusing lecture unclear",
    "clear useful examples",
    "unclear poor examples"
]
train_y = np.array([1, 1, 0, 0, 1, 0])

vocab = sorted(set(word for text in train_texts for word in text.split()))
word_to_id = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(vocab)}

X = np.zeros((len(train_texts), len(vocab)))
for i, text in enumerate(train_texts):
    counts = Counter(text.split())
    for word, count in counts.items():
        X[i, word_to_id[word]] = count
    X[i] /= max(1, X[i].sum())

rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
K, d = 2, X.shape[1]
W = rng.normal(scale=0.01, size=(K, d))
b = np.zeros(K)

lr = 0.8
loss_history = []
for epoch in range(100):
    loss, grad_W, grad_b = loss_and_grad(W, b, X, train_y)
    W -= lr * grad_W
    b -= lr * grad_b
    loss_history.append(loss)

P = softmax_rows(X @ W.T + b)
pred = P.argmax(axis=1)
print("vocabulary:", vocab)
print("final loss:", round(loss_history[-1], 4))
print("predictions:", pred.tolist())
print("training accuracy:", float(np.mean(pred == train_y)))

weights_for_positive = W[1] - W[0]
for word, weight in sorted(zip(vocab, weights_for_positive), key=lambda x: -x[1]):
    print(f"{word:12s} {weight: .3f}")
```

This example connects optimization with interpretability.  The learned weight difference $W_1-W_0$ gives a linear score direction for the positive class.

## 22.24 Common mistakes

1. **Confusing logits and probabilities.**  Softmax converts logits into probabilities.  Cross-entropy should usually be computed using a numerically stable log-softmax.
2. **Averaging over padding tokens.**  Padding tokens should be masked out of token-level losses.
3. **Using a learning rate without checking gradient scale.**  The product $\eta\|g\|$ controls step size.
4. **Assuming smaller training loss means better model.**  Training loss and test performance can move differently.
5. **Treating Adam as magic.**  Adam rescales coordinates using gradient moments; it still requires learning-rate tuning and regularization.
6. **Ignoring random variation.**  Mini-batch order, initialization, and hardware can change training trajectories.

## 22.25 Worked calculation

Suppose a three-class model outputs probabilities

$$
p=(0.20,0.60,0.20)
$$

and the correct class is class $2$.  With one-hot target

$$
e_2=(0,1,0),
$$

the gradient with respect to logits is

$$
\nabla_z\ell=p-e_2=(0.20,-0.40,0.20).
$$

If the logit model is $z=Wh+b$ with

$$
h=(1,2)^T,
$$

then

$$
\nabla_W\ell=(p-e_2)h^T
=
\begin{pmatrix}
0.20 \\
-0.40 \\
0.20
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 2
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
0.20 & 0.40 \\
-0.40 & -0.80 \\
0.20 & 0.40
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Thus gradient descent subtracts a positive multiple of this matrix.  The row for the correct class increases in the direction of $h$, while the rows for incorrect classes decrease in that direction.

## 22.29 What to remember

- Most NLP training objectives are empirical risks built from negative log-likelihood or cross-entropy.
- The softmax cross-entropy gradient is $p-e_y$.
- Backpropagation is repeated use of the chain rule.
- SGD uses noisy but inexpensive gradient estimates.
- Momentum averages gradient directions over time.
- Adam rescales coordinates using first and second gradient moments.
- AdamW separates weight decay from adaptive gradient scaling.
- Gradient clipping controls the norm of extreme updates.
- Optimization performance and statistical generalization are related but not identical.
- In mathematical NLP, every training run should specify the model, data distribution, loss, optimizer, schedule, and evaluation metric.
