26  Chapter 21. Information Theory and Language

Entropy, Divergence, Mutual Information, and Compression

27 Chapter 21. Information Theory and Language

Information theory gives a mathematical language for uncertainty, prediction, compression, and dependence. For natural language processing, these ideas are not optional background. They appear directly in language-model training, perplexity, tokenization, decoding, representation learning, retrieval, and alignment.

The central observation is simple:

A language model assigns probabilities to possible tokens and sequences. Therefore, a language model can be studied using the mathematics of probability distributions and information.

Earlier chapters used cross-entropy as a loss function and perplexity as an evaluation metric. This chapter explains why those quantities are natural. The goal is to make explicit the connection between prediction and compression:

\[ \text{good probabilistic prediction} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \text{short code length on average}. \]

For MA Applied Mathematics and MS Statistics students, information theory is especially useful because it gives clean definitions that apply to both classical statistical NLP and modern neural language models.

TipGuiding principle

Entropy measures uncertainty under a true distribution. Cross-entropy measures the average loss when one distribution is used to predict another. KL divergence measures the extra cost caused by using the wrong distribution.

27.1 21.1 Learning goals

After studying this chapter, you should be able to:

  1. compute entropy, cross-entropy, KL divergence, and perplexity for discrete token distributions;
  2. explain why cross-entropy is the standard training loss for language models;

  4. use the entropy chain rule to decompose uncertainty in a sequence;
  5. define conditional entropy and mutual information;
  6. connect pointwise mutual information with word co-occurrence and embeddings;
  7. explain how tokenization changes entropy and code length;
  8. interpret KL regularization in fine-tuning and alignment;
  9. implement information-theoretic quantities from scratch in Python;
  10. critique language-model evaluations using information-theoretic reasoning.

27.2 21.2 Discrete distributions over tokens

Let \(V=\{1,2,\ldots,d\}\) be a finite vocabulary. A token distribution is a vector

\[ p=(p_1,\ldots,p_d) \in \Delta^{d-1}, \]

where

\[ p_j \ge 0, \qquad \sum_{j=1}^d p_j=1. \]

Here \(\Delta^{d-1}\) is the probability simplex. In NLP, \(p_j\) may represent the empirical frequency of token \(j\) in a corpus, the conditional probability assigned by a language model to token \(j\), or the predicted class probability in a classifier.

Given counts \(c_1,\ldots,c_d\) from a corpus, the empirical distribution is

\[ \widehat p_j = \frac{c_j}{\sum_{r=1}^d c_r}. \]

This is the maximum likelihood estimator for a categorical distribution. It is also the simplest bridge between text and information theory: a corpus becomes a point in a probability simplex.

NoteLogarithm base

If logs use base \(2\), entropy is measured in bits. If logs use base \(e\), entropy is measured in nats. Machine learning usually uses natural logarithms because gradients are simpler. This chapter mostly uses natural logarithms.

27.3 21.3 Entropy

Let \(X\) be a discrete random variable with distribution \(p\) on \(V\). The entropy of \(X\) is

\[ H(X)=H(p)=-\sum_{j=1}^d p_j \log p_j. \]

By convention, \(0\log 0=0\). Entropy measures the average uncertainty before observing \(X\). Equivalently, it is the average information content of the outcome:

\[ H(X)=E[-\log p_X]. \]

The quantity

\[ I(x)=-\log p(x) \]

is called the surprisal or self-information of the event \(X=x\). Rare events have high surprisal; common events have low surprisal.

27.3.1 Basic properties

For a distribution on \(d\) outcomes,

\[ 0 \le H(p) \le \log d. \]

The lower bound occurs when all probability mass is concentrated on one outcome. The upper bound occurs at the uniform distribution \(p_j=1/d\). Thus entropy is largest when all tokens are equally likely.

This upper bound is easy to prove using KL divergence. Let \(u_j=1/d\) be the uniform distribution. Because \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|u)\ge 0\),

\[ 0 \le \sum_{j=1}^d p_j\log\frac{p_j}{1/d} = \sum_{j=1}^d p_j\log p_j + \log d. \]

Therefore,

\[ H(p) = -\sum_{j=1}^d p_j\log p_j \le \log d. \]

The inequality says that no distribution on \(d\) tokens has more uncertainty than the uniform distribution.

27.4 21.4 Cross-entropy and the log score

Suppose the true token distribution is \(p\), but a model predicts distribution \(q\). The cross-entropy of \(p\) relative to \(q\) is

\[ H(p,q) = -\sum_{j=1}^d p_j \log q_j. \]

It is the expected negative log-probability assigned by \(q\) when the data are generated from \(p\):

\[ H(p,q)=E_{X\sim p}[-\log q_X]. \]

This is exactly the population version of the language-model loss. Given tokens \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\), the empirical cross-entropy loss is

\[ \widehat H(p,q) = -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \log q_{x_i}. \]

If \(q=q_\theta(\cdot \mid \text{context})\) is a neural language model, then minimizing cross-entropy is the same as maximizing likelihood.

27.4.1 Why the negative log score?

The negative log score has several special properties. It rewards assigning high probability to the observed token, but it also punishes overconfident mistakes severely. If \(q_x\) is close to zero for the observed token \(x\), then \(-\log q_x\) is very large.

For one-hot target vector \(e_y\) and predicted distribution \(q\), the cross-entropy loss is

\[ \ell(q,y)=-\sum_{j=1}^d (e_y)_j\log q_j=-\log q_y. \]

This is the loss used in next-token prediction.

27.5 21.5 KL divergence

The Kullback–Leibler divergence from \(q\) to \(p\) is

\[ D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)=\sum_{j=1}^d p_j\log\frac{p_j}{q_j}. \]

It is not symmetric, so it is not a metric. However, it has a crucial interpretation:

\[ D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q) = H(p,q)-H(p). \]

Thus KL divergence is the extra expected log loss incurred by predicting with \(q\) when the true distribution is \(p\). Since \(H(p)\) does not depend on \(q\), minimizing cross-entropy over \(q\) is equivalent to minimizing KL divergence from the data distribution to the model distribution.

27.5.1 Gibbs inequality

For distributions \(p\) and \(q\) with \(q_j>0\) whenever \(p_j>0\),

\[ D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)\ge 0, \]

with equality if and only if \(p=q\). One proof uses the concavity of \(\log\). Let \(Z=q_X/p_X\) when \(X\sim p\). Then

\[ -D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q) = \sum_j p_j\log\frac{q_j}{p_j} \le \log\left(\sum_j p_j\frac{q_j}{p_j}\right) =\log\left(\sum_j q_j\right)=0. \]

Therefore \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)\ge 0\).

WarningSupport mismatch

If \(p_j>0\) but \(q_j=0\), then \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)=\infty\). This is the information-theoretic version of the zero-probability problem in n-gram language models. Smoothing is necessary because a model should not assign zero probability to plausible future tokens.

27.6 21.6 Perplexity

For a sequence of \(n\) observed tokens \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\), suppose a language model assigns conditional probabilities

\[ q_i = q_\theta(x_i\mid x_{<i}). \]

The average negative log-likelihood is

\[ \operatorname{NLL}_{\text{avg}} = -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \log q_i. \]

The perplexity is

\[ \operatorname{PPL}=\exp\left(\operatorname{NLL}_{\text{avg}}\right). \]

When natural logarithms are used, perplexity is the exponential of average cross-entropy in nats. When base-\(2\) logarithms are used,

\[ \operatorname{PPL}=2^{H_2}, \]

where \(H_2\) is measured in bits.

Perplexity can be interpreted as an average branching factor. A perplexity of \(20\) means the model is, in an information-theoretic sense, as uncertain as if it were choosing uniformly among about \(20\) equally likely tokens at each step.

NotePerplexity depends on tokenization

Perplexity is not directly comparable across different tokenizers. A character-level model, word-level model, and subword-level model define different random variables. Changing the tokenization changes the unit of prediction and therefore changes the entropy scale.

27.7 21.7 Sequence entropy and the chain rule

Let \(W_{1:T}=(W_1,\ldots,W_T)\) be a sequence of tokens. The joint entropy is

\[ H(W_{1:T})=-\sum_{w_{1:T}}p(w_{1:T})\log p(w_{1:T}). \]

The entropy chain rule states that

\[ H(W_{1:T})=\sum_{t=1}^T H(W_t\mid W_{1:t-1}). \]

This identity matches the autoregressive factorization

\[ p(w_{1:T})=\prod_{t=1}^T p(w_t\mid w_{1:t-1}). \]

Taking negative logs and expectations gives the entropy chain rule. Thus autoregressive language modeling is not just an engineering trick. It is aligned with the fundamental decomposition of uncertainty in a sequence.

27.7.1 Entropy rate

For a stationary stochastic process \(W_1,W_2,\ldots\), the entropy rate is

\[ \bar H = \lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}H(W_{1:T}), \]

when the limit exists. Equivalently, under suitable conditions,

\[ \bar H = \lim_{T\to\infty}H(W_T\mid W_{1:T-1}). \]

For language, the entropy rate measures the long-run average uncertainty per token after using all previous context. Better context modeling should reduce conditional entropy.

27.8 21.8 Conditional entropy and mutual information

For random variables \(X\) and \(Y\), the conditional entropy is

\[ H(Y\mid X) = -\sum_x p(x)\sum_y p(y\mid x)\log p(y\mid x). \]

It measures the remaining uncertainty in \(Y\) after observing \(X\). The mutual information between \(X\) and \(Y\) is

\[ I(X;Y)=H(Y)-H(Y\mid X). \]

It is also

\[ I(X;Y)=\sum_{x,y}p(x,y)\log\frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)}. \]

Therefore,

\[ I(X;Y)=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p(x,y)\|p(x)p(y)). \]

Mutual information measures how far the joint distribution is from independence. If \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent, then \(p(x,y)=p(x)p(y)\) and \(I(X;Y)=0\).

27.8.1 Mutual information in NLP

Mutual information appears naturally in several places:

  • word co-occurrence: how much does seeing one word tell us about a nearby word?
  • classification: how informative is a token about a label?
  • representation learning: how much information does an embedding retain about input or target variables?
  • retrieval: how informative is a document about a query?
  • prompting: how much does an instruction reduce uncertainty about the desired answer?

27.9 21.9 Pointwise mutual information and word co-occurrence

For two events \(X=x\) and \(Y=y\), the pointwise mutual information is

\[ \operatorname{PMI}(x,y)=\log\frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)}. \]

PMI is positive when \(x\) and \(y\) co-occur more often than expected under independence. It is negative when they co-occur less often than expected.

In word-context analysis, we may let \(X\) be a center word and \(Y\) be a context word. Then PMI becomes

\[ \operatorname{PMI}(w,c)=\log\frac{p(w,c)}{p(w)p(c)}. \]

The positive part is called positive PMI:

\[ \operatorname{PPMI}(w,c)=\max\{\operatorname{PMI}(w,c),0\}. \]

Chapter 8 used PPMI matrices for word embeddings. This chapter explains why the ratio is meaningful: it compares observed co-occurrence with what independence would predict.

27.10 21.10 Compression interpretation

Information theory connects probability and coding. If an event \(x\) has probability \(p(x)\), an ideal code assigns it length approximately

\[ \ell(x)=-\log_2 p(x) \]

bits. Frequent events receive shorter codes; rare events receive longer codes. The expected code length is

\[ E[\ell(X)] = H_2(X). \]

If we encode data generated from \(p\) using a code designed for \(q\), the expected code length becomes

\[ H_2(p,q)=H_2(p)+D_{\mathrm{KL},2}(p\|q). \]

Thus KL divergence is the excess number of bits per token caused by using the wrong probability model. This gives a concrete interpretation of language-model loss:

Lower cross-entropy means the model gives a shorter average code for the text.

This viewpoint is useful for understanding why language modeling is such a general pretraining task. To compress text well, a model must learn spelling, syntax, semantics, style, factual regularities, and long-range dependencies.

27.11 21.11 Tokenization and information

Tokenization changes the random variable being modeled. Suppose the same text can be represented as characters, words, or subwords. Each representation gives a different sequence:

\[ C_{1:m}, \qquad W_{1:n}, \qquad S_{1:r}. \]

The entropy per token is not directly comparable across these representations because the token units are different. A word-level model may have a large vocabulary and high entropy per token. A character-level model may have a small vocabulary and lower entropy per token, but many more tokens per sentence.

A more meaningful comparison is often code length per original character or per original byte:

\[ \text{bits per character} = \frac{\text{total code length in bits}}{\text{number of characters}}. \]

This is why compression-based comparisons must specify the coding unit.

27.12 21.12 KL divergence in fine-tuning and alignment

In Chapter 18, we saw objectives that include a KL penalty. A common form is

\[ \max_\theta E_{y\sim \pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}[r(x,y)] -\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\|\pi_0(\cdot\mid x)). \]

Here \(\pi_0\) is a reference model and \(\pi_\theta\) is the fine-tuned model. The reward term encourages helpful behavior. The KL term discourages the new model from moving too far from the reference distribution.

Mathematically, the KL penalty is a distributional trust region. It does not simply penalize parameter distance. Two models may have very different parameter vectors but similar output distributions, or similar parameters but different output distributions. KL regularization measures distance in probability behavior.

27.13 21.13 Information bottleneck viewpoint

Let \(X\) be an input sequence, \(Z\) be a learned representation, and \(Y\) be a target. The information bottleneck principle asks for a representation \(Z\) that is useful for predicting \(Y\) but compressed relative to \(X\). One idealized objective is

\[ \min \ I(X;Z) - \beta I(Z;Y). \]

The first term encourages compression: \(Z\) should not retain irrelevant details of \(X\). The second term encourages prediction: \(Z\) should retain information about \(Y\).

This viewpoint is not a complete theory of large language models, but it provides useful language for asking mathematical questions:

  • Which information about the input is preserved in an embedding?
  • Which information is discarded by pooling, truncation, or retrieval?
  • How does fine-tuning change the information available for a downstream task?
  • How can we measure whether a representation contains syntactic, semantic, or factual information?

27.15 21.15 Python example: empirical entropy


The first example computes token counts, an empirical distribution, and entropy from scratch.




from collections import Counter
import math

text = "machine learning models learn from data and language models learn from text data"
tokens = text.split()
counts = Counter(tokens)
N = sum(counts.values())
probs = {w: c / N for w, c in counts.items()}

entropy = -sum(p * math.log(p) for p in probs.values())
perplexity = math.exp(entropy)

print("Counts:", counts)
print("Empirical probabilities:")
for w, p in sorted(probs.items()):
    print(f"  {w:10s} {p:.3f}")
print("Entropy in nats:", round(entropy, 4))
print("Entropy in bits:", round(entropy / math.log(2), 4))
print("Empirical perplexity:", round(perplexity, 4))

27.16 21.16 Python example: cross-entropy, KL divergence, and smoothing


Now compare an empirical distribution \(p\) with two model distributions \(q\).




import numpy as np

vocab = ["machine", "language", "data", "model"]
p = np.array([0.45, 0.30, 0.20, 0.05])
q_good = np.array([0.40, 0.32, 0.20, 0.08])
q_bad = np.array([0.10, 0.20, 0.30, 0.40])

def entropy_np(p):
    p = np.asarray(p, dtype=float)
    return -np.sum(np.where(p > 0, p * np.log(p), 0.0))

def cross_entropy_np(p, q):
    p = np.asarray(p, dtype=float)
    q = np.asarray(q, dtype=float)
    return -np.sum(p * np.log(q))

def kl_np(p, q):
    return cross_entropy_np(p, q) - entropy_np(p)

for name, q in [("good", q_good), ("bad", q_bad)]:
    print(name)
    print("  cross entropy:", round(cross_entropy_np(p, q), 4))
    print("  KL divergence:", round(kl_np(p, q), 4))
    print("  perplexity:", round(np.exp(cross_entropy_np(p, q)), 4))

27.17 21.17 Python example: sequence entropy for a bigram model


For a bigram Markov chain, conditional entropy is computed row by row. If \(P\) is a transition matrix and \(\pi\) is the stationary or empirical distribution over previous tokens, then


\[
H(W_t\mid W_{t-1})=\sum_i \pi_i H(P_{i,\cdot}).
\]




import numpy as np

vocab = ["<s>", "the", "model", "learns"]
P = np.array([
    [0.00, 0.70, 0.20, 0.10],
    [0.00, 0.05, 0.80, 0.15],
    [0.00, 0.10, 0.10, 0.80],
    [0.00, 0.60, 0.20, 0.20]
])
pi = np.array([0.10, 0.35, 0.30, 0.25])

def row_entropy(row):
    positive = row[row > 0]
    return -np.sum(positive * np.log(positive))

row_entropies = np.array([row_entropy(row) for row in P])
conditional_entropy = np.sum(pi * row_entropies)

for word, h in zip(vocab, row_entropies):
    print(f"H(next | {word:6s}) = {h:.4f} nats")
print("Average conditional entropy:", round(conditional_entropy, 4))
print("Conditional perplexity:", round(np.exp(conditional_entropy), 4))

import pandas as pd

    [8, 1, 0],

27.19 21.19 Python example: mutual information between token and label


Suppose \(X\) is the indicator of whether a token appears in a document and \(Y\) is a binary sentiment label. We can compute \(I(X;Y)\) from a \(2\times 2\) table.




import numpy as np

def mutual_information_from_table(table):
    table = np.asarray(table, dtype=float)
    pxy = table / table.sum()
    px = pxy.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    py = pxy.sum(axis=0, keepdims=True)
    expected = px @ py
    mask = pxy > 0
    return np.sum(pxy[mask] * np.log(pxy[mask] / expected[mask]))

# rows: token absent/present, columns: negative/positive
excellent_table = np.array([
    [40, 20],
    [5, 35]
])
the_table = np.array([
    [10, 12],
    [35, 43]
])

print("I(excellent present; label):", round(mutual_information_from_table(excellent_table), 4))
print("I(the present; label):      ", round(mutual_information_from_table(the_table), 4))

27.27 21.27 Summary


Information theory gives a common mathematical vocabulary for many parts of NLP. Entropy measures uncertainty, cross-entropy measures predictive loss, KL divergence measures distributional mismatch, and mutual information measures statistical dependence. Perplexity is the exponential form of average cross-entropy and is central to language-model evaluation. PMI connects co-occurrence statistics with word embeddings. KL regularization connects information theory with fine-tuning and alignment.


The most important lesson is that information-theoretic quantities are always defined relative to random variables and probability distributions. Before computing entropy, perplexity, or mutual information, first identify the tokenization, the sample space, the data distribution, and the model distribution.

# Chapter 21. Information Theory and Language

Information theory gives a mathematical language for uncertainty, prediction, compression, and dependence.
For natural language processing, these ideas are not optional background.
They appear directly in language-model training, perplexity, tokenization, decoding, representation learning, retrieval, and alignment.

The central observation is simple:

> A language model assigns probabilities to possible tokens and sequences.  Therefore, a language model can be studied using the mathematics of probability distributions and information.

Earlier chapters used cross-entropy as a loss function and perplexity as an evaluation metric.
This chapter explains why those quantities are natural.
The goal is to make explicit the connection between prediction and compression:

$$
\text{good probabilistic prediction} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \text{short code length on average}.
$$

For MA Applied Mathematics and MS Statistics students, information theory is especially useful because it gives clean definitions that apply to both classical statistical NLP and modern neural language models.

::: {.callout-tip}
## Guiding principle
Entropy measures uncertainty under a true distribution.
Cross-entropy measures the average loss when one distribution is used to predict another.
KL divergence measures the extra cost caused by using the wrong distribution.
:::

## 21.1 Learning goals

After studying this chapter, you should be able to:

1. compute entropy, cross-entropy, KL divergence, and perplexity for discrete token distributions;
2. explain why cross-entropy is the standard training loss for language models;

5. define conditional entropy and mutual information;
6. connect pointwise mutual information with word co-occurrence and embeddings;
7. explain how tokenization changes entropy and code length;
8. interpret KL regularization in fine-tuning and alignment;
9. implement information-theoretic quantities from scratch in Python;
10. critique language-model evaluations using information-theoretic reasoning.

## 21.2 Discrete distributions over tokens

Let $V=\{1,2,\ldots,d\}$ be a finite vocabulary.
A token distribution is a vector

$$
p=(p_1,\ldots,p_d) \in \Delta^{d-1},
$$

where

$$
p_j \ge 0, \qquad \sum_{j=1}^d p_j=1.
$$

Here $\Delta^{d-1}$ is the probability simplex.
In NLP, $p_j$ may represent the empirical frequency of token $j$ in a corpus, the conditional probability assigned by a language model to token $j$, or the predicted class probability in a classifier.

Given counts $c_1,\ldots,c_d$ from a corpus, the empirical distribution is

$$
\widehat p_j = \frac{c_j}{\sum_{r=1}^d c_r}.
$$

This is the maximum likelihood estimator for a categorical distribution.
It is also the simplest bridge between text and information theory: a corpus becomes a point in a probability simplex.

::: {.callout-note}
## Logarithm base
If logs use base $2$, entropy is measured in bits.
If logs use base $e$, entropy is measured in nats.
Machine learning usually uses natural logarithms because gradients are simpler.
This chapter mostly uses natural logarithms.
:::

## 21.3 Entropy

Let $X$ be a discrete random variable with distribution $p$ on $V$.
The entropy of $X$ is

$$
H(X)=H(p)=-\sum_{j=1}^d p_j \log p_j.
$$

By convention, $0\log 0=0$.
Entropy measures the average uncertainty before observing $X$.
Equivalently, it is the average information content of the outcome:

$$
H(X)=E[-\log p_X].
$$

The quantity

$$
I(x)=-\log p(x)
$$

is called the surprisal or self-information of the event $X=x$.
Rare events have high surprisal; common events have low surprisal.

### Basic properties

For a distribution on $d$ outcomes,

$$
0 \le H(p) \le \log d.
$$

The lower bound occurs when all probability mass is concentrated on one outcome.
The upper bound occurs at the uniform distribution $p_j=1/d$.
Thus entropy is largest when all tokens are equally likely.

This upper bound is easy to prove using KL divergence.
Let $u_j=1/d$ be the uniform distribution.
Because $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|u)\ge 0$,

$$
0 \le \sum_{j=1}^d p_j\log\frac{p_j}{1/d}
= \sum_{j=1}^d p_j\log p_j + \log d.
$$

Therefore,

$$
H(p) = -\sum_{j=1}^d p_j\log p_j \le \log d.
$$

The inequality says that no distribution on $d$ tokens has more uncertainty than the uniform distribution.

## 21.4 Cross-entropy and the log score

Suppose the true token distribution is $p$, but a model predicts distribution $q$.
The cross-entropy of $p$ relative to $q$ is

$$
H(p,q) = -\sum_{j=1}^d p_j \log q_j.
$$

It is the expected negative log-probability assigned by $q$ when the data are generated from $p$:

$$
H(p,q)=E_{X\sim p}[-\log q_X].
$$

This is exactly the population version of the language-model loss.
Given tokens $x_1,\ldots,x_n$, the empirical cross-entropy loss is

$$
\widehat H(p,q) = -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \log q_{x_i}.
$$

If $q=q_\theta(\cdot \mid \text{context})$ is a neural language model, then minimizing cross-entropy is the same as maximizing likelihood.

### Why the negative log score?

The negative log score has several special properties.
It rewards assigning high probability to the observed token, but it also punishes overconfident mistakes severely.
If $q_x$ is close to zero for the observed token $x$, then $-\log q_x$ is very large.

For one-hot target vector $e_y$ and predicted distribution $q$, the cross-entropy loss is

$$
\ell(q,y)=-\sum_{j=1}^d (e_y)_j\log q_j=-\log q_y.
$$

This is the loss used in next-token prediction.

## 21.5 KL divergence

The Kullback--Leibler divergence from $q$ to $p$ is

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)=\sum_{j=1}^d p_j\log\frac{p_j}{q_j}.
$$

It is not symmetric, so it is not a metric.
However, it has a crucial interpretation:

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q) = H(p,q)-H(p).
$$

Thus KL divergence is the extra expected log loss incurred by predicting with $q$ when the true distribution is $p$.
Since $H(p)$ does not depend on $q$, minimizing cross-entropy over $q$ is equivalent to minimizing KL divergence from the data distribution to the model distribution.

### Gibbs inequality

For distributions $p$ and $q$ with $q_j>0$ whenever $p_j>0$,

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)\ge 0,
$$

with equality if and only if $p=q$.
One proof uses the concavity of $\log$.
Let $Z=q_X/p_X$ when $X\sim p$.
Then

$$
-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)
= \sum_j p_j\log\frac{q_j}{p_j}
\le \log\left(\sum_j p_j\frac{q_j}{p_j}\right)
=\log\left(\sum_j q_j\right)=0.
$$

Therefore $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)\ge 0$.

::: {.callout-warning}
## Support mismatch
If $p_j>0$ but $q_j=0$, then $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)=\infty$.
This is the information-theoretic version of the zero-probability problem in n-gram language models.
Smoothing is necessary because a model should not assign zero probability to plausible future tokens.
:::

## 21.6 Perplexity

For a sequence of $n$ observed tokens $x_1,\ldots,x_n$, suppose a language model assigns conditional probabilities

$$
q_i = q_\theta(x_i\mid x_{<i}).
$$

The average negative log-likelihood is

$$
\operatorname{NLL}_{\text{avg}} = -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \log q_i.
$$

The perplexity is

$$
\operatorname{PPL}=\exp\left(\operatorname{NLL}_{\text{avg}}\right).
$$

When natural logarithms are used, perplexity is the exponential of average cross-entropy in nats.
When base-$2$ logarithms are used,

$$
\operatorname{PPL}=2^{H_2},
$$

where $H_2$ is measured in bits.

Perplexity can be interpreted as an average branching factor.
A perplexity of $20$ means the model is, in an information-theoretic sense, as uncertain as if it were choosing uniformly among about $20$ equally likely tokens at each step.

::: {.callout-note}
## Perplexity depends on tokenization
Perplexity is not directly comparable across different tokenizers.
A character-level model, word-level model, and subword-level model define different random variables.
Changing the tokenization changes the unit of prediction and therefore changes the entropy scale.
:::

## 21.7 Sequence entropy and the chain rule

Let $W_{1:T}=(W_1,\ldots,W_T)$ be a sequence of tokens.
The joint entropy is

$$
H(W_{1:T})=-\sum_{w_{1:T}}p(w_{1:T})\log p(w_{1:T}).
$$

The entropy chain rule states that

$$
H(W_{1:T})=\sum_{t=1}^T H(W_t\mid W_{1:t-1}).
$$

This identity matches the autoregressive factorization

$$
p(w_{1:T})=\prod_{t=1}^T p(w_t\mid w_{1:t-1}).
$$

Taking negative logs and expectations gives the entropy chain rule.
Thus autoregressive language modeling is not just an engineering trick.
It is aligned with the fundamental decomposition of uncertainty in a sequence.

### Entropy rate

For a stationary stochastic process $W_1,W_2,\ldots$, the entropy rate is

$$
\bar H = \lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}H(W_{1:T}),
$$

when the limit exists.
Equivalently, under suitable conditions,

$$
\bar H = \lim_{T\to\infty}H(W_T\mid W_{1:T-1}).
$$

For language, the entropy rate measures the long-run average uncertainty per token after using all previous context.
Better context modeling should reduce conditional entropy.

## 21.8 Conditional entropy and mutual information

For random variables $X$ and $Y$, the conditional entropy is

$$
H(Y\mid X) = -\sum_x p(x)\sum_y p(y\mid x)\log p(y\mid x).
$$

It measures the remaining uncertainty in $Y$ after observing $X$.
The mutual information between $X$ and $Y$ is

$$
I(X;Y)=H(Y)-H(Y\mid X).
$$

It is also

$$
I(X;Y)=\sum_{x,y}p(x,y)\log\frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)}.
$$

Therefore,

$$
I(X;Y)=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p(x,y)\|p(x)p(y)).
$$

Mutual information measures how far the joint distribution is from independence.
If $X$ and $Y$ are independent, then $p(x,y)=p(x)p(y)$ and $I(X;Y)=0$.

### Mutual information in NLP

Mutual information appears naturally in several places:

- word co-occurrence: how much does seeing one word tell us about a nearby word?
- classification: how informative is a token about a label?
- representation learning: how much information does an embedding retain about input or target variables?
- retrieval: how informative is a document about a query?
- prompting: how much does an instruction reduce uncertainty about the desired answer?

## 21.9 Pointwise mutual information and word co-occurrence

For two events $X=x$ and $Y=y$, the pointwise mutual information is

$$
\operatorname{PMI}(x,y)=\log\frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)}.
$$

PMI is positive when $x$ and $y$ co-occur more often than expected under independence.
It is negative when they co-occur less often than expected.

In word-context analysis, we may let $X$ be a center word and $Y$ be a context word.
Then PMI becomes

$$
\operatorname{PMI}(w,c)=\log\frac{p(w,c)}{p(w)p(c)}.
$$

The positive part is called positive PMI:

$$
\operatorname{PPMI}(w,c)=\max\{\operatorname{PMI}(w,c),0\}.
$$

Chapter 8 used PPMI matrices for word embeddings.
This chapter explains why the ratio is meaningful: it compares observed co-occurrence with what independence would predict.

## 21.10 Compression interpretation

Information theory connects probability and coding.
If an event $x$ has probability $p(x)$, an ideal code assigns it length approximately

$$
\ell(x)=-\log_2 p(x)
$$

bits.
Frequent events receive shorter codes; rare events receive longer codes.
The expected code length is

$$
E[\ell(X)] = H_2(X).
$$

If we encode data generated from $p$ using a code designed for $q$, the expected code length becomes

$$
H_2(p,q)=H_2(p)+D_{\mathrm{KL},2}(p\|q).
$$

Thus KL divergence is the excess number of bits per token caused by using the wrong probability model.
This gives a concrete interpretation of language-model loss:

> Lower cross-entropy means the model gives a shorter average code for the text.

This viewpoint is useful for understanding why language modeling is such a general pretraining task.
To compress text well, a model must learn spelling, syntax, semantics, style, factual regularities, and long-range dependencies.

## 21.11 Tokenization and information

Tokenization changes the random variable being modeled.
Suppose the same text can be represented as characters, words, or subwords.
Each representation gives a different sequence:

$$
C_{1:m}, \qquad W_{1:n}, \qquad S_{1:r}.
$$

The entropy per token is not directly comparable across these representations because the token units are different.
A word-level model may have a large vocabulary and high entropy per token.
A character-level model may have a small vocabulary and lower entropy per token, but many more tokens per sentence.

A more meaningful comparison is often code length per original character or per original byte:

$$
\text{bits per character} = \frac{\text{total code length in bits}}{\text{number of characters}}.
$$

This is why compression-based comparisons must specify the coding unit.

## 21.12 KL divergence in fine-tuning and alignment

In Chapter 18, we saw objectives that include a KL penalty.
A common form is

$$
\max_\theta E_{y\sim \pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}[r(x,y)]
-\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\|\pi_0(\cdot\mid x)).
$$

Here $\pi_0$ is a reference model and $\pi_\theta$ is the fine-tuned model.
The reward term encourages helpful behavior.
The KL term discourages the new model from moving too far from the reference distribution.

Mathematically, the KL penalty is a distributional trust region.
It does not simply penalize parameter distance.
Two models may have very different parameter vectors but similar output distributions, or similar parameters but different output distributions.
KL regularization measures distance in probability behavior.

## 21.13 Information bottleneck viewpoint

Let $X$ be an input sequence, $Z$ be a learned representation, and $Y$ be a target.
The information bottleneck principle asks for a representation $Z$ that is useful for predicting $Y$ but compressed relative to $X$.
One idealized objective is

$$
\min \ I(X;Z) - \beta I(Z;Y).
$$

The first term encourages compression: $Z$ should not retain irrelevant details of $X$.
The second term encourages prediction: $Z$ should retain information about $Y$.

This viewpoint is not a complete theory of large language models, but it provides useful language for asking mathematical questions:

- Which information about the input is preserved in an embedding?
- Which information is discarded by pooling, truncation, or retrieval?
- How does fine-tuning change the information available for a downstream task?
- How can we measure whether a representation contains syntactic, semantic, or factual information?

## 21.15 Python example: empirical entropy

The first example computes token counts, an empirical distribution, and entropy from scratch.

```{python}
from collections import Counter
import math

text = "machine learning models learn from data and language models learn from text data"
tokens = text.split()
counts = Counter(tokens)
N = sum(counts.values())
probs = {w: c / N for w, c in counts.items()}

entropy = -sum(p * math.log(p) for p in probs.values())
perplexity = math.exp(entropy)

print("Counts:", counts)
print("Empirical probabilities:")
for w, p in sorted(probs.items()):
    print(f"  {w:10s} {p:.3f}")
print("Entropy in nats:", round(entropy, 4))
print("Entropy in bits:", round(entropy / math.log(2), 4))
print("Empirical perplexity:", round(perplexity, 4))
```

Entropy is not merely a vocabulary-size measure.
It depends on the full distribution.
A corpus with many rare tokens can have high vocabulary size but still lower entropy if a few tokens dominate.

## 21.16 Python example: cross-entropy, KL divergence, and smoothing

Now compare an empirical distribution $p$ with two model distributions $q$.

```{python}
import numpy as np

vocab = ["machine", "language", "data", "model"]
p = np.array([0.45, 0.30, 0.20, 0.05])
q_good = np.array([0.40, 0.32, 0.20, 0.08])
q_bad = np.array([0.10, 0.20, 0.30, 0.40])

def entropy_np(p):
    p = np.asarray(p, dtype=float)
    return -np.sum(np.where(p > 0, p * np.log(p), 0.0))

def cross_entropy_np(p, q):
    p = np.asarray(p, dtype=float)
    q = np.asarray(q, dtype=float)
    return -np.sum(p * np.log(q))

def kl_np(p, q):
    return cross_entropy_np(p, q) - entropy_np(p)

for name, q in [("good", q_good), ("bad", q_bad)]:
    print(name)
    print("  cross entropy:", round(cross_entropy_np(p, q), 4))
    print("  KL divergence:", round(kl_np(p, q), 4))
    print("  perplexity:", round(np.exp(cross_entropy_np(p, q)), 4))
```

If a model assigns zero probability to an event with positive empirical probability, the cross-entropy becomes infinite.
Additive smoothing prevents this.

```{python}
counts = np.array([9, 6, 4, 1])
alpha = 0.5
p_unsmoothed = counts / counts.sum()
p_smoothed = (counts + alpha) / (counts.sum() + alpha * len(counts))

print("Unsmoothed:", np.round(p_unsmoothed, 3))
print("Smoothed:  ", np.round(p_smoothed, 3))
print("Entropy unsmoothed:", round(entropy_np(p_unsmoothed), 4))
print("Entropy smoothed:", round(entropy_np(p_smoothed), 4))
```

## 21.17 Python example: sequence entropy for a bigram model

For a bigram Markov chain, conditional entropy is computed row by row.
If $P$ is a transition matrix and $\pi$ is the stationary or empirical distribution over previous tokens, then

$$
H(W_t\mid W_{t-1})=\sum_i \pi_i H(P_{i,\cdot}).
$$

```{python}
import numpy as np

vocab = ["<s>", "the", "model", "learns"]
P = np.array([
    [0.00, 0.70, 0.20, 0.10],
    [0.00, 0.05, 0.80, 0.15],
    [0.00, 0.10, 0.10, 0.80],
    [0.00, 0.60, 0.20, 0.20]
])
pi = np.array([0.10, 0.35, 0.30, 0.25])

def row_entropy(row):
    positive = row[row > 0]
    return -np.sum(positive * np.log(positive))

row_entropies = np.array([row_entropy(row) for row in P])
conditional_entropy = np.sum(pi * row_entropies)

for word, h in zip(vocab, row_entropies):
    print(f"H(next | {word:6s}) = {h:.4f} nats")
print("Average conditional entropy:", round(conditional_entropy, 4))
print("Conditional perplexity:", round(np.exp(conditional_entropy), 4))
```

This calculation shows why context matters.
If the previous token makes the next-token distribution sharper, then conditional entropy decreases.

## 21.18 Python example: PMI matrix from co-occurrence counts

The following example computes a PMI matrix from a small word-context count table.

```{python}
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

words = ["king", "queen", "bank", "river"]
contexts = ["royal", "money", "water"]
C = np.array([
    [9, 1, 0],
    [8, 1, 0],
    [0, 7, 4],
    [0, 1, 9]
], dtype=float)

N = C.sum()
p_wc = C / N
p_w = p_wc.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
p_c = p_wc.sum(axis=0, keepdims=True)

with np.errstate(divide="ignore", invalid="ignore"):
    PMI = np.log(p_wc / (p_w @ p_c))
PMI = np.where(C > 0, PMI, np.nan)
PPMI = np.where(np.isnan(PMI), 0.0, np.maximum(PMI, 0.0))

print("PMI matrix")
display(pd.DataFrame(PMI, index=words, columns=contexts).round(3))
print("PPMI matrix")
display(pd.DataFrame(PPMI, index=words, columns=contexts).round(3))
```

Positive PMI highlights associations beyond chance.
This is one reason PMI-like transformations are useful before low-rank factorization.

## 21.19 Python example: mutual information between token and label

Suppose $X$ is the indicator of whether a token appears in a document and $Y$ is a binary sentiment label.
We can compute $I(X;Y)$ from a $2\times 2$ table.

```{python}
import numpy as np

def mutual_information_from_table(table):
    table = np.asarray(table, dtype=float)
    pxy = table / table.sum()
    px = pxy.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    py = pxy.sum(axis=0, keepdims=True)
    expected = px @ py
    mask = pxy > 0
    return np.sum(pxy[mask] * np.log(pxy[mask] / expected[mask]))

# rows: token absent/present, columns: negative/positive
excellent_table = np.array([
    [40, 20],
    [5, 35]
])
the_table = np.array([
    [10, 12],
    [35, 43]
])

print("I(excellent present; label):", round(mutual_information_from_table(excellent_table), 4))
print("I(the present; label):      ", round(mutual_information_from_table(the_table), 4))
```

A sentiment-bearing word usually has higher mutual information with the label than a function word.
However, mutual information is still a statistical estimate and can be biased upward for rare features.

## 21.20 Python example: coding length and model comparison

For a held-out sequence, compare two language models by average code length.

```{python}
import numpy as np

observed = ["data", "model", "data", "language", "model", "data"]
vocab = ["data", "language", "model"]

q1 = {"data": 0.50, "language": 0.20, "model": 0.30}
q2 = {"data": 0.25, "language": 0.50, "model": 0.25}

def average_code_length_bits(sequence, q):
    return np.mean([-np.log2(q[token]) for token in sequence])

for name, q in [("model 1", q1), ("model 2", q2)]:
    bits = average_code_length_bits(observed, q)
    print(name)
    print("  average bits per token:", round(bits, 4))
    print("  perplexity:", round(2 ** bits, 4))
```

The model with shorter average code length is also the model with lower held-out cross-entropy.

## 21.22 Common mistakes

1. **Comparing perplexity across tokenizers.**  Perplexity depends on the prediction unit.
2. **Treating KL divergence as a distance metric.**  KL is asymmetric and does not satisfy the triangle inequality.
3. **Forgetting support conditions.**  If $q_j=0$ where $p_j>0$, cross-entropy and KL divergence are infinite.
4. **Confusing entropy and cross-entropy.**  Entropy uses the true distribution inside both the expectation and the logarithm.  Cross-entropy uses the true distribution in the expectation and the model distribution in the logarithm.
5. **Overinterpreting PMI for rare events.**  PMI can be large for very rare co-occurrences.  Smoothing and minimum-count thresholds are often needed.
6. **Ignoring logarithm base.**  Natural logs give nats.  Base-$2$ logs give bits.
7. **Assuming lower entropy always means better language.**  Entropy measures uncertainty under a distribution, not semantic quality by itself.

## 21.23 Worked calculation

Let

$$
p=(0.5,0.3,0.2), \qquad q=(0.4,0.4,0.2).
$$

Then

$$
H(p)=-0.5\log 0.5-0.3\log 0.3-0.2\log 0.2.
$$

Numerically,

$$
H(p) \approx 1.0297.
$$

The cross-entropy is

$$
H(p,q)=-0.5\log 0.4-0.3\log 0.4-0.2\log 0.2.
$$

Numerically,

$$
H(p,q)\approx 1.0559.
$$

Therefore,

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)=H(p,q)-H(p)\approx 0.0262.
$$

The perplexity associated with $H(p,q)$ is

$$
\exp(1.0559)\approx 2.875.
$$

This means that under model $q$, the average uncertainty is comparable to choosing uniformly from about $2.875$ options.

## 21.27 Summary

Information theory gives a common mathematical vocabulary for many parts of NLP.
Entropy measures uncertainty, cross-entropy measures predictive loss, KL divergence measures distributional mismatch, and mutual information measures statistical dependence.
Perplexity is the exponential form of average cross-entropy and is central to language-model evaluation.
PMI connects co-occurrence statistics with word embeddings.
KL regularization connects information theory with fine-tuning and alignment.

The most important lesson is that information-theoretic quantities are always defined relative to random variables and probability distributions.
Before computing entropy, perplexity, or mutual information, first identify the tokenization, the sample space, the data distribution, and the model distribution.
