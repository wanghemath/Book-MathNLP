9  Probability and Language

Random tokens, conditional distributions, likelihood, entropy, and perplexity

Natural language has uncertainty. A sentence may continue in many possible ways, a word may have several meanings, and the same document may be assigned different labels depending on context. This chapter develops the probabilistic viewpoint needed for modern NLP. We treat tokens, sentences, labels, and model outputs as random objects. The goal is to understand language modeling as conditional probability estimation and supervised NLP as learning probability distributions from data.

9.1 Learning goals

After studying this chapter, you should be able to:

  1. describe a token sequence as a realization of random variables over a finite vocabulary;
  2. distinguish empirical token frequencies, population probabilities, and model probabilities;
  3. use the chain rule of probability to factor the probability of a sentence;
  4. explain why language modeling is conditional distribution estimation;
  5. derive maximum likelihood estimation for a unigram model;
  6. compute negative log-likelihood, cross-entropy, entropy, KL divergence, and perplexity;
  7. explain the difference between generative and discriminative NLP models;
  8. implement simple probability models for text using Python;
  9. use an interactive Plotly.js visualization to explore entropy, temperature, and next-token probabilities;
  10. use AI tools to critique probabilistic assumptions without treating the AI response as a proof.

9.2 Why probability enters NLP

In the previous chapters, we treated text as data: strings, token sequences, count vectors, and document-term matrices. These representations are useful, but they do not yet answer questions such as:

  • How likely is a sentence?
  • Which word is most likely to come next?
  • How uncertain is a model’s prediction?
  • How should a classifier combine evidence from many words?
  • How should we compare two language models statistically?

Probability provides the mathematical language for these questions.

Suppose the vocabulary is

\[ V=\{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_d\}. \]

A single token can be modeled as a random variable

\[ W:\Omega \to V, \]

where \(\Omega\) is an underlying sample space. For a sentence or document of length \(T\), we use a sequence of random variables

\[ W_1,W_2,\ldots,W_T, \]

with each \(W_t\) taking values in \(V\). A particular observed sentence is a realization

\[ w_{1:T}=(w_1,w_2,\ldots,w_T). \]

Central idea. A probabilistic language model assigns probabilities to token sequences:

\[ p(w_{1:T})=P(W_1=w_1,\ldots,W_T=w_T). \]

Modern large language models usually estimate conditional probabilities of the form

\[ p(w_t \mid w_1,\ldots,w_{t-1}). \]

A probability model does not claim that language is random in a philosophical sense. Rather, it gives a mathematical way to represent uncertainty from the viewpoint of an observer who has partial information.

9.3 Probability distributions on a finite vocabulary

For a fixed vocabulary \(V=\{v_1,\ldots,v_d\}\), a probability distribution over tokens is a vector

\[ p=(p_1,p_2,\ldots,p_d) \in R^d \]

such that

\[ p_j \geq 0, \qquad \sum_{j=1}^d p_j = 1. \]

The set of all such vectors is the probability simplex

\[ \Delta^{d-1} =\left\{p\in R^d: p_j\geq 0,\ \sum_{j=1}^d p_j=1\right\}. \]

If \(p_j=P(W=v_j)\), then \(p\) is the distribution of the random token \(W\).

9.3.1 Example

Let

\[ V=\{\text{data},\text{language},\text{model},\text{learns}\}. \]

One possible token distribution is

\[ p=(0.35,0.25,0.30,0.10). \]

This means

\[ P(W=\text{data})=0.35, \qquad P(W=\text{learns})=0.10. \]

A model’s prediction at a particular position in a sentence is also a probability vector in a simplex. If a neural language model reads the context

language models

then it may output a conditional distribution

\[ p_\theta(\cdot \mid \text{language models}) \in \Delta^{d-1}. \]

The subscript \(\theta\) denotes trainable model parameters.

9.4 Empirical distributions

In practice we do not know the true population distribution of language. We observe a corpus and estimate probabilities from counts.

Let \(c_j\) be the number of times token \(v_j\) appears in a corpus and let

\[ N=\sum_{j=1}^d c_j \]

be the total number of vocabulary tokens. The empirical token distribution is

\[ \widehat p_j=\frac{c_j}{N}, \qquad j=1,\ldots,d. \]

This is a probability vector because \(\widehat p_j\geq 0\) and

\[ \sum_{j=1}^d \widehat p_j = \sum_{j=1}^d \frac{c_j}{N} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^d c_j =1. \]

Empirical distribution. A corpus turns counts into probabilities by normalization:

\[ \widehat p_j=\frac{\text{count of }v_j}{\text{total token count}}. \]

This is the simplest statistical estimator in language modeling.

9.4.1 Python example: empirical token probabilities

from collections import Counter
import math

corpus = [
    "language models learn from data",
    "data models predict tokens",
    "language data has structure",
    "models learn probability"
]

def tokenize(text):
    return text.lower().split()

tokens = []
for doc in corpus:
    tokens.extend(tokenize(doc))

counts = Counter(tokens)
N = sum(counts.values())
empirical_p = {token: count / N for token, count in counts.items()}

print("Token counts:")
print(counts)
print("\nEmpirical probabilities:")
for token, prob in sorted(empirical_p.items()):
    print(f"{token:12s} {prob:.3f}")

9.5 Population distribution, data distribution, and model distribution

It is useful to distinguish three distributions.

Distribution Meaning Usually known?
\(p^*\) true data-generating distribution of language in the target domain no
\(\widehat p\) empirical distribution from the observed corpus yes
\(p_\theta\) model distribution with parameters \(\theta\) yes after training

The true distribution \(p^*\) is an idealized object. It represents the probability law of text in the population or domain we care about. We never observe \(p^*\) directly. Instead, we observe samples and build an empirical approximation \(\widehat p\). Training tries to choose parameters \(\theta\) so that \(p_\theta\) is close to \(p^*\), using the corpus as evidence.

A clean statistical formulation is:

\[ \text{Choose }\theta\text{ so that }p_\theta\text{ assigns high probability to observed text.} \]

This principle leads to maximum likelihood estimation.

9.6 Maximum likelihood for a unigram model

The simplest language model assumes that tokens are independent and identically distributed from a vocabulary distribution \(p\).

Let the corpus tokens be

\[ w_1,w_2,\ldots,w_N. \]

The unigram model says

\[ P(W_1=w_1,\ldots,W_N=w_N) = \prod_{i=1}^N p(w_i). \]

If token \(v_j\) appears \(c_j\) times, the likelihood is

\[ L(p) = \prod_{j=1}^d p_j^{c_j}. \]

The log-likelihood is

\[ \ell(p) = \log L(p) = \sum_{j=1}^d c_j\log p_j. \]

We maximize \(\ell(p)\) subject to

\[ p_j\geq 0,\qquad \sum_{j=1}^d p_j=1. \]

Ignoring zero-count boundary details, use a Lagrange multiplier:

\[ \mathcal L(p,\lambda) = \sum_{j=1}^d c_j\log p_j + \lambda\left(1-\sum_{j=1}^d p_j\right). \]

For \(c_j>0\),

\[ \frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial p_j} = \frac{c_j}{p_j}-\lambda=0. \]

Therefore

\[ p_j=\frac{c_j}{\lambda}. \]

Using \(\sum_j p_j=1\) gives

\[ 1=\sum_{j=1}^d \frac{c_j}{\lambda} =\frac{N}{\lambda}, \]

so \(\lambda=N\). Hence the maximum likelihood estimator is

\[ \widehat p_j=\frac{c_j}{N}. \]

MLE for the unigram model. Under the independent-token model, the maximum likelihood estimate of the token probability is the empirical frequency:

\[ \widehat p_j=\frac{c_j}{N}. \]

This derivation is important because it shows how a simple counting rule is actually a likelihood-based statistical estimator.

9.7 Sentences and the chain rule

A sentence is not just a bag of independent tokens. Word order and context matter. The full probability of a sequence is

\[ P(W_1=w_1,\ldots,W_T=w_T). \]

The chain rule of probability gives the exact factorization

\[ P(w_1,\ldots,w_T) = P(w_1)P(w_2\mid w_1)P(w_3\mid w_1,w_2)\cdots P(w_T\mid w_1, \ldots,w_{T-1}). \]

Equivalently,

\[ P(w_{1:T}) = \prod_{t=1}^T P(w_t\mid w_{1:t-1}), \]

where \(w_{1:t-1}=(w_1,\ldots,w_{t-1})\).

Language modeling problem. Estimate the conditional distribution

\[ P(W_t=\cdot \mid W_1=w_1,\ldots,W_{t-1}=w_{t-1}). \]

A language model is a machine for producing this distribution.

This formula is exact. The difficulty is statistical and computational: there are many possible histories \(w_{1:t-1}\). N-gram models solve this by truncating the history. Transformers solve it by learning a flexible representation of the history through attention.

9.8 Conditional probability and prediction

The basic object in next-token prediction is a conditional probability. Given a context \(C\), a language model outputs

\[ p_\theta(v_j\mid C),\qquad j=1,\ldots,d. \]

This is a probability vector satisfying

\[ p_\theta(v_j\mid C)\geq 0, \qquad \sum_{j=1}^d p_\theta(v_j\mid C)=1. \]

The most likely next token is

\[ \widehat w = \arg\max_{v_j\in V} p_\theta(v_j\mid C). \]

But the full probability vector is more informative than only the maximum. It tells us how uncertain the model is. For example, after the context

9.9 Negative log-likelihood

Suppose a model assigns probability \(p_\theta(w_i)\) to each observed token \(w_i\). The likelihood of the observed corpus is

\[ L(\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^N p_\theta(w_i). \]

The log-likelihood is

\[ \ell(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^N \log p_\theta(w_i). \]

Instead of maximizing log-likelihood, machine learning usually minimizes negative log-likelihood:

\[ \operatorname{NLL}(\theta) = -\sum_{i=1}^N \log p_\theta(w_i). \]

For a sequence model, this becomes

\[ \operatorname{NLL}(\theta) = -\sum_{t=1}^T \log p_\theta(w_t\mid w_{1:t-1}). \]

The negative sign appears because optimization algorithms are often written as minimization algorithms. Minimizing negative log-likelihood is equivalent to maximizing likelihood.

9.9.1 Python example: negative log-likelihood

observed_tokens = ["data", "models", "learn", "data"]
model_p = {
    "data": 0.40,
    "models": 0.25,
    "learn": 0.20,
    "language": 0.15
}

nll = -sum(math.log(model_p[token]) for token in observed_tokens)
avg_nll = nll / len(observed_tokens)

print("Negative log-likelihood:", round(nll, 4))
print("Average negative log-likelihood:", round(avg_nll, 4))
print("Perplexity:", round(math.exp(avg_nll), 4))

9.10 Entropy

Entropy measures uncertainty in a probability distribution. For a distribution \(p\) on \(V\), the entropy is

\[ H(p)=-\sum_{j=1}^d p_j\log p_j. \]

We use the convention \(0\log 0=0\), interpreted as a limit.

If \(p\) is concentrated on one token, entropy is small. If \(p\) is spread evenly across many tokens, entropy is large.

9.10.1 Example

For a four-token vocabulary, compare

\[ p=(1,0,0,0) \]

and

\[ q=\left(\frac14,\frac14,\frac14,\frac14\right). \]

Then

\[ H(p)=0, \]

while

\[ H(q)=-4\left(\frac14\log\frac14\right)=\log 4. \]

The uniform distribution has the largest entropy among distributions on four outcomes.

9.10.2 Python example: entropy of token distributions

def entropy(probs):
    return -sum(p * math.log(p) for p in probs if p > 0)

sharp = [0.90, 0.05, 0.03, 0.02]
spread = [0.25, 0.25, 0.25, 0.25]

print("Entropy of sharp distribution:", round(entropy(sharp), 4))
print("Entropy of spread distribution:", round(entropy(spread), 4))
Entropy of sharp distribution: 0.428
Entropy of spread distribution: 1.3863

In NLP, entropy helps quantify uncertainty in token prediction. A model with high next-token entropy is unsure among many possible continuations. A model with low next-token entropy strongly prefers a small number of continuations.

9.11 Cross-entropy

Suppose \(p\) is the true distribution and \(q\) is a model distribution. The cross-entropy from \(p\) to \(q\) is

\[ H(p,q)=-\sum_{j=1}^d p_j\log q_j. \]

This measures the expected negative log-probability assigned by model \(q\) when the data are drawn from \(p\).

If \(p\) is replaced by an empirical distribution \(\widehat p\), then

\[ H(\widehat p,q) = -\sum_{j=1}^d \widehat p_j\log q_j. \]

Using \(\widehat p_j=c_j/N\), we get

\[ H(\widehat p,q) = -\sum_{j=1}^d \frac{c_j}{N}\log q_j = -\frac1N \sum_{j=1}^d c_j\log q_j. \]

This is average negative log-likelihood.

Cross-entropy and training. When we train a language model with next-token prediction, the usual loss is empirical cross-entropy:

\[ -\frac1N \sum_{i=1}^N \log p_\theta(w_i\mid \text{context}_i). \]

Thus cross-entropy is not just an evaluation metric. It is the main training objective for many language models.

9.12 KL divergence

The Kullback-Leibler divergence from \(p\) to \(q\) is

\[ D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q) = \sum_{j=1}^d p_j\log\frac{p_j}{q_j}. \]

It satisfies

\[ D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)\geq 0, \]

with equality if \(p=q\) on the support of \(p\).

The relationship between entropy, cross-entropy, and KL divergence is

\[ H(p,q)=H(p)+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q). \]

Since \(H(p)\) does not depend on the model \(q\), minimizing cross-entropy is equivalent to minimizing KL divergence from the data distribution to the model distribution.

This identity gives a clean mathematical interpretation of language-model training:

\[ \text{training reduces the discrepancy between the data distribution and the model distribution.} \]

9.13 Perplexity

Perplexity is a common metric for language models. If the average negative log-likelihood per token is

\[ \overline L = -\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N \log p_\theta(w_i\mid \text{context}_i), \]

then perplexity is

\[ \operatorname{PPL}=\exp(\overline L). \]

If logarithms base \(2\) are used, then

\[ \operatorname{PPL}=2^{\overline L_2}. \]

Perplexity can be interpreted as an effective number of equally likely choices. For example, a perplexity of \(10\) roughly means the model is as uncertain as if it were choosing among \(10\) equally likely tokens at each step.

Important caution. Lower perplexity usually means better next-token prediction on similar data, but it does not automatically mean better reasoning, truthfulness, robustness, or usefulness.

The softmax function converts scores, or logits, into probabilities. Given logits \(z_1,\ldots,z_d\) and temperature \(T>0\), define

\[ p_j(T)=\frac{\exp(z_j/T)}{\sum_{k=1}^d \exp(z_k/T)}. \]

Small temperature makes the distribution sharper. Large temperature makes it flatter. This affects entropy and perplexity.

9.15 Conditional language models as statistical estimators

Let \(C_t=(W_1,\ldots,W_{t-1})\) denote the context before position \(t\). A conditional language model estimates

\[ p^*(w_t\mid C_t). \]

The model is a parameterized function

\[ f_\theta:C_t \mapsto p_\theta(\cdot\mid C_t)\in \Delta^{d-1}. \]

Training data consist of context-target pairs

\[ (C_i,y_i),\qquad i=1,\ldots,N, \]

where \(y_i\) is the observed next token. The empirical cross-entropy loss is

\[ \widehat R(\theta) = -\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N \log p_\theta(y_i\mid C_i). \]

This is the same mathematical structure whether \(f_\theta\) is an n-gram table, a recurrent neural network, or a transformer.

Unified view. Classical and neural language models differ in how they represent the context \(C\) and parameterize \(p_\theta(\cdot\mid C)\), but they share the same probabilistic target: estimate a conditional distribution over the next token.

9.16 From counts to conditional probabilities

A simple conditional model estimates probabilities from co-occurrence counts. Let \(a\) be a previous token and \(b\) be a next token. Define

\[ c(a,b)=\#\{t: w_{t-1}=a,\ w_t=b\} \]

and

\[ c(a)=\sum_{b\in V} c(a,b). \]

The empirical conditional probability is

\[ \widehat P(W_t=b\mid W_{t-1}=a) = \frac{c(a,b)}{c(a)}. \]

This is the bigram maximum likelihood estimator. Chapter 5 develops this idea systematically.

9.16.1 Python example: conditional next-token probabilities

from collections import defaultdict, Counter

sentences = [
    "language models learn from data",
    "language models predict tokens",
    "models learn probability from data",
    "data models learn patterns"
]

bigram_counts = defaultdict(Counter)

for sentence in sentences:
    toks = tokenize(sentence)
    for a, b in zip(toks[:-1], toks[1:]):
        bigram_counts[a][b] += 1

previous_token = "models"
total_after_previous = sum(bigram_counts[previous_token].values())
conditional_probs = {
    token: count / total_after_previous
    for token, count in bigram_counts[previous_token].items()
}

print(f"Next-token distribution after '{previous_token}':")
for token, prob in sorted(conditional_probs.items()):
    print(f"P({token} | {previous_token}) = {prob:.3f}")
Next-token distribution after 'models':
P(learn | models) = 0.750
P(predict | models) = 0.250

This model uses only the previous token as context. It is simple enough to inspect by hand, but it already illustrates the central conditional-probability structure of language modeling.

9.17 Zero probabilities and smoothing

A serious problem occurs when a token sequence has not appeared in the training corpus. If \(c(a,b)=0\), then the maximum likelihood bigram estimate gives

\[ \widehat P(b\mid a)=0. \]

This means the model says the event is impossible. But in language, unseen does not mean impossible. A small training corpus cannot contain every valid phrase.

A common remedy is smoothing. For example, add-alpha smoothing estimates

\[ \widehat P_\alpha(b\mid a) = \frac{c(a,b)+\alpha}{c(a)+\alpha d}, \]

where \(\alpha>0\) and \(d=|V|\).

When \(\alpha=1\), this is called add-one smoothing or Laplace smoothing.

Smoothing principle. Smoothing moves probability mass away from observed events and assigns some mass to unseen events. It is a statistical correction for finite data.

Chapter 5 studies smoothing in more detail for n-gram language models.

9.18 Generative and discriminative probabilistic models

Many NLP tasks involve a label \(Y\) and an input text \(X\). For example:

  • \(X\) is a movie review and \(Y\) is its sentiment label;
  • \(X\) is an email and \(Y\) indicates spam or not spam;
  • \(X\) is a student question and \(Y\) is the correct resource category.

There are two common probabilistic viewpoints.

9.18.1 Generative modeling

A generative model describes the joint distribution

\[ P(X,Y). \]

Using the product rule,

\[ P(X,Y)=P(Y)P(X\mid Y). \]

For classification, Bayes’ rule gives

\[ P(Y\mid X)=\frac{P(X\mid Y)P(Y)}{P(X)}. \]

Naive Bayes, developed in Chapter 6, is a generative text classifier. It models how each class generates words.

9.18.2 Discriminative modeling

A discriminative model directly estimates

\[ P(Y\mid X). \]

Logistic regression, neural classifiers, and transformer classifiers are usually discriminative models. They do not need to model the probability of the text itself; they only model the label distribution given the text.

Type Models Main object
Generative Naive Bayes, language models \(P(X,Y)\) or \(P(X)\)
Discriminative Logistic regression, neural classifiers \(P(Y\mid X)\)

Both viewpoints are useful. Generative models are often interpretable and useful with limited data. Discriminative models are often more accurate when sufficient labeled data are available.

9.19 Bayesian updating for text classification

Bayes’ rule is central to probabilistic NLP classification. Suppose \(Y\in\{0,1\}\) is a class label and \(X\) is a text. Then

\[ P(Y=1\mid X) = \frac{P(X\mid Y=1)P(Y=1)}{P(X)}. \]

Since

\[ P(X)=P(X\mid Y=0)P(Y=0)+P(X\mid Y=1)P(Y=1), \]

we can compute the posterior probability of the class once we have class priors and class-conditional text probabilities.

This equation is the mathematical foundation of Naive Bayes classifiers. Its practical success depends on how we model \(P(X\mid Y)\).

9.20 Worked example: a tiny probabilistic classifier

Suppose we classify short texts into two labels:

\[ Y\in\{\text{positive},\text{negative}\}. \]

Assume equal class priors:

\[ P(Y=\text{positive})=P(Y=\text{negative})=\frac12. \]

Let the text be represented by tokens. A simple generative model estimates class-conditional token probabilities. Suppose

\[ P(\text{good}\mid \text{positive})=0.30, \qquad P(\text{good}\mid \text{negative})=0.05, \]

and

\[ P(\text{clear}\mid \text{positive})=0.20, \qquad P(\text{clear}\mid \text{negative})=0.10. \]

For the two-token document

good clear

and under the naive conditional independence approximation,

\[ P(X\mid Y=\text{positive}) =0.30\cdot 0.20=0.060, \]

while

\[ P(X\mid Y=\text{negative}) =0.05\cdot 0.10=0.005. \]

Multiplying by equal priors gives scores

\[ 0.5\cdot 0.060=0.030, \qquad 0.5\cdot 0.005=0.0025. \]

The posterior probability of the positive class is

\[ \frac{0.030}{0.030+0.0025}\approx 0.923. \]

Therefore the model predicts positive sentiment.

9.20.1 Python example: tiny Bayesian classifier

priors = {"positive": 0.5, "negative": 0.5}

    "positive": {"good": 0.30, "clear": 0.20},
    "negative": {"good": 0.05, "clear": 0.10}
}

    score = priors[label]
    for token in document:
        score *= class_token_probs[label][token]
    unnormalized[label] = score

posterior = {
    label: score / normalizing_constant
    for label, score in unnormalized.items()
}

print("Posterior probabilities:", posterior)
print("Prediction:", max(posterior, key=posterior.get))

9.21 Log probabilities and numerical stability

Products of many probabilities become extremely small. For a sentence of length \(T\), the probability

\[ \prod_{t=1}^T p_\theta(w_t\mid w_{1:t-1}) \]

may underflow on a computer. Therefore NLP systems usually work with log probabilities:

\[ \log \prod_{t=1}^T p_\theta(w_t\mid w_{1:t-1}) = \sum_{t=1}^T \log p_\theta(w_t\mid w_{1:t-1}). \]

Log probabilities turn products into sums, making computation more stable and easier to optimize.

9.21.1 Python example: product versus log probability

probs = [0.02] * 100

product_prob = 1.0
for p in probs:
    product_prob *= p

log_prob = sum(math.log(p) for p in probs)

print("Product probability:", product_prob)
print("Log probability:", log_prob)
Product probability: 1.2676506002282316e-170
Log probability: -391.20230054281404

For long sequences, the raw probability may be too small to interpret directly. The log probability remains manageable.

9.22 Statistical assumptions in probabilistic NLP

A mathematical NLP model is built from assumptions. Some assumptions are explicit; others are hidden in the architecture or training data.

Important assumptions include:

  1. Sampling assumption. The training corpus is representative of the target distribution.
  2. Tokenization assumption. The chosen tokens preserve the relevant information.
  3. Context assumption. The model uses enough context to predict the target.
  4. Stationarity assumption. The relationship between context and token does not change too much between training and deployment.
  5. Loss assumption. Cross-entropy is an appropriate objective for the downstream goal.
  6. Evaluation assumption. Test data represent the kinds of examples where the model will be used.

These assumptions are not merely philosophical. When they fail, a model may have low training loss but poor real-world behavior.

9.24 Common mistakes

9.24.1 Mistake 1: Treating the most likely token as the whole model

A language model returns a full probability distribution. The argmax token is only one summary of that distribution.

9.24.2 Mistake 2: Confusing frequency with probability

Empirical frequency estimates probability from data, but it is not the same as the unknown population probability.

9.24.3 Mistake 3: Ignoring the role of the context

The probability of a token depends on context. For example, \(P(\text{bank})\) and \(P(\text{bank}\mid \text{river})\) represent different quantities.

9.24.4 Mistake 4: Comparing raw likelihoods across corpora of different lengths

Longer corpora usually have smaller raw likelihoods. Use average log-likelihood, cross-entropy, or perplexity to compare across datasets.

9.24.5 Mistake 5: Assuming low perplexity implies high-quality generation

Perplexity measures next-token prediction on a distribution. It does not directly measure truthfulness, reasoning, safety, or usefulness.

## Why probability enters NLP

In the previous chapters, we treated text as data: strings, token sequences, count vectors, and document-term matrices.  These representations are useful, but they do not yet answer questions such as:

- How likely is a sentence?
- Which word is most likely to come next?
- How uncertain is a model's prediction?
- How should a classifier combine evidence from many words?
- How should we compare two language models statistically?

Probability provides the mathematical language for these questions.

Suppose the vocabulary is

$$
V=\{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_d\}.
$$

A single token can be modeled as a random variable

$$
W:\Omega \to V,
$$

where $\Omega$ is an underlying sample space.  For a sentence or document of length $T$, we use a sequence of random variables

$$
W_1,W_2,\ldots,W_T,
$$

with each $W_t$ taking values in $V$.  A particular observed sentence is a realization

$$
w_{1:T}=(w_1,w_2,\ldots,w_T).
$$

::: {.math-card}
**Central idea.**  A probabilistic language model assigns probabilities to token sequences:

$$
p(w_{1:T})=P(W_1=w_1,\ldots,W_T=w_T).
$$

Modern large language models usually estimate conditional probabilities of the form

$$
p(w_t \mid w_1,\ldots,w_{t-1}).
$$
:::

A probability model does not claim that language is random in a philosophical sense.  Rather, it gives a mathematical way to represent uncertainty from the viewpoint of an observer who has partial information.

## Probability distributions on a finite vocabulary

For a fixed vocabulary $V=\{v_1,\ldots,v_d\}$, a probability distribution over tokens is a vector

$$
p=(p_1,p_2,\ldots,p_d) \in R^d
$$

such that

$$
p_j \geq 0, \qquad \sum_{j=1}^d p_j = 1.
$$

The set of all such vectors is the probability simplex

$$
\Delta^{d-1}
=\left\{p\in R^d: p_j\geq 0,\ \sum_{j=1}^d p_j=1\right\}.
$$

If $p_j=P(W=v_j)$, then $p$ is the distribution of the random token $W$.

### Example

Let

$$
V=\{\text{data},\text{language},\text{model},\text{learns}\}.
$$

One possible token distribution is

$$
p=(0.35,0.25,0.30,0.10).
$$

This means

$$
P(W=\text{data})=0.35, \qquad P(W=\text{learns})=0.10.
$$

A model's prediction at a particular position in a sentence is also a probability vector in a simplex.  If a neural language model reads the context

```text
language models
```

then it may output a conditional distribution

$$
p_\theta(\cdot \mid \text{language models}) \in \Delta^{d-1}.
$$

The subscript $\theta$ denotes trainable model parameters.

## Empirical distributions

In practice we do not know the true population distribution of language.  We observe a corpus and estimate probabilities from counts.

Let $c_j$ be the number of times token $v_j$ appears in a corpus and let

$$
N=\sum_{j=1}^d c_j
$$

be the total number of vocabulary tokens.  The empirical token distribution is

$$
\widehat p_j=\frac{c_j}{N}, \qquad j=1,\ldots,d.
$$

This is a probability vector because $\widehat p_j\geq 0$ and

$$
\sum_{j=1}^d \widehat p_j
=
\sum_{j=1}^d \frac{c_j}{N}
=
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^d c_j
=1.
$$

::: {.math-card}
**Empirical distribution.**  A corpus turns counts into probabilities by normalization:

$$
\widehat p_j=\frac{\text{count of }v_j}{\text{total token count}}.
$$

This is the simplest statistical estimator in language modeling.
:::

### Python example: empirical token probabilities

```{python}
from collections import Counter
import math

corpus = [
    "language models learn from data",
    "data models predict tokens",
    "language data has structure",
    "models learn probability"
]

def tokenize(text):
    return text.lower().split()

tokens = []
for doc in corpus:
    tokens.extend(tokenize(doc))

counts = Counter(tokens)
N = sum(counts.values())
empirical_p = {token: count / N for token, count in counts.items()}

print("Token counts:")
print(counts)
print("\nEmpirical probabilities:")
for token, prob in sorted(empirical_p.items()):
    print(f"{token:12s} {prob:.3f}")
```

The output is an empirical distribution on the vocabulary built from the corpus.  This distribution is not a deep model, but it is the mathematical beginning of language modeling.

## Population distribution, data distribution, and model distribution

It is useful to distinguish three distributions.

| Distribution | Meaning | Usually known? |
|---|---|---|
| $p^*$ | true data-generating distribution of language in the target domain | no |
| $\widehat p$ | empirical distribution from the observed corpus | yes |
| $p_\theta$ | model distribution with parameters $\theta$ | yes after training |

The true distribution $p^*$ is an idealized object.  It represents the probability law of text in the population or domain we care about.  We never observe $p^*$ directly.  Instead, we observe samples and build an empirical approximation $\widehat p$.  Training tries to choose parameters $\theta$ so that $p_\theta$ is close to $p^*$, using the corpus as evidence.

A clean statistical formulation is:

$$
\text{Choose }\theta\text{ so that }p_\theta\text{ assigns high probability to observed text.}
$$

This principle leads to maximum likelihood estimation.

## Maximum likelihood for a unigram model

The simplest language model assumes that tokens are independent and identically distributed from a vocabulary distribution $p$.

Let the corpus tokens be

$$
w_1,w_2,\ldots,w_N.
$$

The unigram model says

$$
P(W_1=w_1,\ldots,W_N=w_N)
=
\prod_{i=1}^N p(w_i).
$$

If token $v_j$ appears $c_j$ times, the likelihood is

$$
L(p)
=
\prod_{j=1}^d p_j^{c_j}.
$$

The log-likelihood is

$$
\ell(p)
=
\log L(p)
=
\sum_{j=1}^d c_j\log p_j.
$$

We maximize $\ell(p)$ subject to

$$
p_j\geq 0,\qquad \sum_{j=1}^d p_j=1.
$$

Ignoring zero-count boundary details, use a Lagrange multiplier:

$$
\mathcal L(p,\lambda)
=
\sum_{j=1}^d c_j\log p_j
+
\lambda\left(1-\sum_{j=1}^d p_j\right).
$$

For $c_j>0$,

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial p_j}
=
\frac{c_j}{p_j}-\lambda=0.
$$

Therefore

$$
p_j=\frac{c_j}{\lambda}.
$$

Using $\sum_j p_j=1$ gives

$$
1=\sum_{j=1}^d \frac{c_j}{\lambda}
=\frac{N}{\lambda},
$$

so $\lambda=N$.  Hence the maximum likelihood estimator is

$$
\widehat p_j=\frac{c_j}{N}.
$$

::: {.math-card}
**MLE for the unigram model.**  Under the independent-token model, the maximum likelihood estimate of the token probability is the empirical frequency:

$$
\widehat p_j=\frac{c_j}{N}.
$$
:::

This derivation is important because it shows how a simple counting rule is actually a likelihood-based statistical estimator.

## Sentences and the chain rule

A sentence is not just a bag of independent tokens.  Word order and context matter.  The full probability of a sequence is

$$
P(W_1=w_1,\ldots,W_T=w_T).
$$

The chain rule of probability gives the exact factorization

$$
P(w_1,\ldots,w_T)
=
P(w_1)P(w_2\mid w_1)P(w_3\mid w_1,w_2)\cdots P(w_T\mid w_1,
\ldots,w_{T-1}).
$$

Equivalently,

$$
P(w_{1:T})
=
\prod_{t=1}^T P(w_t\mid w_{1:t-1}),
$$

where $w_{1:t-1}=(w_1,\ldots,w_{t-1})$.

::: {.math-card}
**Language modeling problem.**  Estimate the conditional distribution

$$
P(W_t=\cdot \mid W_1=w_1,\ldots,W_{t-1}=w_{t-1}).
$$

A language model is a machine for producing this distribution.
:::

This formula is exact.  The difficulty is statistical and computational: there are many possible histories $w_{1:t-1}$.  N-gram models solve this by truncating the history.  Transformers solve it by learning a flexible representation of the history through attention.

## Conditional probability and prediction

The basic object in next-token prediction is a conditional probability.  Given a context $C$, a language model outputs

$$
p_\theta(v_j\mid C),\qquad j=1,\ldots,d.
$$

This is a probability vector satisfying

$$
p_\theta(v_j\mid C)\geq 0,
\qquad
\sum_{j=1}^d p_\theta(v_j\mid C)=1.
$$

The most likely next token is

$$
\widehat w
=
\arg\max_{v_j\in V} p_\theta(v_j\mid C).
$$

But the full probability vector is more informative than only the maximum.  It tells us how uncertain the model is.  For example, after the context

```text
machine learning models can
```

the model may assign high probability to several plausible next tokens such as "predict," "learn," "classify," or "generate."  A deterministic answer hides that uncertainty.

## Negative log-likelihood

Suppose a model assigns probability $p_\theta(w_i)$ to each observed token $w_i$.  The likelihood of the observed corpus is

$$
L(\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^N p_\theta(w_i).
$$

The log-likelihood is

$$
\ell(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^N \log p_\theta(w_i).
$$

Instead of maximizing log-likelihood, machine learning usually minimizes negative log-likelihood:

$$
\operatorname{NLL}(\theta)
=
-\sum_{i=1}^N \log p_\theta(w_i).
$$

For a sequence model, this becomes

$$
\operatorname{NLL}(\theta)
=
-\sum_{t=1}^T \log p_\theta(w_t\mid w_{1:t-1}).
$$

The negative sign appears because optimization algorithms are often written as minimization algorithms.  Minimizing negative log-likelihood is equivalent to maximizing likelihood.

### Python example: negative log-likelihood

```{python}
observed_tokens = ["data", "models", "learn", "data"]
model_p = {
    "data": 0.40,
    "models": 0.25,
    "learn": 0.20,
    "language": 0.15
}

nll = -sum(math.log(model_p[token]) for token in observed_tokens)
avg_nll = nll / len(observed_tokens)

print("Negative log-likelihood:", round(nll, 4))
print("Average negative log-likelihood:", round(avg_nll, 4))
print("Perplexity:", round(math.exp(avg_nll), 4))
```

The average negative log-likelihood is also the empirical cross-entropy of the observed data relative to the model.

## Entropy

Entropy measures uncertainty in a probability distribution.  For a distribution $p$ on $V$, the entropy is

$$
H(p)=-\sum_{j=1}^d p_j\log p_j.
$$

We use the convention $0\log 0=0$, interpreted as a limit.

If $p$ is concentrated on one token, entropy is small.  If $p$ is spread evenly across many tokens, entropy is large.

### Example

For a four-token vocabulary, compare

$$
p=(1,0,0,0)
$$

and

$$
q=\left(\frac14,\frac14,\frac14,\frac14\right).
$$

Then

$$
H(p)=0,
$$

while

$$
H(q)=-4\left(\frac14\log\frac14\right)=\log 4.
$$

The uniform distribution has the largest entropy among distributions on four outcomes.

### Python example: entropy of token distributions

```{python}
def entropy(probs):
    return -sum(p * math.log(p) for p in probs if p > 0)

sharp = [0.90, 0.05, 0.03, 0.02]
spread = [0.25, 0.25, 0.25, 0.25]

print("Entropy of sharp distribution:", round(entropy(sharp), 4))
print("Entropy of spread distribution:", round(entropy(spread), 4))
```

In NLP, entropy helps quantify uncertainty in token prediction.  A model with high next-token entropy is unsure among many possible continuations.  A model with low next-token entropy strongly prefers a small number of continuations.

## Cross-entropy

Suppose $p$ is the true distribution and $q$ is a model distribution.  The cross-entropy from $p$ to $q$ is

$$
H(p,q)=-\sum_{j=1}^d p_j\log q_j.
$$

This measures the expected negative log-probability assigned by model $q$ when the data are drawn from $p$.

If $p$ is replaced by an empirical distribution $\widehat p$, then

$$
H(\widehat p,q)
=
-\sum_{j=1}^d \widehat p_j\log q_j.
$$

Using $\widehat p_j=c_j/N$, we get

$$
H(\widehat p,q)
=
-\sum_{j=1}^d \frac{c_j}{N}\log q_j
=
-\frac1N \sum_{j=1}^d c_j\log q_j.
$$

This is average negative log-likelihood.

::: {.math-card}
**Cross-entropy and training.**  When we train a language model with next-token prediction, the usual loss is empirical cross-entropy:

$$
-\frac1N \sum_{i=1}^N \log p_\theta(w_i\mid \text{context}_i).
$$
:::

Thus cross-entropy is not just an evaluation metric.  It is the main training objective for many language models.

## KL divergence

The Kullback-Leibler divergence from $p$ to $q$ is

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)
=
\sum_{j=1}^d p_j\log\frac{p_j}{q_j}.
$$

It satisfies

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)\geq 0,
$$

with equality if $p=q$ on the support of $p$.

The relationship between entropy, cross-entropy, and KL divergence is

$$
H(p,q)=H(p)+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q).
$$

Since $H(p)$ does not depend on the model $q$, minimizing cross-entropy is equivalent to minimizing KL divergence from the data distribution to the model distribution.

This identity gives a clean mathematical interpretation of language-model training:

$$
\text{training reduces the discrepancy between the data distribution and the model distribution.}
$$

## Perplexity

Perplexity is a common metric for language models.  If the average negative log-likelihood per token is

$$
\overline L
=
-\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N \log p_\theta(w_i\mid \text{context}_i),
$$

then perplexity is

$$
\operatorname{PPL}=\exp(\overline L).
$$

If logarithms base $2$ are used, then

$$
\operatorname{PPL}=2^{\overline L_2}.
$$

Perplexity can be interpreted as an effective number of equally likely choices.  For example, a perplexity of $10$ roughly means the model is as uncertain as if it were choosing among $10$ equally likely tokens at each step.

::: {.math-card}
**Important caution.**  Lower perplexity usually means better next-token prediction on similar data, but it does not automatically mean better reasoning, truthfulness, robustness, or usefulness.
:::

### Mathematical lesson from the interactive

The temperature transformation

$$
p_j(T)=\frac{\exp(z_j/T)}{\sum_{k=1}^d \exp(z_k/T)}
$$

is not a new training procedure.  It is a transformation of the output distribution.  When $T<1$, the largest logits become more dominant.  When $T>1$, the probabilities become more balanced.  Thus temperature controls randomness in generation by changing the entropy of the next-token distribution.

## Conditional language models as statistical estimators

Let $C_t=(W_1,\ldots,W_{t-1})$ denote the context before position $t$.  A conditional language model estimates

$$
p^*(w_t\mid C_t).
$$

The model is a parameterized function

$$
f_\theta:C_t \mapsto p_\theta(\cdot\mid C_t)\in \Delta^{d-1}.
$$

Training data consist of context-target pairs

$$
(C_i,y_i),\qquad i=1,\ldots,N,
$$

where $y_i$ is the observed next token.  The empirical cross-entropy loss is

$$
\widehat R(\theta)
=
-\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N \log p_\theta(y_i\mid C_i).
$$

This is the same mathematical structure whether $f_\theta$ is an n-gram table, a recurrent neural network, or a transformer.

::: {.math-card}
**Unified view.**  Classical and neural language models differ in how they represent the context $C$ and parameterize $p_\theta(\cdot\mid C)$, but they share the same probabilistic target: estimate a conditional distribution over the next token.
:::

## From counts to conditional probabilities

A simple conditional model estimates probabilities from co-occurrence counts.  Let $a$ be a previous token and $b$ be a next token.  Define

$$
c(a,b)=\#\{t: w_{t-1}=a,\ w_t=b\}
$$

and

$$
c(a)=\sum_{b\in V} c(a,b).
$$

The empirical conditional probability is

$$
\widehat P(W_t=b\mid W_{t-1}=a)
=
\frac{c(a,b)}{c(a)}.
$$

This is the bigram maximum likelihood estimator.  Chapter 5 develops this idea systematically.

### Python example: conditional next-token probabilities

```{python}
from collections import defaultdict, Counter

sentences = [
    "language models learn from data",
    "language models predict tokens",
    "models learn probability from data",
    "data models learn patterns"
]

bigram_counts = defaultdict(Counter)

for sentence in sentences:
    toks = tokenize(sentence)
    for a, b in zip(toks[:-1], toks[1:]):
        bigram_counts[a][b] += 1

previous_token = "models"
total_after_previous = sum(bigram_counts[previous_token].values())
conditional_probs = {
    token: count / total_after_previous
    for token, count in bigram_counts[previous_token].items()
}

print(f"Next-token distribution after '{previous_token}':")
for token, prob in sorted(conditional_probs.items()):
    print(f"P({token} | {previous_token}) = {prob:.3f}")
```

This model uses only the previous token as context.  It is simple enough to inspect by hand, but it already illustrates the central conditional-probability structure of language modeling.

## Zero probabilities and smoothing

A serious problem occurs when a token sequence has not appeared in the training corpus.  If $c(a,b)=0$, then the maximum likelihood bigram estimate gives

$$
\widehat P(b\mid a)=0.
$$

This means the model says the event is impossible.  But in language, unseen does not mean impossible.  A small training corpus cannot contain every valid phrase.

A common remedy is smoothing.  For example, add-alpha smoothing estimates

$$
\widehat P_\alpha(b\mid a)
=
\frac{c(a,b)+\alpha}{c(a)+\alpha d},
$$

where $\alpha>0$ and $d=|V|$.

When $\alpha=1$, this is called add-one smoothing or Laplace smoothing.

::: {.math-card}
**Smoothing principle.**  Smoothing moves probability mass away from observed events and assigns some mass to unseen events.  It is a statistical correction for finite data.
:::

Chapter 5 studies smoothing in more detail for n-gram language models.

## Generative and discriminative probabilistic models

Many NLP tasks involve a label $Y$ and an input text $X$.  For example:

- $X$ is a movie review and $Y$ is its sentiment label;
- $X$ is an email and $Y$ indicates spam or not spam;
- $X$ is a student question and $Y$ is the correct resource category.

There are two common probabilistic viewpoints.

### Generative modeling

A generative model describes the joint distribution

$$
P(X,Y).
$$

Using the product rule,

$$
P(X,Y)=P(Y)P(X\mid Y).
$$

For classification, Bayes' rule gives

$$
P(Y\mid X)=\frac{P(X\mid Y)P(Y)}{P(X)}.
$$

Naive Bayes, developed in Chapter 6, is a generative text classifier.  It models how each class generates words.

### Discriminative modeling

A discriminative model directly estimates

$$
P(Y\mid X).
$$

Logistic regression, neural classifiers, and transformer classifiers are usually discriminative models.  They do not need to model the probability of the text itself; they only model the label distribution given the text.

| Type | Models | Main object |
|---|---|---|
| Generative | Naive Bayes, language models | $P(X,Y)$ or $P(X)$ |
| Discriminative | Logistic regression, neural classifiers | $P(Y\mid X)$ |

Both viewpoints are useful.  Generative models are often interpretable and useful with limited data.  Discriminative models are often more accurate when sufficient labeled data are available.

## Bayesian updating for text classification

Bayes' rule is central to probabilistic NLP classification.  Suppose $Y\in\{0,1\}$ is a class label and $X$ is a text.  Then

$$
P(Y=1\mid X)
=
\frac{P(X\mid Y=1)P(Y=1)}{P(X)}.
$$

Since

$$
P(X)=P(X\mid Y=0)P(Y=0)+P(X\mid Y=1)P(Y=1),
$$

we can compute the posterior probability of the class once we have class priors and class-conditional text probabilities.

This equation is the mathematical foundation of Naive Bayes classifiers.  Its practical success depends on how we model $P(X\mid Y)$.

## Worked example: a tiny probabilistic classifier

Suppose we classify short texts into two labels:

$$
Y\in\{\text{positive},\text{negative}\}.
$$

Assume equal class priors:

$$
P(Y=\text{positive})=P(Y=\text{negative})=\frac12.
$$

Let the text be represented by tokens.  A simple generative model estimates class-conditional token probabilities.  Suppose

$$
P(\text{good}\mid \text{positive})=0.30,
\qquad
P(\text{good}\mid \text{negative})=0.05,
$$

and

$$
P(\text{clear}\mid \text{positive})=0.20,
\qquad
P(\text{clear}\mid \text{negative})=0.10.
$$

For the two-token document

```text
good clear
```

and under the naive conditional independence approximation,

$$
P(X\mid Y=\text{positive})
=0.30\cdot 0.20=0.060,
$$

while

$$
P(X\mid Y=\text{negative})
=0.05\cdot 0.10=0.005.
$$

Multiplying by equal priors gives scores

$$
0.5\cdot 0.060=0.030,
\qquad
0.5\cdot 0.005=0.0025.
$$

The posterior probability of the positive class is

$$
\frac{0.030}{0.030+0.0025}\approx 0.923.
$$

Therefore the model predicts positive sentiment.

### Python example: tiny Bayesian classifier

```{python}
priors = {"positive": 0.5, "negative": 0.5}
class_token_probs = {
    "positive": {"good": 0.30, "clear": 0.20},
    "negative": {"good": 0.05, "clear": 0.10}
}

document = ["good", "clear"]

unnormalized = {}
for label in priors:
    score = priors[label]
    for token in document:
        score *= class_token_probs[label][token]
    unnormalized[label] = score

normalizing_constant = sum(unnormalized.values())
posterior = {
    label: score / normalizing_constant
    for label, score in unnormalized.items()
}

print("Unnormalized scores:", unnormalized)
print("Posterior probabilities:", posterior)
print("Prediction:", max(posterior, key=posterior.get))
```

This small calculation previews Chapter 6.  The important point here is not the particular classifier, but the probabilistic structure: prior, likelihood, posterior, and normalization.

## Log probabilities and numerical stability

Products of many probabilities become extremely small.  For a sentence of length $T$, the probability

$$
\prod_{t=1}^T p_\theta(w_t\mid w_{1:t-1})
$$

may underflow on a computer.  Therefore NLP systems usually work with log probabilities:

$$
\log \prod_{t=1}^T p_\theta(w_t\mid w_{1:t-1})
=
\sum_{t=1}^T \log p_\theta(w_t\mid w_{1:t-1}).
$$

Log probabilities turn products into sums, making computation more stable and easier to optimize.

### Python example: product versus log probability

```{python}
probs = [0.02] * 100

product_prob = 1.0
for p in probs:
    product_prob *= p

log_prob = sum(math.log(p) for p in probs)

print("Product probability:", product_prob)
print("Log probability:", log_prob)
```

For long sequences, the raw probability may be too small to interpret directly.  The log probability remains manageable.

## Statistical assumptions in probabilistic NLP

A mathematical NLP model is built from assumptions.  Some assumptions are explicit; others are hidden in the architecture or training data.

Important assumptions include:

1. **Sampling assumption.**  The training corpus is representative of the target distribution.
2. **Tokenization assumption.**  The chosen tokens preserve the relevant information.
3. **Context assumption.**  The model uses enough context to predict the target.
4. **Stationarity assumption.**  The relationship between context and token does not change too much between training and deployment.
5. **Loss assumption.**  Cross-entropy is an appropriate objective for the downstream goal.
6. **Evaluation assumption.**  Test data represent the kinds of examples where the model will be used.

These assumptions are not merely philosophical.  When they fail, a model may have low training loss but poor real-world behavior.

## Common mistakes

### Mistake 1: Treating the most likely token as the whole model

A language model returns a full probability distribution.  The argmax token is only one summary of that distribution.

### Mistake 2: Confusing frequency with probability

Empirical frequency estimates probability from data, but it is not the same as the unknown population probability.

### Mistake 3: Ignoring the role of the context

The probability of a token depends on context.  For example, $P(\text{bank})$ and $P(\text{bank}\mid \text{river})$ represent different quantities.

### Mistake 4: Comparing raw likelihoods across corpora of different lengths

Longer corpora usually have smaller raw likelihoods.  Use average log-likelihood, cross-entropy, or perplexity to compare across datasets.

### Mistake 5: Assuming low perplexity implies high-quality generation

Perplexity measures next-token prediction on a distribution.  It does not directly measure truthfulness, reasoning, safety, or usefulness.

## Chapter summary

This chapter introduced the probabilistic foundation of NLP.

1. A token is modeled as a random variable taking values in a finite vocabulary.
2. A sentence is a sequence of random variables.
3. A language model assigns probabilities to token sequences or conditional probabilities to next tokens.
4. Empirical frequencies estimate probability distributions from corpora.
5. The unigram maximum likelihood estimator is the empirical token distribution.
6. The chain rule factors a sentence probability into conditional next-token probabilities.
7. Cross-entropy and negative log-likelihood are the central training losses for language models.
8. Entropy measures uncertainty; perplexity exponentiates average negative log-likelihood.
9. Generative models estimate $P(X,Y)$ or $P(X)$; discriminative models estimate $P(Y\mid X)$.
10. Probabilistic models are useful only when their assumptions match the intended data and task.
