28  Graphs and Language

Dependency, knowledge, and message passing

Language is not only a sequence. A sentence has syntactic relations, a document collection has citation and hyperlink structure, a knowledge base has subject-relation-object triples, and a corpus has co-occurrence patterns among words. Graphs provide a mathematical language for representing these relational structures. This chapter develops graph-based NLP from a clean mathematical point of view: nodes, edges, matrices, Laplacians, walks, propagation, and message passing.

28.1 23.1 Learning goals

After reading this chapter, students should be able to:

  • represent linguistic objects as graphs;
  • convert graphs into adjacency, degree, incidence, and Laplacian matrices;
  • distinguish directed, undirected, weighted, bipartite, heterogeneous, and relational graphs;
  • construct word co-occurrence graphs, dependency graphs, document-term graphs, and knowledge graphs;
  • interpret graph walks, centrality, PageRank, and label propagation mathematically;
  • derive one layer of graph neural message passing;
  • explain graph convolution, graph attention, and relation-specific message passing;
  • implement small graph algorithms from scratch in Python;
  • use AI tools to audit graph construction choices rather than simply generate graph code.
NoteGuiding question

When should an NLP problem be represented as a graph rather than as a sequence or a bag of words?

A graph is useful when the relational structure is part of the signal. The important mathematical decision is not “use a graph neural network” but first: choose nodes, choose edges, choose weights, and decide what invariances the representation should have.

28.2 23.2 From sequences to relational objects

Earlier chapters represented a text as a sequence

\[ w_{1:T}=(w_1,\\ldots,w_T) \]

over a vocabulary \(V\), or as a vector \(x\in R^d\). These representations are powerful but not always natural for relational information.

A graph representation starts with a set of nodes

\[ \mathcal V = \{1,2,\ldots,n\} \]

and a set of edges

\[ \mathcal E \subseteq \mathcal V \times \mathcal V. \]

In NLP, nodes may represent:

  • tokens in a sentence;
  • word types in a vocabulary;
  • documents in a corpus;
  • entities in a knowledge base;
  • concepts in an ontology;
  • users, questions, courses, or resources in an advising system.

Edges may represent:

  • adjacency in a sentence;
  • syntactic dependency;
  • word co-occurrence;
  • citation or hyperlink;
  • semantic relation;
  • membership of a word in a document;
  • retrieval similarity.

Thus a graph is not just a data structure. It is a modeling assumption about which relations are relevant.

Graph modeling decision. A graph-based NLP model begins with a map

\[ \text{text data} \longmapsto G=(\mathcal V,\mathcal E,W,X), \]

where \(W\) stores edge weights and \(X\) stores node features. The quality of the downstream model depends strongly on this construction.

28.3 23.3 Basic graph definitions

An undirected weighted graph on \(n\) nodes is described by a symmetric adjacency matrix

\[ A\in R^{n\times n}, \qquad A_{ij}=A_{ji}\ge 0. \]

Here \(A_{ij}>0\) means that nodes \(i\) and \(j\) are connected, and \(A_{ij}\) gives the edge weight. For an unweighted graph, \(A_{ij}\in \{0,1\}\).

A directed graph uses a possibly nonsymmetric adjacency matrix. Then \(A_{ij}>0\) may mean an edge from node \(i\) to node \(j\). The direction convention must be stated because different software packages use different conventions.

The weighted degree of node \(i\) is

\[ d_i = \sum_{j=1}^n A_{ij}. \]

The degree matrix is diagonal:

\[ D=\operatorname{diag}(d_1,\ldots,d_n). \]

For undirected graphs, the combinatorial graph Laplacian is

\[ L=D-A. \]

The normalized adjacency matrix is often written

\[ S=D^{-1/2} A D^{-1/2}, \]

and the normalized Laplacian is

\[ L_{\mathrm{norm}} = I-D^{-1/2}AD^{-1/2}=I-S. \]

When isolated nodes occur, \(d_i=0\), and the inverse degree factors must be handled carefully.

28.4 23.4 Why the Laplacian matters

For a scalar function \(f\in R^n\) on nodes, the quadratic form of the Laplacian is

\[ f^T L f = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^n A_{ij}(f_i-f_j)^2. \]

This identity is central. It says that \(f^T L f\) is small when connected nodes have similar values. In NLP, \(f_i\) may be a label score, a semantic score, or one coordinate of a node embedding.

TipSmoothness prior on graphs

A graph Laplacian encodes the prior that connected nodes should have similar predictions or representations. For example, if two documents are very similar, a classifier may penalize assigning very different label scores to them.

The Laplacian also appears in spectral clustering. Eigenvectors associated with small eigenvalues reveal low-frequency graph signals, meaning functions that vary slowly across edges. This is the graph analogue of using low-frequency Fourier modes to describe a smooth signal.

28.5 23.5 A sentence as a dependency graph

A dependency parse represents a sentence by directed grammatical relations. For example, consider

language models predict tokens

A possible simplified dependency graph is:

predict -> models
models  -> language
predict -> tokens

The words are nodes. Directed edges represent syntactic relations. The main verb predict is connected to subject-like and object-like components.

Mathematically, for a sentence with \(T\) tokens, a dependency graph has an adjacency matrix

\[ A\in \{0,1\}^{T\times T}. \]

If \(A_{ij}=1\) means token \(i\) points to token \(j\), then row \(i\) lists outgoing syntactic neighbors. A graph-based model may update a token representation by aggregating information from its syntactic neighbors rather than from all tokens.

Dependency graphs are useful because they remove some irrelevant word-distance effects. In a long sentence, two syntactically related words may be far apart in the sequence but close in the dependency graph.

28.6 23.6 Word co-occurrence graphs

A word co-occurrence graph has one node per word type. Two words are connected if they occur near each other in a corpus. A simple construction uses a window size \(r\). For every occurrence of word \(w_t\), connect it to words \(w_s\) satisfying

\[ 0 < |s-t| \le r. \]

The edge weight may count co-occurrence frequency:

\[ A_{ij}=\#\{(s,t): w_s=i, w_t=j, 0<|s-t|\le r\}. \]

After symmetrization, \(A\) becomes an undirected weighted graph. This graph captures the distributional hypothesis: words appearing in similar neighborhoods tend to have related meanings.

This connects graph methods to Chapter 8. Co-occurrence matrices, PMI matrices, and word graphs are different views of the same empirical object.

28.7 23.7 Document-term graphs as bipartite graphs

A document-term matrix

\[ X\in R^{m\times d} \]

can be interpreted as a bipartite graph. One part contains document nodes

\[ \{D_1,\ldots,D_m\}, \]

and the other part contains term nodes

\[ \{t_1,\ldots,t_d\}. \]

There is an edge between document \(D_i\) and term \(t_j\) with weight \(X_{ij}\). The full adjacency matrix has block form

\[ A_{\\text{bip}}= \begin{bmatrix} 0 & X \\ X^T & 0 \end{bmatrix}. \]

This construction lets information propagate between documents through shared terms and between terms through shared documents. For example, two documents need not share a direct edge; they may become related through paths of length two:

\[ D_i \to t_j \to D_k. \]

The document-document similarity matrix is

\[ XX^T, \]

and the term-term similarity matrix is

\[ X^T X. \]

Thus the familiar Gram matrices from vector-space NLP are also two-step walk matrices in a bipartite graph.

28.8 23.8 Knowledge graphs

A knowledge graph stores triples

\[ (h,r,t), \]

where \(h\) is a head entity, \(r\) is a relation, and \(t\) is a tail entity. For example:

(Transformer, is_a, neural_architecture)
(BERT, uses, self_attention)
(NLP, uses, probability)

A knowledge graph is not merely a graph; it is usually a labeled directed multigraph. Different relations should not always be treated as the same type of edge.

For \(R\) relation types, one may define relation-specific adjacency matrices

\[ A^{(1)},\ldots,A^{(R)}. \]

The entry \(A^{(r)}_{ij}=1\) means that relation \(r\) connects entity \(i\) to entity \(j\). This creates an adjacency tensor

\[ \mathcal A\in R^{R\times n\times n}. \]

Relation-specific structure is important. The relation is_a is not the same as part_of, and neither is the same as cites or teaches.

28.9 23.9 Random walks and centrality

Graphs support random walks. For an undirected weighted graph with degree matrix \(D\), the row-stochastic transition matrix is

\[ P=D^{-1}A. \]

If \(p_t\in R^n\) is a row vector distribution over nodes at time \(t\), then

\[ p_{t+1}=p_t P. \]

A stationary distribution satisfies

\[ \pi = \pi P. \]

For connected non-bipartite undirected graphs, the stationary distribution is proportional to degree:

\[ \pi_i = \frac{d_i}{\sum_j d_j}. \]

Random walks appear in NLP through ranking, graph-based disambiguation, label propagation, and representation learning. PageRank modifies the walk by adding teleportation:

\[ p_{t+1}=\alpha p_t P + (1-\alpha)v, \]

where \(v\) is a restart distribution and \(0<\alpha<1\).

28.10 23.10 Label propagation

Suppose a graph has a small set of labeled nodes and many unlabeled nodes. Let \(Y\in R^{n\times K}\) store label scores. A simple label propagation iteration is

\[ F^{(k+1)} = \alpha S F^{(k)} + (1-\alpha)Y, \]

where \(S\) is a normalized adjacency matrix. At convergence, if \(0<\alpha<1\), the fixed point satisfies

\[ F^* = \alpha S F^* + (1-\alpha)Y, \]

so

\[ F^* = (1-\alpha)(I-\alpha S)^{-1}Y, \]

provided the inverse exists.

This formula shows that label propagation is not magic. It is repeated averaging over a graph with a restart to the initial labels.

In NLP, this can be used to propagate sentiment labels through a word graph, topic labels through a document graph, or resource categories through a knowledge graph.

28.11 23.11 Message passing as the common abstraction

Many graph neural networks use message passing. Each node \(i\) has a hidden state

\[ h_i^{(\ell)}\in R^{d_\ell} \]

at layer \(\ell\). A generic message passing layer has the form

\[ m_i^{(\ell)} = \operatorname{AGG}^{(\ell)}\left(\{\psi^{(\ell)}(h_i^{(\ell)},h_j^{(\ell)},e_{ij}) : j\in N(i)\}\right), \]

then

\[ h_i^{(\ell+1)} = \phi^{(\ell)}\left(h_i^{(\ell)},m_i^{(\ell)}\right). \]

Here:

  • \(N(i)\) is the neighborhood of node \(i\);
  • \(e_{ij}\) may store edge features;
  • \(\psi\) creates messages;
  • \(\operatorname{AGG}\) aggregates messages, often by sum, mean, or attention;
  • \(\phi\) updates the node state.

The aggregation should usually be permutation invariant with respect to the ordering of neighbors. This is the graph analogue of a set function.

28.12 23.12 A graph convolution layer

A common simplified graph convolution layer is

\[ H^{(\ell+1)} = \sigma(\tilde S H^{(\ell)} W^{(\ell)}), \]

where:

  • \(H^{(\ell)}\in R^{n\times d_\ell}\) stores node features;
  • \(W^{(\ell)}\in R^{d_\ell\times d_{\ell+1}}\) is a trainable weight matrix;
  • \(\sigma\) is a nonlinear activation;
  • \(\tilde S\) is a normalized adjacency matrix, often built after adding self-loops.

With self-loops,

\[ \tilde A = A + I, \qquad \tilde D_{ii}=\sum_j \tilde A_{ij}, \]

and

\[ \tilde S = \tilde D^{-1/2}\tilde A\tilde D^{-1/2}. \]

The layer has two operations:

  1. mix nearby node features by multiplying by \(\tilde S\);
  2. transform feature coordinates by multiplying by \(W^{(\ell)}\).

Graph convolution as smoothing plus learning. The matrix \(\tilde S\) performs graph smoothing. The matrix \(W^{(\ell)}\) learns feature combinations. Their product creates a trainable local averaging operator on graph signals.

28.13 23.13 Graph attention

Self-attention from Chapters 13 and 14 allows every token to attend to every other token. Graph attention restricts attention to graph neighborhoods. For node \(i\), compute a score for each neighbor \(j\in N(i)\):

\[ e_{ij} = a(h_i,h_j). \]

Then normalize over the neighborhood:

\[ \alpha_{ij} = \frac{\exp(e_{ij})}{\sum_{k\in N(i)}\exp(e_{ik})}. \]

The update is

\[ h_i' = \sigma\left(\sum_{j\in N(i)} \alpha_{ij} W h_j\right). \]

This is attention on a sparse graph. In NLP, it can be used on dependency graphs, document graphs, entity graphs, and hybrid graphs connecting documents, passages, entities, and concepts.

28.14 23.14 Relational message passing

Knowledge graphs require relation-specific updates. A simple relational message passing layer is

\[ h_i' = \sigma\left( W_0 h_i + \sum_{r=1}^R\sum_{j\in N_r(i)} \frac{1}{c_{i,r}} W_r h_j \right), \]

where \(N_r(i)\) is the set of neighbors connected to \(i\) by relation \(r\), \(W_r\) is a relation-specific matrix, and \(c_{i,r}\) is a normalization constant.

This is important because edge labels carry meaning. Treating all relations as identical may lose the distinction between syntactic, semantic, citation, and membership relations.

28.16 23.16 Python example: build a word co-occurrence graph


The following example builds a weighted undirected word graph from a tiny corpus. The code avoids external graph libraries so that the matrices are visible.




import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from collections import Counter

corpus = [
    "graph models represent language structure",
    "language models use tokens and context",
    "probability models describe uncertainty",
    "graph neural networks pass messages",
    "tokens cooccur with context in language"
]

def tokenize(text):
    return text.lower().split()

tokenized = [tokenize(doc) for doc in corpus]
vocab = sorted(set(tok for doc in tokenized for tok in doc))
index = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(vocab)}

window = 2
A = np.zeros((len(vocab), len(vocab)), dtype=float)
for doc in tokenized:
    for t, w in enumerate(doc):
        i = index[w]
        for s in range(max(0, t - window), min(len(doc), t + window + 1)):
            if s == t:
                continue
            j = index[doc[s]]
            A[i, j] += 1

# Symmetrize the directed window counts.
A = 0.5 * (A + A.T)

degrees = A.sum(axis=1)
summary = pd.DataFrame({"word": vocab, "degree": degrees}).sort_values("degree", ascending=False)
print(summary.head(8).to_string(index=False))

The matrix \(A\) is a weighted adjacency matrix. The degree of a word is the total co-occurrence weight attached to it.






28.17 23.17 Python example: Laplacian and smoothness


For an undirected graph, the Laplacian is \(L=D-A\). The quantity \(f^TLf\) measures how much a signal varies across edges.




D = np.diag(A.sum(axis=1))
L = D - A

f = np.zeros(len(vocab))
for word, value in {"graph": 1.0, "models": 0.8, "language": 0.5, "uncertainty": -0.5}.items():
    if word in index:
        f[index[word]] = value

energy_matrix = float(f.T @ L @ f)
energy_sum = 0.0
for i in range(len(vocab)):
    for j in range(len(vocab)):
        energy_sum += 0.5 * A[i, j] * (f[i] - f[j]) ** 2

print("f^T L f:", round(energy_matrix, 6))
print("edge-sum formula:", round(energy_sum, 6))
print("smallest Laplacian eigenvalues:", np.round(np.linalg.eigvalsh(L)[:5], 6))

28.18 23.18 Python example: random walk and PageRank


We now convert the adjacency matrix to a row-stochastic transition matrix.




def row_stochastic(A):
    row_sums = A.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    P = np.zeros_like(A, dtype=float)
    mask = row_sums[:, 0] > 0
    P[mask] = A[mask] / row_sums[mask]
    P[~mask, ~mask] = 1.0
    return P

P = row_stochastic(A)

alpha = 0.85
n = A.shape[0]
v = np.ones(n) / n
p = v.copy()
for _ in range(100):
    p = alpha * (p @ P) + (1 - alpha) * v

pagerank = pd.DataFrame({"word": vocab, "pagerank": p}).sort_values("pagerank", ascending=False)
print(pagerank.head(8).to_string(index=False))
print("sum of scores:", round(p.sum(), 6))

28.19 23.19 Python example: label propagation


Suppose we label a few words as mathematical/technical and a few as nontechnical. Label propagation spreads these scores across the graph.




Y = np.zeros(len(vocab))
for word, value in {
    "graph": 1.0,
    "probability": 1.0,
    "neural": 1.0,
    "tokens": 0.4,
    "uncertainty": 0.8,
    "and": -0.5,
    "with": -0.5

    if word in index:
        Y[index[word]] = value

alpha = 0.7
F = Y.copy()
for _ in range(20):

28.25 23.25 Worked calculation


Consider the undirected path graph with three nodes:


\[
1-2-3.
\]


The adjacency matrix is


\[
A=\begin{bmatrix}
0 & 1 & 0\\
1 & 0 & 1\\
0 & 1 & 0
\end{bmatrix}.
\]


The degree matrix is


\[
D=\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 & 0\\
0 & 2 & 0\\
0 & 0 & 1
\end{bmatrix}.
\]


The Laplacian is


\[
L=D-A=
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & -1 & 0\\
-1 & 2 & -1\\
0 & -1 & 1
\end{bmatrix}.
\]


For a signal \(f=(1,0,-1)^T\),


\[

so


\[

Using the edge formula gives


\[
(1-0)^2 + (0-(-1))^2 = 2.

Thus the Laplacian energy equals the sum of squared differences over undirected edges.

Language is not only a sequence.  A sentence has syntactic relations, a document collection has citation and hyperlink structure, a knowledge base has subject-relation-object triples, and a corpus has co-occurrence patterns among words.  Graphs provide a mathematical language for representing these relational structures.  This chapter develops graph-based NLP from a clean mathematical point of view: nodes, edges, matrices, Laplacians, walks, propagation, and message passing.

## 23.1 Learning goals

After reading this chapter, students should be able to:

- convert graphs into adjacency, degree, incidence, and Laplacian matrices;
- distinguish directed, undirected, weighted, bipartite, heterogeneous, and relational graphs;
- construct word co-occurrence graphs, dependency graphs, document-term graphs, and knowledge graphs;
- interpret graph walks, centrality, PageRank, and label propagation mathematically;
- derive one layer of graph neural message passing;

::: {.callout-note}
## Guiding question
When should an NLP problem be represented as a graph rather than as a sequence or a bag of words?

A graph is useful when the relational structure is part of the signal.  The important mathematical decision is not "use a graph neural network" but first: choose nodes, choose edges, choose weights, and decide what invariances the representation should have.
:::

## 23.2 From sequences to relational objects

Earlier chapters represented a text as a sequence

$$
w_{1:T}=(w_1,\\ldots,w_T)
$$

over a vocabulary $V$, or as a vector $x\in R^d$.  These representations are powerful but not always natural for relational information.

A graph representation starts with a set of nodes

$$
\mathcal V = \{1,2,\ldots,n\}
$$

and a set of edges

$$
\mathcal E \subseteq \mathcal V \times \mathcal V.
$$

In NLP, nodes may represent:

- tokens in a sentence;
- word types in a vocabulary;
- documents in a corpus;
- entities in a knowledge base;
- concepts in an ontology;
- users, questions, courses, or resources in an advising system.

Edges may represent:

- adjacency in a sentence;
- syntactic dependency;
- word co-occurrence;
- citation or hyperlink;
- semantic relation;
- membership of a word in a document;
- retrieval similarity.

Thus a graph is not just a data structure.  It is a modeling assumption about which relations are relevant.

::: {.math-card}
**Graph modeling decision.**  A graph-based NLP model begins with a map

$$
\text{text data} \longmapsto G=(\mathcal V,\mathcal E,W,X),
$$

where $W$ stores edge weights and $X$ stores node features.  The quality of the downstream model depends strongly on this construction.
:::

## 23.3 Basic graph definitions

An undirected weighted graph on $n$ nodes is described by a symmetric adjacency matrix

$$
A\in R^{n\times n},
\qquad
A_{ij}=A_{ji}\ge 0.
$$

Here $A_{ij}>0$ means that nodes $i$ and $j$ are connected, and $A_{ij}$ gives the edge weight.  For an unweighted graph, $A_{ij}\in \{0,1\}$.

A directed graph uses a possibly nonsymmetric adjacency matrix.  Then $A_{ij}>0$ may mean an edge from node $i$ to node $j$.  The direction convention must be stated because different software packages use different conventions.

The weighted degree of node $i$ is

$$
d_i = \sum_{j=1}^n A_{ij}.
$$

The degree matrix is diagonal:

$$
D=\operatorname{diag}(d_1,\ldots,d_n).
$$

For undirected graphs, the combinatorial graph Laplacian is

$$
L=D-A.
$$

The normalized adjacency matrix is often written

$$
S=D^{-1/2} A D^{-1/2},
$$

and the normalized Laplacian is

$$
L_{\mathrm{norm}} = I-D^{-1/2}AD^{-1/2}=I-S.
$$

When isolated nodes occur, $d_i=0$, and the inverse degree factors must be handled carefully.

## 23.4 Why the Laplacian matters

For a scalar function $f\in R^n$ on nodes, the quadratic form of the Laplacian is

$$
f^T L f
= \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^n A_{ij}(f_i-f_j)^2.
$$

This identity is central.  It says that $f^T L f$ is small when connected nodes have similar values.  In NLP, $f_i$ may be a label score, a semantic score, or one coordinate of a node embedding.

::: {.callout-tip}
## Smoothness prior on graphs
A graph Laplacian encodes the prior that connected nodes should have similar predictions or representations.  For example, if two documents are very similar, a classifier may penalize assigning very different label scores to them.
:::

The Laplacian also appears in spectral clustering.  Eigenvectors associated with small eigenvalues reveal low-frequency graph signals, meaning functions that vary slowly across edges.  This is the graph analogue of using low-frequency Fourier modes to describe a smooth signal.

## 23.5 A sentence as a dependency graph

A dependency parse represents a sentence by directed grammatical relations.  For example, consider

```text
language models predict tokens
```

A possible simplified dependency graph is:

```text
predict -> models
models  -> language
predict -> tokens
```

The words are nodes.  Directed edges represent syntactic relations.  The main verb `predict` is connected to subject-like and object-like components.

Mathematically, for a sentence with $T$ tokens, a dependency graph has an adjacency matrix

$$
A\in \{0,1\}^{T\times T}.
$$

If $A_{ij}=1$ means token $i$ points to token $j$, then row $i$ lists outgoing syntactic neighbors.  A graph-based model may update a token representation by aggregating information from its syntactic neighbors rather than from all tokens.

Dependency graphs are useful because they remove some irrelevant word-distance effects.  In a long sentence, two syntactically related words may be far apart in the sequence but close in the dependency graph.

## 23.6 Word co-occurrence graphs

A word co-occurrence graph has one node per word type.  Two words are connected if they occur near each other in a corpus.  A simple construction uses a window size $r$.  For every occurrence of word $w_t$, connect it to words $w_s$ satisfying

$$
0 < |s-t| \le r.
$$

The edge weight may count co-occurrence frequency:

$$
A_{ij}=\#\{(s,t): w_s=i, w_t=j, 0<|s-t|\le r\}.
$$

After symmetrization, $A$ becomes an undirected weighted graph.  This graph captures the distributional hypothesis: words appearing in similar neighborhoods tend to have related meanings.

This connects graph methods to Chapter 8.  Co-occurrence matrices, PMI matrices, and word graphs are different views of the same empirical object.

## 23.7 Document-term graphs as bipartite graphs

A document-term matrix

$$
X\in R^{m\times d}
$$

can be interpreted as a bipartite graph.  One part contains document nodes

$$
\{D_1,\ldots,D_m\},
$$

and the other part contains term nodes

$$
\{t_1,\ldots,t_d\}.
$$

There is an edge between document $D_i$ and term $t_j$ with weight $X_{ij}$.  The full adjacency matrix has block form

$$
A_{\\text{bip}}=
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & X \\
X^T & 0
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

This construction lets information propagate between documents through shared terms and between terms through shared documents.  For example, two documents need not share a direct edge; they may become related through paths of length two:

$$
D_i \to t_j \to D_k.
$$

The document-document similarity matrix is

$$
XX^T,
$$

and the term-term similarity matrix is

$$
X^T X.
$$

Thus the familiar Gram matrices from vector-space NLP are also two-step walk matrices in a bipartite graph.

## 23.8 Knowledge graphs

A knowledge graph stores triples

$$
(h,r,t),
$$

where $h$ is a head entity, $r$ is a relation, and $t$ is a tail entity.  For example:

```text
(Transformer, is_a, neural_architecture)
(BERT, uses, self_attention)
(NLP, uses, probability)
```

A knowledge graph is not merely a graph; it is usually a labeled directed multigraph.  Different relations should not always be treated as the same type of edge.

For $R$ relation types, one may define relation-specific adjacency matrices

$$
A^{(1)},\ldots,A^{(R)}.
$$

The entry $A^{(r)}_{ij}=1$ means that relation $r$ connects entity $i$ to entity $j$.  This creates an adjacency tensor

$$
\mathcal A\in R^{R\times n\times n}.
$$

Relation-specific structure is important.  The relation `is_a` is not the same as `part_of`, and neither is the same as `cites` or `teaches`.

## 23.9 Random walks and centrality

Graphs support random walks.  For an undirected weighted graph with degree matrix $D$, the row-stochastic transition matrix is

$$
P=D^{-1}A.
$$

If $p_t\in R^n$ is a row vector distribution over nodes at time $t$, then

$$
p_{t+1}=p_t P.
$$

A stationary distribution satisfies

$$
\pi = \pi P.
$$

For connected non-bipartite undirected graphs, the stationary distribution is proportional to degree:

$$
\pi_i = \frac{d_i}{\sum_j d_j}.
$$

Random walks appear in NLP through ranking, graph-based disambiguation, label propagation, and representation learning.  PageRank modifies the walk by adding teleportation:

$$
p_{t+1}=\alpha p_t P + (1-\alpha)v,
$$

where $v$ is a restart distribution and $0<\alpha<1$.

## 23.10 Label propagation

Suppose a graph has a small set of labeled nodes and many unlabeled nodes.  Let $Y\in R^{n\times K}$ store label scores.  A simple label propagation iteration is

$$
F^{(k+1)} = \alpha S F^{(k)} + (1-\alpha)Y,
$$

where $S$ is a normalized adjacency matrix.  At convergence, if $0<\alpha<1$, the fixed point satisfies

$$
F^* = \alpha S F^* + (1-\alpha)Y,
$$

so

$$
F^* = (1-\alpha)(I-\alpha S)^{-1}Y,
$$

provided the inverse exists.

This formula shows that label propagation is not magic.  It is repeated averaging over a graph with a restart to the initial labels.

In NLP, this can be used to propagate sentiment labels through a word graph, topic labels through a document graph, or resource categories through a knowledge graph.

## 23.11 Message passing as the common abstraction

Many graph neural networks use message passing.  Each node $i$ has a hidden state

$$
h_i^{(\ell)}\in R^{d_\ell}
$$

at layer $\ell$.  A generic message passing layer has the form

$$
m_i^{(\ell)} = \operatorname{AGG}^{(\ell)}\left(\{\psi^{(\ell)}(h_i^{(\ell)},h_j^{(\ell)},e_{ij}) : j\in N(i)\}\right),
$$

then

$$
h_i^{(\ell+1)} = \phi^{(\ell)}\left(h_i^{(\ell)},m_i^{(\ell)}\right).
$$

Here:

- $N(i)$ is the neighborhood of node $i$;
- $e_{ij}$ may store edge features;
- $\psi$ creates messages;
- $\operatorname{AGG}$ aggregates messages, often by sum, mean, or attention;
- $\phi$ updates the node state.

The aggregation should usually be permutation invariant with respect to the ordering of neighbors.  This is the graph analogue of a set function.

## 23.12 A graph convolution layer

A common simplified graph convolution layer is

$$
H^{(\ell+1)} = \sigma(\tilde S H^{(\ell)} W^{(\ell)}),
$$

where:

- $H^{(\ell)}\in R^{n\times d_\ell}$ stores node features;
- $W^{(\ell)}\in R^{d_\ell\times d_{\ell+1}}$ is a trainable weight matrix;
- $\sigma$ is a nonlinear activation;
- $\tilde S$ is a normalized adjacency matrix, often built after adding self-loops.

With self-loops,

$$
\tilde A = A + I,
\qquad
\tilde D_{ii}=\sum_j \tilde A_{ij},
$$

and

$$
\tilde S = \tilde D^{-1/2}\tilde A\tilde D^{-1/2}.
$$

The layer has two operations:

1. mix nearby node features by multiplying by $\tilde S$;
2. transform feature coordinates by multiplying by $W^{(\ell)}$.

::: {.math-card}
**Graph convolution as smoothing plus learning.**  The matrix $\tilde S$ performs graph smoothing.  The matrix $W^{(\ell)}$ learns feature combinations.  Their product creates a trainable local averaging operator on graph signals.
:::

## 23.13 Graph attention

Self-attention from Chapters 13 and 14 allows every token to attend to every other token.  Graph attention restricts attention to graph neighborhoods.  For node $i$, compute a score for each neighbor $j\in N(i)$:

$$
e_{ij} = a(h_i,h_j).
$$

Then normalize over the neighborhood:

$$
\alpha_{ij} = \frac{\exp(e_{ij})}{\sum_{k\in N(i)}\exp(e_{ik})}.
$$

The update is

$$
h_i' = \sigma\left(\sum_{j\in N(i)} \alpha_{ij} W h_j\right).
$$

This is attention on a sparse graph.  In NLP, it can be used on dependency graphs, document graphs, entity graphs, and hybrid graphs connecting documents, passages, entities, and concepts.

## 23.14 Relational message passing

Knowledge graphs require relation-specific updates.  A simple relational message passing layer is

$$
h_i' = \sigma\left(
W_0 h_i + \sum_{r=1}^R\sum_{j\in N_r(i)} \frac{1}{c_{i,r}} W_r h_j
\right),
$$

where $N_r(i)$ is the set of neighbors connected to $i$ by relation $r$, $W_r$ is a relation-specific matrix, and $c_{i,r}$ is a normalization constant.

This is important because edge labels carry meaning.  Treating all relations as identical may lose the distinction between syntactic, semantic, citation, and membership relations.

    const A = Array.from({length: n}, function() { return Array(n).fill(0); });
    const index = Object.fromEntries(nodes.map(function(name, idx) { return [name, idx]; }));
    edges.forEach(function(e) {
      if (e[2] >= threshold) {
        const i = index[e[0]];
        const j = index[e[1]];
        A[i][j] += e[2];
        A[j][i] += e[2];
      }
    });
    const P = Array.from({length: n}, function() { return Array(n).fill(0); });
    for (let i = 0; i < n; i++) {
      const d = A[i].reduce(function(a, b) { return a + b; }, 0);
      if (d > 0) {
        for (let j = 0; j < n; j++) P[i][j] = A[i][j] / d;
      } else {
        P[i][i] = 1;
      }
    }
    return {A: A, P: P};
  }

  function propagate(alpha, steps, threshold) {
    const mats = rowStochasticMatrix(threshold);
    const n = nodes.length;
    const f0 = nodes.map(function(name) { return initial[name]; });
    let f = f0.slice();
    for (let s = 0; s < steps; s++) {
      const Pf = Array(n).fill(0);
      for (let i = 0; i < n; i++) {
        for (let j = 0; j < n; j++) Pf[i] += mats.P[i][j] * f[j];
      }
      f = f.map(function(val, i) { return (1 - alpha) * f0[i] + alpha * Pf[i]; });
    }
    return {values: f, A: mats.A};
  }

## 23.16 Python example: build a word co-occurrence graph

The following example builds a weighted undirected word graph from a tiny corpus.  The code avoids external graph libraries so that the matrices are visible.

```{python}
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from collections import Counter

corpus = [
    "graph models represent language structure",
    "language models use tokens and context",
    "probability models describe uncertainty",
    "graph neural networks pass messages",
    "tokens cooccur with context in language"
]

def tokenize(text):
    return text.lower().split()

tokenized = [tokenize(doc) for doc in corpus]
vocab = sorted(set(tok for doc in tokenized for tok in doc))
index = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(vocab)}

window = 2
A = np.zeros((len(vocab), len(vocab)), dtype=float)
for doc in tokenized:
    for t, w in enumerate(doc):
        i = index[w]
        for s in range(max(0, t - window), min(len(doc), t + window + 1)):
            if s == t:
                continue
            j = index[doc[s]]
            A[i, j] += 1

# Symmetrize the directed window counts.
A = 0.5 * (A + A.T)

degrees = A.sum(axis=1)
summary = pd.DataFrame({"word": vocab, "degree": degrees}).sort_values("degree", ascending=False)
print(summary.head(8).to_string(index=False))
```

The matrix $A$ is a weighted adjacency matrix.  The degree of a word is the total co-occurrence weight attached to it.

## 23.17 Python example: Laplacian and smoothness

For an undirected graph, the Laplacian is $L=D-A$.  The quantity $f^TLf$ measures how much a signal varies across edges.

```{python}
D = np.diag(A.sum(axis=1))
L = D - A

f = np.zeros(len(vocab))
for word, value in {"graph": 1.0, "models": 0.8, "language": 0.5, "uncertainty": -0.5}.items():
    if word in index:
        f[index[word]] = value

energy_matrix = float(f.T @ L @ f)
energy_sum = 0.0
for i in range(len(vocab)):
    for j in range(len(vocab)):
        energy_sum += 0.5 * A[i, j] * (f[i] - f[j]) ** 2

print("f^T L f:", round(energy_matrix, 6))
print("edge-sum formula:", round(energy_sum, 6))
print("smallest Laplacian eigenvalues:", np.round(np.linalg.eigvalsh(L)[:5], 6))
```

The two values agree up to numerical precision.  This verifies the identity

$$
f^T L f = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j} A_{ij}(f_i-f_j)^2.
$$

## 23.18 Python example: random walk and PageRank

We now convert the adjacency matrix to a row-stochastic transition matrix.

```{python}
def row_stochastic(A):
    row_sums = A.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    P = np.zeros_like(A, dtype=float)
    mask = row_sums[:, 0] > 0
    P[mask] = A[mask] / row_sums[mask]
    P[~mask, ~mask] = 1.0
    return P

P = row_stochastic(A)

alpha = 0.85
n = A.shape[0]
v = np.ones(n) / n
p = v.copy()
for _ in range(100):
    p = alpha * (p @ P) + (1 - alpha) * v

pagerank = pd.DataFrame({"word": vocab, "pagerank": p}).sort_values("pagerank", ascending=False)
print(pagerank.head(8).to_string(index=False))
print("sum of scores:", round(p.sum(), 6))
```

PageRank is a smoothed centrality measure.  In a word graph, high PageRank words are connected to many other important words, but this should not be confused with semantic importance in every task.

## 23.19 Python example: label propagation

Suppose we label a few words as mathematical/technical and a few as nontechnical.  Label propagation spreads these scores across the graph.

```{python}
Y = np.zeros(len(vocab))
for word, value in {
    "graph": 1.0,
    "probability": 1.0,
    "neural": 1.0,
    "tokens": 0.4,
    "uncertainty": 0.8,
    "and": -0.5,
    "with": -0.5
}.items():
    if word in index:
        Y[index[word]] = value

alpha = 0.7
F = Y.copy()
for _ in range(20):
    F = alpha * (P @ F) + (1 - alpha) * Y

propagation = pd.DataFrame({"word": vocab, "score": F}).sort_values("score", ascending=False)
print(propagation.head(10).to_string(index=False))
```

The formula is simple, but the modeling choices matter.  If the graph is noisy, propagation spreads noise.  If the graph is meaningful, propagation spreads useful information.

## 23.20 Python example: one graph convolution layer

We implement one normalized graph convolution layer

$$
H' = \operatorname{ReLU}(\tilde S H W).
$$

```{python}
rng = np.random.default_rng(7243)

A_tilde = A + np.eye(len(vocab))
d_tilde = A_tilde.sum(axis=1)
D_inv_sqrt = np.diag(1 / np.sqrt(np.maximum(d_tilde, 1e-12)))
S_tilde = D_inv_sqrt @ A_tilde @ D_inv_sqrt

# Simple node features: degree, PageRank, and seeded label score.
H = np.column_stack([
    degrees / max(degrees.max(), 1),
    p / p.max(),
    Y
])

W = rng.normal(scale=0.5, size=(H.shape[1], 2))
Z = S_tilde @ H @ W
H_next = np.maximum(Z, 0.0)

print("H shape:", H.shape)
print("W shape:", W.shape)
print("H_next shape:", H_next.shape)
print(pd.DataFrame(H_next, index=vocab, columns=["coord_1", "coord_2"]).head())
```

This example is not meant to train a useful model.  It is meant to make the dimensions visible.

## 23.22 Python example: graph attention weights

The following code computes attention weights over graph neighbors for one node.  It mirrors the attention formulas from Chapters 13 and 14 but restricts the normalization to neighbors.

```{python}
# Use H as node features from the previous example.
q = rng.normal(size=H.shape[1])
k = rng.normal(size=H.shape[1])
node = index["models"] if "models" in index else 0
neighbors = np.where(A[node] > 0)[0]

scores = []
for j in neighbors:
    score = (H[node] @ q) + (H[j] @ k)
    scores.append(score)
scores = np.array(scores)
weights = np.exp(scores - scores.max())
weights = weights / weights.sum()

attn_table = pd.DataFrame({
    "neighbor": [vocab[j] for j in neighbors],
    "score": scores,
    "attention_weight": weights
}).sort_values("attention_weight", ascending=False)
print("central node:", vocab[node])
print(attn_table.to_string(index=False))
```

Graph attention is local self-attention on a specified graph.  The graph determines which attention scores are even allowed to exist.

## 23.24 Common mistakes

1. **Confusing a graph with a drawing.**  The graph is the pair $(\mathcal V,\mathcal E)$ and its weights, not the plotted layout.
2. **Forgetting direction.**  A dependency relation and its reverse are not the same unless explicitly symmetrized.
3. **Using unnormalized adjacency blindly.**  High-degree nodes can dominate message passing.
4. **Ignoring edge labels.**  Knowledge graph relations often require relation-specific parameters.
5. **Over-smoothing.**  Too many propagation layers can make node representations too similar.
6. **Assuming graph edges are true semantics.**  Edges are constructed from data and assumptions; they may be noisy.

## 23.25 Worked calculation

Consider the undirected path graph with three nodes:

$$
1-2-3.
$$

The adjacency matrix is

$$
A=\begin{bmatrix}
0 & 1 & 0\\
1 & 0 & 1\\
0 & 1 & 0
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The degree matrix is

$$
D=\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 & 0\\
0 & 2 & 0\\
0 & 0 & 1
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The Laplacian is

$$
L=D-A=
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & -1 & 0\\
-1 & 2 & -1\\
0 & -1 & 1
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

For a signal $f=(1,0,-1)^T$,

$$
Lf = (1,0,-1)^T,
$$

so

$$
f^TLf = 2.
$$

Using the edge formula gives

$$
(1-0)^2 + (0-(-1))^2 = 2.
$$

Thus the Laplacian energy equals the sum of squared differences over undirected edges.

## 23.29 What to remember

- A graph representation is a modeling decision about relations.
- Matrices make graph structure computable: $A$, $D$, $L$, $P$, and $S$.
- The Laplacian measures variation of signals across edges.
- Bipartite document-term graphs connect vector-space NLP with graph walks.
- Message passing is local aggregation followed by transformation.
- Graph attention is attention restricted by an edge set.
- Knowledge graphs require relation-specific structure.
- Graph methods are powerful when edges represent meaningful relations and risky when edges are noisy or arbitrary.
