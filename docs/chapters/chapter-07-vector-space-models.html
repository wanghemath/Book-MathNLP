12  Vector Space Models

Document-term matrices, cosine retrieval, SVD, and latent semantic structure

In the previous chapters, documents were represented by count vectors, TF-IDF vectors, and probabilistic models. In this chapter we study these representations from a systematic linear-algebraic point of view. A corpus becomes a matrix, documents become rows, vocabulary terms become columns, similarity becomes an inner product, and latent semantic structure is extracted by projection onto low-dimensional subspaces.

The central mathematical tool is the singular value decomposition. It turns a large sparse document-term matrix into a sequence of best low-rank approximations. This gives the classical method known as latent semantic analysis and also prepares us for modern embedding models.

12.1 Learning goals

After studying this chapter, you should be able to:

  1. represent a corpus as a document-term matrix \(X\in R^{n\times d}\);
  2. interpret rows, columns, row space, column space, rank, and Gram matrices in NLP terms;
  3. compute document similarity using inner products and cosine similarity;
  4. express retrieval scores in matrix form;
  5. explain why normalization changes the geometry of document comparison;
  6. state the singular value decomposition and connect it to eigenvalue problems;
  7. use the Eckart–Young–Mirsky theorem to justify low-rank approximation;
  8. construct latent semantic analysis from a truncated SVD;
  9. compare raw vector-space retrieval with low-rank latent retrieval;
  10. implement the main ideas in Python using NumPy;
  11. use Plotly.js to visualize latent document geometry;
  12. use AI tools to critique representation choices, identify hidden assumptions, and explain model behavior.

Throughout this chapter, \(R\) denotes the real numbers. In code we use ordinary NumPy arrays, but the mathematical object is a matrix representation of a finite corpus.

12.2 The main object: a document-term matrix

Let

\[ D=\{d_1,d_2,\ldots,d_n\} \]

be a corpus of \(n\) documents, and let

\[ V=\{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_d\} \]

be a fixed vocabulary of \(d\) tokens. A vector-space model chooses a representation map

\[ \phi:D\longrightarrow R^d. \]

The \(i\)th document is represented by

\[ x_i=\phi(d_i)=(x_{i1},x_{i2},\ldots,x_{id})\in R^d. \]

The full corpus is represented by the matrix

\[ X= \begin{bmatrix} --- & x_1^T & ---\\ --- & x_2^T & ---\\ & \vdots & \\ --- & x_n^T & --- \end{bmatrix} \in R^{n\times d}. \]

The entry \(X_{ij}\) is the weight of term \(v_j\) in document \(d_i\). Depending on the representation, \(X_{ij}\) may be a count, a binary indicator, a relative frequency, or a TF-IDF weight.

Document-term matrix. A corpus is converted into a matrix

\[ X\in R^{n\times d}, \]

where rows index documents and columns index vocabulary terms. Most classical NLP methods can be described as operations on this matrix.

This viewpoint is powerful because it reduces many NLP operations to familiar linear algebra:

NLP operation Linear algebra operation
represent a document choose a row vector \(x_i\in R^d\)
compare two documents compute \(x_i^T x_j\) or cosine similarity
compare all documents compute the Gram matrix \(XX^T\)
compare all terms compute the Gram matrix \(X^TX\)
remove unimportant dimensions project onto a subspace
find latent topics compute a low-rank approximation
search with a query compute matrix-vector scores \(Xq\)

12.3 Rows, columns, and geometry

The row vector \(x_i\in R^d\) describes document \(d_i\) as a point in vocabulary space. The \(j\)th column

\[ X_{:j}=\begin{bmatrix}X_{1j}\\X_{2j}\\\vdots\\X_{nj}\end{bmatrix}\in R^n \]

describes how term \(v_j\) is distributed across documents.

These two perspectives are dual:

  • rows describe documents by terms;
  • columns describe terms by documents.

The document Gram matrix is

\[ G_D=XX^T\in R^{n\times n}, \]

with entries

\[ (G_D)_{ij}=x_i^Tx_j. \]

The term Gram matrix is

\[ G_T=X^TX\in R^{d\times d}, \]

with entries

\[ (G_T)_{jk}=X_{:j}^T X_{:k}. \]

Thus \(G_D\) measures document-document similarity, while \(G_T\) measures term-term co-occurrence similarity.

Two Gram matrices.

\[ XX^T \quad \text{compares documents}, \]

while

\[ X^TX \quad \text{compares terms}. \]

The singular value decomposition links these two matrices through the same singular values.

12.4 Inner product retrieval

Suppose a query is represented by a vector

\[ q\in R^d. \]

A simple retrieval score for document \(d_i\) is

\[ s_i=x_i^Tq. \]

For the whole corpus, the score vector is

\[ s=Xq\in R^n. \]

Documents with larger scores are considered more relevant. This is the foundation of classical vector-space search.

The inner product has a clear meaning for nonnegative text vectors:

\[ x_i^Tq=\sum_{j=1}^d x_{ij}q_j. \]

Only terms that appear in both the document and the query contribute positively. Large weights produce larger scores.

However, raw inner products favor long documents because long documents tend to have larger norms. To reduce this length bias, we often use cosine similarity.

12.5 Cosine similarity and normalized geometry

For nonzero vectors \(x,y\in R^d\), the cosine similarity is

\[ \operatorname{cos}(x,y)=\frac{x^Ty}{\lVert x\rVert_2\lVert y\rVert_2}. \]

This is the inner product after projecting both vectors to the unit sphere:

\[ \widehat x=\frac{x}{\lVert x\rVert_2}, \qquad \widehat y=\frac{y}{\lVert y\rVert_2}. \]

Then

\[ \operatorname{cos}(x,y)=\widehat x^T\widehat y. \]

Cosine similarity compares direction rather than magnitude. In document analysis, direction often represents relative term usage, while magnitude is partly influenced by document length.

If \(\widetilde X\) is the row-normalized document matrix, then all pairwise cosine similarities are contained in

\[ C=\widetilde X\widetilde X^T. \]

Similarly, query scores are

\[ s=\widetilde X\widehat q. \]

Normalization changes the model. Raw inner products ask: “How much weighted vocabulary overlap is there?” Cosine similarity asks: “How similar are the directions of the two term-weight vectors?” These are different statistical assumptions about what should count as similarity.

12.7 Python example: cosine search from scratch

Now we represent a query and rank the documents by cosine similarity.

def vectorize_query(query, vocab_index):
    q = np.zeros(len(vocab_index), dtype=float)
    for token in tokenize(query):
        if token in vocab_index:
            q[vocab_index[token]] += 1.0
    return q

def row_normalize(A, eps=1e-12):
    norms = np.linalg.norm(A, axis=1, keepdims=True)

12.8 Term weighting as diagonal scaling

Raw count matrices are often not the best representation. Common words may dominate similarity. TF-IDF corrects this by increasing the weight of terms that are frequent in a document but rare across the corpus.

Let

\[ \operatorname{tf}_{ij}=X_{ij} \]

or a transformed frequency such as \(\log(1+X_{ij})\). The document frequency of term \(j\) is

\[ \operatorname{df}_j=\#\{i:X_{ij}>0\}. \]

A smoothed inverse document frequency is

\[ \operatorname{idf}_j=\log\left(\frac{1+n}{1+\operatorname{df}_j}\right)+1. \]

Then

\[ X^{\operatorname{tfidf}}_{ij}=\operatorname{tf}_{ij}\operatorname{idf}_j. \]

In matrix form, this is column scaling:

\[ X^{\operatorname{tfidf}}=XW, \]

where

\[ W=\operatorname{diag}(\operatorname{idf}_1,\ldots,\operatorname{idf}_d). \]

Thus TF-IDF is not just a heuristic. It is a diagonal linear transformation of the feature space.

def tfidf_transform(X):
    n = X.shape[0]
    df = np.sum(X > 0, axis=0)
    idf = np.log((1 + n) / (1 + df)) + 1
    return X * idf, idf

12.9 Rank and latent structure

The rank of \(X\) is

\[ \operatorname{rank}(X)=\dim(\operatorname{Row}(X))=\dim(\operatorname{Col}(X)). \]

In NLP, a high-dimensional vocabulary space often contains redundancy:

  • different words may carry related meanings;
  • topics create correlations among terms;
  • document collections often lie near lower-dimensional structures;
  • noise and rare words inflate the apparent dimension.

A low-rank approximation tries to replace \(X\) by a simpler matrix

\[ X_k\in R^{n\times d}, \qquad \operatorname{rank}(X_k)\le k, \]

where \(k\ll \min(n,d)\).

The goal is to keep the most important geometric structure while discarding high-dimensional noise.

12.10 Singular value decomposition

Every matrix \(X\in R^{n\times d}\) has a singular value decomposition

\[ X=U\Sigma V^T, \]

where:

  • \(U\in R^{n\times r}\) has orthonormal columns;
  • \(V\in R^{d\times r}\) has orthonormal columns;
  • \(\Sigma=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_r)\);
  • \(r=\operatorname{rank}(X)\);
  • \(\sigma_1\ge \sigma_2\ge \cdots\ge \sigma_r>0\).

The columns of \(U\) are left singular vectors. The columns of \(V\) are right singular vectors. The singular values measure how much variation lies in each singular direction.

The SVD diagonalizes the two Gram matrices:

\[ XX^T=U\Sigma^2U^T, \]

and

\[ X^TX=V\Sigma^2V^T. \]

Thus left singular vectors describe principal directions among documents, while right singular vectors describe principal directions among terms.

12.11 Best low-rank approximation

For \(k<r\), define

\[ U_k=[u_1,\ldots,u_k], \qquad V_k=[v_1,\ldots,v_k], \qquad \Sigma_k=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_k). \]

The rank-\(k\) truncated SVD approximation is

\[ X_k=U_k\Sigma_kV_k^T. \]

The Eckart–Young–Mirsky theorem says that \(X_k\) is the best rank-\(k\) approximation to \(X\) in Frobenius norm:

\[ X_k=\arg\min_{\operatorname{rank}(A)\le k}\lVert X-A\rVert_F. \]

Moreover,

\[ \lVert X-X_k\rVert_F^2=\sum_{j=k+1}^r \sigma_j^2. \]

This theorem is the mathematical foundation of latent semantic analysis.

SVD interpretation for NLP. The truncated SVD replaces sparse surface-level term matching by a lower-dimensional coordinate system. Documents that do not share many exact words may become close if they have similar coordinates in the latent space.

12.12 Latent semantic analysis

Latent semantic analysis, or LSA, applies truncated SVD to a term-weighted document matrix such as \(X^{\operatorname{tfidf}}\).

Let

\[ X\approx X_k=U_k\Sigma_kV_k^T. \]

The row vector

\[ z_i=(U_k\Sigma_k)_{i:}\in R^k \]

is a \(k\)-dimensional latent representation of document \(d_i\). The row vector

\[ t_j=(V_k\Sigma_k)_{j:}\in R^k \]

may be interpreted as a latent representation of term \(v_j\).

A query vector \(q\in R^d\) can be projected into the same latent coordinate system by

\[ z_q=q^TV_k\in R^k. \]

Then retrieval can be performed by comparing \(z_q\) with the document coordinates \(z_i\).

There are several conventions for scaling latent coordinates. The important point is that documents and queries are compared after projection onto the subspace spanned by the top right singular vectors.

12.14 Reconstruction error

A rank-\(k\) approximation is useful only if it captures enough structure. The following code computes reconstruction error for each possible \(k\).

def svd_reconstruction(X, k):
    U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(X, full_matrices=False)
    return (U[:, :k] * s[:k]) @ Vt[:k, :]

rows = []
for k in range(1, min(X.shape) + 1):
    Xk = svd_reconstruction(X, k)
    error = np.linalg.norm(X - Xk, ord="fro")
    relative_error = error / np.linalg.norm(X, ord="fro")
    rows.append((k, error, relative_error))

12.17 Low-rank retrieval

In raw vector-space retrieval, the query score is computed in the original vocabulary space:

\[ s=Xq. \]

In LSA retrieval, we first project the query into the latent right-singular-vector space:

\[ z_q=q^TV_k. \]

The document coordinates are

\[ Z=XV_k=U_k\Sigma_k. \]

Then the latent retrieval score is

\[ s_i=z_i^Tz_q. \]

In matrix form,

\[ s=Zz_q^T. \]

If cosine similarity is desired, normalize the latent document coordinates and the latent query coordinate before scoring.

def lsa_search(X, q, k):
    U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(X, full_matrices=False)
    Vk = Vt[:k, :].T
    Z_docs = X @ Vk
    z_query = q @ Vk
    Zn = row_normalize(Z_docs)
    zqn = z_query / max(np.linalg.norm(z_query), 1e-12)
    return Zn @ zqn

12.18 How LSA handles synonymy and polysemy

Two common language difficulties are synonymy and polysemy.

Synonymy means that different words have related meanings. For example, “car” and “automobile” may not match exactly in a count-vector model. If they occur in similar document contexts, a low-rank model may place them near each other.

Polysemy means that one word has multiple meanings. For example, “bank” may refer to a financial institution or a river bank. A simple vector-space model assigns one column to the token “bank,” so it cannot fully separate meanings. LSA may partially separate contexts through document-level directions, but it still lacks the contextual flexibility of modern neural embeddings.

What LSA can and cannot do. LSA can discover linear correlation structure in a corpus. It cannot truly understand syntax, word order, or context-dependent meaning. Its strength is low-rank linear geometry; its limitation is that language is not purely linear bag-of-words geometry.

12.19 Centering, scaling, and preprocessing as linear transformations

Many preprocessing steps can be described as transformations of \(X\).

12.19.1 Row normalization

Row normalization replaces each document vector by

\[ \widehat x_i=\frac{x_i}{\lVert x_i\rVert_2}. \]

This maps documents to the unit sphere in \(R^d\).

12.19.2 Column scaling

TF-IDF is column scaling:

\[ X\mapsto XW. \]

This changes the metric by giving different importance to different vocabulary dimensions.

12.19.3 Stop-word removal

Removing a set of stop-word columns is projection onto a coordinate subspace. If \(S\) is the set of retained columns, then

\[ X\mapsto XP_S, \]

where \(P_S\) selects the retained vocabulary coordinates.

12.19.4 Centering

Centering subtracts the column mean:

\[ X_c=X-\mathbf{1}\mu^T, \]

where

\[ \mu=\frac{1}{n}X^T\mathbf{1}. \]

Centering is standard in PCA, but it must be used carefully in sparse nonnegative text matrices because it destroys sparsity and creates negative entries.

12.20 PCA and LSA: same theorem, different data convention

PCA is usually applied to centered data. LSA is usually applied to uncentered TF-IDF data. Both use the SVD.

For centered data \(X_c\), PCA finds directions maximizing variance:

\[ v_1=\arg\max_{\lVert v\rVert=1}\lVert X_cv\rVert_2^2. \]

The solution is the top right singular vector of \(X_c\).

LSA instead applies SVD to a sparse term-weight matrix, often without centering:

\[ X^{\operatorname{tfidf}}=U\Sigma V^T. \]

The purpose is not variance analysis in the classical statistical sense, but low-rank semantic approximation for retrieval and representation.

12.21 A statistical interpretation

A document-term matrix can be viewed as data sampled from an underlying distribution. Suppose each document is generated from a mixture of latent topics. Then terms that often occur together across documents produce correlated columns in \(X\).

A low-rank model assumes that much of this correlation can be explained by a small number of latent factors. Informally,

\[ X\approx AB^T, \]

where

\[ A\in R^{n\times k} \]

contains document factors and

\[ B\in R^{d\times k} \]

contains term factors.

The SVD gives the best unconstrained linear factorization under squared error. Later models, such as nonnegative matrix factorization and topic models, impose additional constraints or probabilistic assumptions.

12.22 Worked hand calculation

Consider three documents and four terms:

\[ X= \begin{bmatrix} 1&1&0&0\\ 1&0&1&0\\ 0&0&1&1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

The document Gram matrix is

\[ XX^T= \begin{bmatrix} 2&1&0\\ 1&2&1\\ 0&1&2 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Thus documents 1 and 2 overlap in one term, documents 2 and 3 overlap in one term, and documents 1 and 3 do not overlap. The cosine similarity matrix is obtained by dividing each row and column by document norms. Since every row has norm \(\sqrt{2}\),

\[ C=\frac{1}{2}XX^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1&1/2&0\\ 1/2&1&1/2\\ 0&1/2&1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

This example shows why vector-space models can be understood entirely through matrix products.

12.24 Common mistakes

  1. Confusing documents and terms. If \(X\in R^{n\times d}\), rows are documents and columns are terms. Some books use the transpose convention, so always check the orientation.
  2. Using raw inner products without thinking about length. Long documents can dominate raw scores.
  3. Calling SVD topics probabilities. SVD factors are real-valued and may be negative. They are not probability distributions.
  4. Assuming low-rank always improves retrieval. Low-rank projection can remove noise, but it can also remove important rare information.
  5. Overinterpreting two-dimensional plots. A 2D map may be useful for visualization but may not preserve all relevant distances.

## The main object: a document-term matrix

Let

$$
D=\{d_1,d_2,\ldots,d_n\}
$$

be a corpus of $n$ documents, and let

$$
V=\{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_d\}
$$

be a fixed vocabulary of $d$ tokens.  A vector-space model chooses a representation map

$$
\phi:D\longrightarrow R^d.
$$

The $i$th document is represented by

$$
x_i=\phi(d_i)=(x_{i1},x_{i2},\ldots,x_{id})\in R^d.
$$

The full corpus is represented by the matrix

$$
X=
\begin{bmatrix}
--- & x_1^T & ---\\
--- & x_2^T & ---\\
& \vdots & \\
--- & x_n^T & ---
\end{bmatrix}
\in R^{n\times d}.
$$

The entry $X_{ij}$ is the weight of term $v_j$ in document $d_i$.  Depending on the representation, $X_{ij}$ may be a count, a binary indicator, a relative frequency, or a TF-IDF weight.

::: {.math-card}
**Document-term matrix.**  A corpus is converted into a matrix

$$
X\in R^{n\times d},
$$

where rows index documents and columns index vocabulary terms.  Most classical NLP methods can be described as operations on this matrix.
:::

This viewpoint is powerful because it reduces many NLP operations to familiar linear algebra:

| NLP operation | Linear algebra operation |
|---|---|
| represent a document | choose a row vector $x_i\in R^d$ |
| compare two documents | compute $x_i^T x_j$ or cosine similarity |
| compare all documents | compute the Gram matrix $XX^T$ |
| compare all terms | compute the Gram matrix $X^TX$ |
| remove unimportant dimensions | project onto a subspace |
| find latent topics | compute a low-rank approximation |
| search with a query | compute matrix-vector scores $Xq$ |

## Rows, columns, and geometry

The row vector $x_i\in R^d$ describes document $d_i$ as a point in vocabulary space.  The $j$th column

$$
X_{:j}=\begin{bmatrix}X_{1j}\\X_{2j}\\\vdots\\X_{nj}\end{bmatrix}\in R^n
$$

describes how term $v_j$ is distributed across documents.

These two perspectives are dual:

- rows describe **documents by terms**;
- columns describe **terms by documents**.

The document Gram matrix is

$$
G_D=XX^T\in R^{n\times n},
$$

with entries

$$
(G_D)_{ij}=x_i^Tx_j.
$$

The term Gram matrix is

$$
G_T=X^TX\in R^{d\times d},
$$

with entries

$$
(G_T)_{jk}=X_{:j}^T X_{:k}.
$$

Thus $G_D$ measures document-document similarity, while $G_T$ measures term-term co-occurrence similarity.

::: {.math-card}
**Two Gram matrices.**

$$
XX^T \quad \text{compares documents},
$$

while

$$
X^TX \quad \text{compares terms}.
$$

The singular value decomposition links these two matrices through the same singular values.
:::

## Inner product retrieval

Suppose a query is represented by a vector

$$
q\in R^d.
$$

A simple retrieval score for document $d_i$ is

$$
s_i=x_i^Tq.
$$

For the whole corpus, the score vector is

$$
s=Xq\in R^n.
$$

Documents with larger scores are considered more relevant.  This is the foundation of classical vector-space search.

The inner product has a clear meaning for nonnegative text vectors:

$$
x_i^Tq=\sum_{j=1}^d x_{ij}q_j.
$$

Only terms that appear in both the document and the query contribute positively.  Large weights produce larger scores.

However, raw inner products favor long documents because long documents tend to have larger norms.  To reduce this length bias, we often use cosine similarity.

## Cosine similarity and normalized geometry

For nonzero vectors $x,y\in R^d$, the cosine similarity is

$$
\operatorname{cos}(x,y)=\frac{x^Ty}{\lVert x\rVert_2\lVert y\rVert_2}.
$$

This is the inner product after projecting both vectors to the unit sphere:

$$
\widehat x=\frac{x}{\lVert x\rVert_2},
\qquad
\widehat y=\frac{y}{\lVert y\rVert_2}.
$$

Then

$$
\operatorname{cos}(x,y)=\widehat x^T\widehat y.
$$

Cosine similarity compares direction rather than magnitude.  In document analysis, direction often represents relative term usage, while magnitude is partly influenced by document length.

If $\widetilde X$ is the row-normalized document matrix, then all pairwise cosine similarities are contained in

$$
C=\widetilde X\widetilde X^T.
$$

Similarly, query scores are

$$
s=\widetilde X\widehat q.
$$

::: {.math-card}
**Normalization changes the model.**  Raw inner products ask: "How much weighted vocabulary overlap is there?"  Cosine similarity asks: "How similar are the directions of the two term-weight vectors?"  These are different statistical assumptions about what should count as similarity.
:::

## Python example: cosine search from scratch

Now we represent a query and rank the documents by cosine similarity.

```{python}
def vectorize_query(query, vocab_index):
    q = np.zeros(len(vocab_index), dtype=float)
    for token in tokenize(query):
        if token in vocab_index:
            q[vocab_index[token]] += 1.0
    return q

def row_normalize(A, eps=1e-12):
    norms = np.linalg.norm(A, axis=1, keepdims=True)
    return A / np.maximum(norms, eps)

qn = q_counts / max(np.linalg.norm(q_counts), 1e-12)
```

## Term weighting as diagonal scaling

Raw count matrices are often not the best representation.  Common words may dominate similarity.  TF-IDF corrects this by increasing the weight of terms that are frequent in a document but rare across the corpus.

Let

$$
\operatorname{tf}_{ij}=X_{ij}
$$

or a transformed frequency such as $\log(1+X_{ij})$.  The document frequency of term $j$ is

$$
\operatorname{df}_j=\#\{i:X_{ij}>0\}.
$$

A smoothed inverse document frequency is

$$
\operatorname{idf}_j=\log\left(\frac{1+n}{1+\operatorname{df}_j}\right)+1.
$$

Then

$$
X^{\operatorname{tfidf}}_{ij}=\operatorname{tf}_{ij}\operatorname{idf}_j.
$$

In matrix form, this is column scaling:

$$
X^{\operatorname{tfidf}}=XW,
$$

where

$$
W=\operatorname{diag}(\operatorname{idf}_1,\ldots,\operatorname{idf}_d).
$$

Thus TF-IDF is not just a heuristic.  It is a diagonal linear transformation of the feature space.

```{python}
def tfidf_transform(X):
    n = X.shape[0]
    df = np.sum(X > 0, axis=0)
    idf = np.log((1 + n) / (1 + df)) + 1
    return X * idf, idf

X_tfidf, idf = tfidf_transform(X_counts)

idf_table = pd.DataFrame({"term": vocab, "idf": idf}).sort_values("idf", ascending=False)
idf_table.head(12)
```

## Rank and latent structure

The rank of $X$ is

$$
\operatorname{rank}(X)=\dim(\operatorname{Row}(X))=\dim(\operatorname{Col}(X)).
$$

In NLP, a high-dimensional vocabulary space often contains redundancy:

- different words may carry related meanings;
- topics create correlations among terms;
- document collections often lie near lower-dimensional structures;
- noise and rare words inflate the apparent dimension.

A low-rank approximation tries to replace $X$ by a simpler matrix

$$
X_k\in R^{n\times d},
\qquad
\operatorname{rank}(X_k)\le k,
$$

where $k\ll \min(n,d)$.

The goal is to keep the most important geometric structure while discarding high-dimensional noise.

## Singular value decomposition

Every matrix $X\in R^{n\times d}$ has a singular value decomposition

$$
X=U\Sigma V^T,
$$

where:

- $U\in R^{n\times r}$ has orthonormal columns;
- $V\in R^{d\times r}$ has orthonormal columns;
- $\Sigma=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_r)$;
- $r=\operatorname{rank}(X)$;
- $\sigma_1\ge \sigma_2\ge \cdots\ge \sigma_r>0$.

The columns of $U$ are left singular vectors.  The columns of $V$ are right singular vectors.  The singular values measure how much variation lies in each singular direction.

The SVD diagonalizes the two Gram matrices:

$$
XX^T=U\Sigma^2U^T,
$$

and

$$
X^TX=V\Sigma^2V^T.
$$

Thus left singular vectors describe principal directions among documents, while right singular vectors describe principal directions among terms.

## Best low-rank approximation

For $k<r$, define

$$
U_k=[u_1,\ldots,u_k],
\qquad
V_k=[v_1,\ldots,v_k],
\qquad
\Sigma_k=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_k).
$$

The rank-$k$ truncated SVD approximation is

$$
X_k=U_k\Sigma_kV_k^T.
$$

The Eckart--Young--Mirsky theorem says that $X_k$ is the best rank-$k$ approximation to $X$ in Frobenius norm:

$$
X_k=\arg\min_{\operatorname{rank}(A)\le k}\lVert X-A\rVert_F.
$$

Moreover,

$$
\lVert X-X_k\rVert_F^2=\sum_{j=k+1}^r \sigma_j^2.
$$

This theorem is the mathematical foundation of latent semantic analysis.

::: {.math-card}
**SVD interpretation for NLP.**  The truncated SVD replaces sparse surface-level term matching by a lower-dimensional coordinate system.  Documents that do not share many exact words may become close if they have similar coordinates in the latent space.
:::

## Latent semantic analysis

Latent semantic analysis, or LSA, applies truncated SVD to a term-weighted document matrix such as $X^{\operatorname{tfidf}}$.

Let

$$
X\approx X_k=U_k\Sigma_kV_k^T.
$$

The row vector

$$
z_i=(U_k\Sigma_k)_{i:}\in R^k
$$

is a $k$-dimensional latent representation of document $d_i$.  The row vector

$$
t_j=(V_k\Sigma_k)_{j:}\in R^k
$$

may be interpreted as a latent representation of term $v_j$.

A query vector $q\in R^d$ can be projected into the same latent coordinate system by

$$
z_q=q^TV_k\in R^k.
$$

Then retrieval can be performed by comparing $z_q$ with the document coordinates $z_i$.

There are several conventions for scaling latent coordinates.  The important point is that documents and queries are compared after projection onto the subspace spanned by the top right singular vectors.

A rank-$k$ approximation is useful only if it captures enough structure.  The following code computes reconstruction error for each possible $k$.

```{python}
def svd_reconstruction(X, k):
    U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(X, full_matrices=False)
    return (U[:, :k] * s[:k]) @ Vt[:k, :]

rows = []
for k in range(1, min(X.shape) + 1):
    Xk = svd_reconstruction(X, k)
    error = np.linalg.norm(X - Xk, ord="fro")
    relative_error = error / np.linalg.norm(X, ord="fro")
    rows.append((k, error, relative_error))

pd.DataFrame(rows, columns=["k", "frobenius_error", "relative_error"])
```

As $k$ increases, the approximation error decreases.  The mathematical reason is that more singular directions are retained.

## Low-rank retrieval

In raw vector-space retrieval, the query score is computed in the original vocabulary space:

$$
s=Xq.
$$

In LSA retrieval, we first project the query into the latent right-singular-vector space:

$$
z_q=q^TV_k.
$$

The document coordinates are

$$
Z=XV_k=U_k\Sigma_k.
$$

Then the latent retrieval score is

$$
s_i=z_i^Tz_q.
$$

In matrix form,

$$
s=Zz_q^T.
$$

If cosine similarity is desired, normalize the latent document coordinates and the latent query coordinate before scoring.

```{python}
def lsa_search(X, q, k):
    U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(X, full_matrices=False)
    Vk = Vt[:k, :].T
    Z_docs = X @ Vk
    z_query = q @ Vk
    Zn = row_normalize(Z_docs)
    zqn = z_query / max(np.linalg.norm(z_query), 1e-12)
    return Zn @ zqn

for k in [1, 2, 3, 4]:
    scores = lsa_search(X_tfidf, q_tfidf, k=k)
    print(f"\nRank k={k}")
    for rank, i in enumerate(np.argsort(-scores), start=1):
        print(f"{rank}. score={scores[i]: .3f} | {corpus[i]}")
```

Low-rank retrieval may retrieve documents that do not share many exact query words but lie in a related latent direction.  This is the main advantage of LSA over purely surface-level matching.

## How LSA handles synonymy and polysemy

Two common language difficulties are **synonymy** and **polysemy**.

Synonymy means that different words have related meanings.  For example, "car" and "automobile" may not match exactly in a count-vector model.  If they occur in similar document contexts, a low-rank model may place them near each other.

Polysemy means that one word has multiple meanings.  For example, "bank" may refer to a financial institution or a river bank.  A simple vector-space model assigns one column to the token "bank," so it cannot fully separate meanings.  LSA may partially separate contexts through document-level directions, but it still lacks the contextual flexibility of modern neural embeddings.

::: {.math-card}
**What LSA can and cannot do.**  LSA can discover linear correlation structure in a corpus.  It cannot truly understand syntax, word order, or context-dependent meaning.  Its strength is low-rank linear geometry; its limitation is that language is not purely linear bag-of-words geometry.
:::

## Centering, scaling, and preprocessing as linear transformations

Many preprocessing steps can be described as transformations of $X$.

### Row normalization

Row normalization replaces each document vector by

$$
\widehat x_i=\frac{x_i}{\lVert x_i\rVert_2}.
$$

This maps documents to the unit sphere in $R^d$.

### Column scaling

TF-IDF is column scaling:

$$
X\mapsto XW.
$$

This changes the metric by giving different importance to different vocabulary dimensions.

### Stop-word removal

Removing a set of stop-word columns is projection onto a coordinate subspace.  If $S$ is the set of retained columns, then

$$
X\mapsto XP_S,
$$

where $P_S$ selects the retained vocabulary coordinates.

### Centering

Centering subtracts the column mean:

$$
X_c=X-\mathbf{1}\mu^T,
$$

where

$$
\mu=\frac{1}{n}X^T\mathbf{1}.
$$

Centering is standard in PCA, but it must be used carefully in sparse nonnegative text matrices because it destroys sparsity and creates negative entries.

## PCA and LSA: same theorem, different data convention

PCA is usually applied to centered data.  LSA is usually applied to uncentered TF-IDF data.  Both use the SVD.

For centered data $X_c$, PCA finds directions maximizing variance:

$$
v_1=\arg\max_{\lVert v\rVert=1}\lVert X_cv\rVert_2^2.
$$

The solution is the top right singular vector of $X_c$.

LSA instead applies SVD to a sparse term-weight matrix, often without centering:

$$
X^{\operatorname{tfidf}}=U\Sigma V^T.
$$

The purpose is not variance analysis in the classical statistical sense, but low-rank semantic approximation for retrieval and representation.

## A statistical interpretation

A document-term matrix can be viewed as data sampled from an underlying distribution.  Suppose each document is generated from a mixture of latent topics.  Then terms that often occur together across documents produce correlated columns in $X$.

A low-rank model assumes that much of this correlation can be explained by a small number of latent factors.  Informally,

$$
X\approx AB^T,
$$

where

$$
A\in R^{n\times k}
$$

contains document factors and

$$
B\in R^{d\times k}
$$

contains term factors.

The SVD gives the best unconstrained linear factorization under squared error.  Later models, such as nonnegative matrix factorization and topic models, impose additional constraints or probabilistic assumptions.

## Worked hand calculation

Consider three documents and four terms:

$$
X=
\begin{bmatrix}
1&1&0&0\\
1&0&1&0\\
0&0&1&1
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The document Gram matrix is

$$
XX^T=
\begin{bmatrix}
2&1&0\\
1&2&1\\
0&1&2
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Thus documents 1 and 2 overlap in one term, documents 2 and 3 overlap in one term, and documents 1 and 3 do not overlap.  The cosine similarity matrix is obtained by dividing each row and column by document norms.  Since every row has norm $\sqrt{2}$,

$$
C=\frac{1}{2}XX^T
=
\begin{bmatrix}
1&1/2&0\\
1/2&1&1/2\\
0&1/2&1
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

This example shows why vector-space models can be understood entirely through matrix products.

## Common mistakes

1. **Confusing documents and terms.**  If $X\in R^{n\times d}$, rows are documents and columns are terms.  Some books use the transpose convention, so always check the orientation.
2. **Using raw inner products without thinking about length.**  Long documents can dominate raw scores.
3. **Calling SVD topics probabilities.**  SVD factors are real-valued and may be negative.  They are not probability distributions.
4. **Assuming low-rank always improves retrieval.**  Low-rank projection can remove noise, but it can also remove important rare information.
5. **Overinterpreting two-dimensional plots.**  A 2D map may be useful for visualization but may not preserve all relevant distances.
