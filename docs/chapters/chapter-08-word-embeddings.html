13  Word Embeddings

Distributional semantics, matrix factorization, skip-gram models, and geometry of meaning

In Chapter 7, a document was represented as a vector in vocabulary space. The coordinates of that vector were counts, TF-IDF weights, or low-rank latent coordinates derived from a document-term matrix. In this chapter we reverse the viewpoint: instead of representing documents by words, we represent words themselves as vectors.

A word embedding is a map from a discrete vocabulary into a continuous vector space. This is one of the central mathematical ideas in modern NLP. It converts symbolic data into geometric data: words have lengths, angles, neighborhoods, projections, and directions. Once words become vectors, we can use linear algebra, probability, optimization, and geometry to study meaning.

13.1 Learning goals

After studying this chapter, you should be able to:

  1. define a word embedding as a function \(e:V\to R^m\);
  2. explain how one-hot vectors differ from dense embeddings;
  3. construct co-occurrence matrices from a corpus;
  4. interpret the distributional hypothesis mathematically;
  5. compute empirical probabilities for word-context pairs;
  6. define pointwise mutual information and positive PMI;
  7. connect matrix factorization to word embeddings;
  8. derive the skip-gram softmax objective;
  9. explain negative sampling as a computational approximation;
  10. state the GloVe weighted least-squares objective;
  11. analyze embedding geometry using norms, cosine similarity, and analogies;
  12. implement toy embeddings from scratch in Python;
  13. visualize embedding spaces with Plotly and Plotly.js;
  14. use AI tools to critique embedding assumptions and detect possible bias.

Throughout this chapter, \(V=\{v_1,\ldots,v_d\}\) denotes a finite vocabulary, \(d=|V|\) is the vocabulary size, and \(m\) is the embedding dimension, usually with \(m\ll d\).

13.2 From symbols to vectors

A token is a discrete object. If the vocabulary is

\[ V=\{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_d\}, \]

then a token \(v_j\) can be represented by the one-hot vector

\[ \delta_j=(0,\ldots,0,1,0,\ldots,0)^T\in R^d, \]

where the \(1\) occurs in position \(j\). This representation is exact but not semantic. For \(i\neq j\),

\[ \delta_i^T\delta_j=0. \]

Thus every pair of distinct words is equally dissimilar. The words “cat” and “dog” are no closer than “cat” and “gradient”.

A word embedding replaces the one-hot representation with a dense vector:

\[ e:V\longrightarrow R^m, \qquad v_j\longmapsto e_j. \]

Equivalently, the embedding is stored in a matrix

\[ E=\begin{bmatrix} --- & e_1^T & ---\\ --- & e_2^T & ---\\ & \vdots & \\ --- & e_d^T & --- \end{bmatrix} \in R^{d\times m}. \]

The embedding of token \(v_j\) is the \(j\)th row of \(E\):

\[ e_j=E_{j:}\in R^m. \]

If a token is encoded as a one-hot vector \(\delta_j\), then the lookup operation can be written as matrix multiplication:

\[ \delta_j^T E=e_j^T. \]

Embedding lookup. A word embedding matrix \(E\in R^{d\times m}\) converts a one-hot token vector into a dense vector:

\[ \delta_j^TE=e_j^T. \]

This is why an embedding layer in a neural network is mathematically a trainable matrix lookup.

13.3 Why dense embeddings are useful

One-hot vectors live in a high-dimensional coordinate system where each coordinate is an arbitrary vocabulary index. Dense embeddings live in a lower-dimensional representation space in which geometry is learned from data.

The central hope is that semantic or syntactic relationships become geometric relationships:

  • similar words have small distance or large cosine similarity;
  • related categories form clusters;
  • some relationships correspond approximately to directions;
  • analogy tasks can be expressed using vector arithmetic;
  • downstream models can use continuous features rather than sparse symbolic indicators.

This does not mean that embeddings contain “true meaning.” It means that the training objective forces words with similar statistical behavior to receive related vectors.

13.4 The distributional hypothesis

The guiding principle behind many word embedding methods is the distributional hypothesis:

Words that occur in similar contexts tend to have related meanings.

Mathematically, each word \(w\in V\) can be described by a context distribution

\[ P(C=c\mid W=w), \]

where \(C\) is a random context word. If two words \(w\) and \(w'\) have similar conditional context distributions, then they should have nearby representations.

One way to state this is:

\[ P(C=\cdot\mid W=w)\approx P(C=\cdot\mid W=w') \quad\Longrightarrow\quad e(w)\approx e(w'). \]

This is a statistical assumption, not a theorem. It is often useful, but it can fail when words have multiple meanings, when corpora are biased, or when context alone is not enough.

Distributional semantics. A word embedding is a finite-dimensional summary of a word’s empirical context distribution. The geometry of the embedding space reflects statistical regularities in the corpus.

13.5 Co-occurrence matrices

Let a corpus be represented as a token sequence

\[ w_1,w_2,\ldots,w_T, \qquad w_t\in V. \]

Choose a context window size \(h\geq 1\). For each position \(t\), the context of \(w_t\) is the set of nearby positions

\[ \{s:1\leq s\leq T,\ 0<|s-t|\leq h\}. \]

The word-context co-occurrence matrix is

\[ C\in R_{\geq 0}^{d\times d}, \]

where

\[ C_{ij}=\#\{(t,s):w_t=v_i,\ w_s=v_j,\ 0<|s-t|\leq h\}. \]

Rows correspond to center words, and columns correspond to context words. The row

\[ C_{i:} \]

is a count-based description of the contexts in which word \(v_i\) appears.

The simplest possible word representation is to use the row vector \(C_{i:}\) itself. This is usually high-dimensional and sparse. Embedding methods compress or transform this information into lower-dimensional vectors.

13.6 Empirical probabilities

Let

\[ N=\sum_{i=1}^d\sum_{j=1}^d C_{ij} \]

be the total number of observed word-context pairs. The empirical joint distribution is

\[ \widehat P(W=v_i,C=v_j)=\frac{C_{ij}}{N}. \]

The marginal distributions are

\[ \widehat P(W=v_i)=\frac{\sum_j C_{ij}}{N}, \qquad \widehat P(C=v_j)=\frac{\sum_i C_{ij}}{N}. \]

The empirical conditional distribution of contexts given a word is

\[ \widehat P(C=v_j\mid W=v_i) = \frac{C_{ij}}{\sum_{k=1}^d C_{ik}}. \]

This conditional distribution is the probabilistic object that the embedding should summarize.

13.7 PMI and PPMI

A raw co-occurrence count is not enough. Common words such as “the” or “and” co-occur with many words simply because they are frequent. Pointwise mutual information corrects for marginal frequency.

The empirical pointwise mutual information of a word-context pair is

\[ \operatorname{PMI}(v_i,v_j) = \log\frac{\widehat P(W=v_i,C=v_j)} {\widehat P(W=v_i)\widehat P(C=v_j)}. \]

Equivalently, using counts,

\[ \operatorname{PMI}(v_i,v_j) = \log\frac{C_{ij}N}{\left(\sum_k C_{ik}\right)\left(\sum_k C_{kj}\right)}, \]

when \(C_{ij}>0\).

Positive PMI keeps only positive association:

\[ \operatorname{PPMI}(v_i,v_j)=\max\{\operatorname{PMI}(v_i,v_j),0\}. \]

A positive PMI value means that the pair occurs more often than expected under independence. A negative PMI value means that the pair occurs less often than expected under independence.

PMI compares joint occurrence with independence.

\[ \operatorname{PMI}(w,c)=\log\frac{P(w,c)}{P(w)P(c)}. \]

If \(W\) and \(C\) were independent, then \(P(w,c)=P(w)P(c)\) and the PMI would be \(0\).

13.8 Matrix factorization viewpoint

Let \(M\in R^{d\times d}\) be a transformed co-occurrence matrix, such as a PPMI matrix. A low-rank embedding seeks matrices

\[ E\in R^{d\times m}, \qquad F\in R^{d\times m}, \]

such that

\[ M\approx EF^T. \]

Here \(E\) contains word embeddings and \(F\) contains context embeddings. The approximation says

\[ M_{ij}\approx e_i^Tf_j. \]

Thus the inner product between a word vector and a context vector approximates a transformed co-occurrence statistic.

A classical way to obtain such embeddings is the singular value decomposition. Suppose

\[ M=U\Sigma V^T. \]

The rank-\(m\) approximation is

\[ M_m=U_m\Sigma_m V_m^T. \]

A common embedding choice is

\[ E=U_m\Sigma_m^{1/2}, \qquad F=V_m\Sigma_m^{1/2}. \]

Then

\[ EF^T=U_m\Sigma_mV_m^T=M_m. \]

This directly connects word embeddings to the SVD theory from Chapter 7.

13.9 Prediction viewpoint

Matrix factorization is one route to embeddings. Neural prediction is another. In the prediction viewpoint, embeddings are learned because they help predict nearby words.

Let \(w\) be a center word and \(c\) be a context word. The skip-gram model assigns

\[ P_\theta(c\mid w)= \frac{\exp(u_c^Tv_w)} {\sum_{c'\in V}\exp(u_{c'}^Tv_w)}, \]

where

  • \(v_w\in R^m\) is the input embedding of the center word;
  • \(u_c\in R^m\) is the output embedding of the context word;
  • \(\theta\) denotes all embedding parameters.

Given observed word-context pairs \(D=\{(w_\ell,c_\ell)\}_{\ell=1}^N\), the negative log-likelihood is

\[ L(\theta) =-\sum_{\ell=1}^N \log P_\theta(c_\ell\mid w_\ell). \]

Equivalently,

\[ L(\theta) =\sum_{\ell=1}^N \left[ - u_{c_\ell}^Tv_{w_\ell} +\log\sum_{c'\in V}\exp(u_{c'}^Tv_{w_\ell}) \right]. \]

This is a multiclass cross-entropy objective.

13.10 Gradient of the skip-gram softmax loss

For one observed pair \((w,c)\), define scores

\[ z_k=u_k^Tv_w, \qquad k=1,\ldots,d, \]

and probabilities

\[ p_k=\frac{\exp(z_k)}{\sum_{r=1}^d\exp(z_r)}. \]

The single-pair loss is

\[ \ell(w,c)=-\log p_c. \]

The gradient with respect to a score is

\[ \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial z_k}=p_k-1_{\{k=c\}}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial v_w} =\sum_{k=1}^d (p_k-1_{\{k=c\}})u_k, \]

and

\[ \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial u_k} =(p_k-1_{\{k=c\}})v_w. \]

This formula shows the learning mechanism:

  • the true context vector \(u_c\) is pulled closer to \(v_w\);
  • all other context vectors are pushed away in proportion to their predicted probabilities;
  • the update is global because the softmax denominator sums over the whole vocabulary.

13.11 Negative sampling

The full softmax is expensive when \(d\) is large. Negative sampling replaces the multiclass softmax problem by several binary classification problems.

For an observed positive pair \((w,c)\) and negative samples \(n_1,\ldots,n_K\), the negative-sampling objective for one pair is often written as

\[ \ell_{\text{NS}}(w,c) = -\log\sigma(u_c^Tv_w) -\sum_{r=1}^K\log\sigma(-u_{n_r}^Tv_w), \]

where

\[ \sigma(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}. \]

The positive pair is encouraged to have a large inner product, while sampled negative pairs are encouraged to have small inner products.

Negative sampling changes the training objective. It is not merely a computational trick for the same loss. It learns vectors by asking whether a word-context pair came from the corpus or from a noise distribution.

13.12 GloVe as weighted least squares

GloVe is another influential embedding method. It begins with a co-occurrence matrix \(C\) and learns word vectors \(v_i\), context vectors \(u_j\), and biases \(a_i,b_j\) by minimizing

\[ J= \sum_{i=1}^d\sum_{j=1}^d f(C_{ij}) \left(v_i^Tu_j+a_i+b_j-\log C_{ij}\right)^2. \]

The weight function \(f(C_{ij})\) reduces the influence of rare and extremely frequent pairs. The model tries to make inner products approximate log co-occurrence counts, up to additive word and context biases.

The mathematical form is a weighted matrix approximation problem. Unlike ordinary SVD, it does not assign equal weight to every matrix entry.

13.13 Embedding geometry

Once words have vectors, we can analyze them geometrically. The most common similarity measure is cosine similarity:

\[ \operatorname{cos}(e_i,e_j) = \frac{e_i^Te_j}{\lVert e_i\rVert_2\lVert e_j\rVert_2}. \]

Nearest-neighbor search asks for

\[ \operatorname{NN}(w)=\arg\max_{v\in V,\ v\neq w}\operatorname{cos}(e(w),e(v)). \]

Analogy tasks use vector arithmetic. Given words \(a,b,c\), the classical query

\[ a:b\approx c:? \]

is approximated by

\[ \hat d = \arg\max_{w\in V\setminus\{a,b,c\}} \operatorname{cos}\left(e(w),e(b)-e(a)+e(c)\right). \]

A famous informal example is

\[ e(\text{king})-e(\text{man})+e(\text{woman})\approx e(\text{queen}). \]

This behavior is not guaranteed by the definition of an embedding. It emerges only when the training data and objective organize certain relationships approximately as linear directions.

13.14 What embeddings do not solve

Word embeddings are powerful, but they have limitations.

First, a single vector per word cannot represent different meanings of the same word. The word “bank” may refer to a financial institution or the side of a river. A static embedding averages across contexts.

Second, embeddings can encode social and historical biases present in the training corpus. Similarity in the embedding space reflects patterns in data, not moral or factual correctness.

Third, nearest neighbors can be unstable in high dimensions. Very frequent words, anisotropy, and hubness can distort neighborhood structure.

Fourth, word embeddings alone do not model word order. They represent tokens, not full contextual syntax.

These limitations motivate contextual embeddings and transformers in later chapters.

def make_pairs(tokenized_docs, window=2):
    pairs = []
    for doc in tokenized_docs:
        ids = [word_to_id[w] for w in doc]
        for t, center in enumerate(ids):
            left = max(0, t - window)
            right = min(len(ids), t + window + 1)
            for s in range(left, right):
                if s != t:
                    pairs.append((center, ids[s]))
    return pairs

14.5 PPMI matrix

def ppmi_matrix(C, eps=1e-12):
    total = C.sum()
    row_sum = C.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    col_sum = C.sum(axis=0, keepdims=True)
    expected = row_sum @ col_sum / max(total, eps)
    with np.errstate(divide="ignore", invalid="ignore"):
        pmi = np.log((C * total + eps) / (row_sum @ col_sum + eps))
    pmi[C == 0] = 0.0
    return np.maximum(pmi, 0.0)

def svd_embeddings(M, m=2):
    U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(M, full_matrices=False)
    E = U[:, :m] * np.sqrt(s[:m])

14.7 Nearest neighbors

def row_normalize(X, eps=1e-12):
    norms = np.linalg.norm(X, axis=1, keepdims=True)
    return X / np.maximum(norms, eps)

def nearest_neighbors(word, E, k=5):
    idx = word_to_id[word]
    En = row_normalize(E)
    scores = En @ En[idx]
    order = np.argsort(-scores)
    rows = []
    for j in order:
        if j != idx:
            rows.append((id_to_word[j], float(scores[j])))
        if len(rows) == k:
            break
    return pd.DataFrame(rows, columns=["word", "cosine_similarity"])

14.9 A tiny skip-gram softmax calculation

rng = np.random.default_rng(7243)

def softmax(z):
    z = z - np.max(z)
    ez = np.exp(z)
    return ez / ez.sum()

def skipgram_probs(center_word):
    i = word_to_id[center_word]

    probs = softmax(scores)

14.10 One negative-sampling update

def sigmoid(x):
    return 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-x))

def negative_sampling_step(V_in, U_out, center, positive, negatives, lr=0.05):
    i = word_to_id[center]
    j = word_to_id[positive]
    neg_ids = [word_to_id[w] for w in negatives]

    v = V_in[i].copy()

    # Positive pair gradient for -log sigmoid(u_j^T v)
    score_pos = U_out[j] @ v
    grad_v = (sigmoid(score_pos) - 1.0) * U_out[j]
    grad_u_pos = (sigmoid(score_pos) - 1.0) * v

    # Negative pair gradients for -log sigmoid(-u_n^T v)
    grad_u_negs = []
    for n in neg_ids:
        score_neg = U_out[n] @ v
        grad_v += sigmoid(score_neg) * U_out[n]
        grad_u_negs.append((n, sigmoid(score_neg) * v))

    V_in[i] -= lr * grad_v
    U_out[j] -= lr * grad_u_pos
    for n, grad_u in grad_u_negs:
        U_out[n] -= lr * grad_u

    return V_in, U_out

16 Worked example: PPMI by hand

Suppose a tiny corpus produces the following co-occurrence counts:

\[ C= \begin{bmatrix} 10 & 2\\ 1 & 7 \end{bmatrix}. \]

The total count is

\[ N=10+2+1+7=20. \]

For entry \((1,1)\),

\[ P(W=1,C=1)=\frac{10}{20}=0.5, \]

while

\[ P(W=1)=\frac{12}{20}=0.6, \qquad P(C=1)=\frac{11}{20}=0.55. \]

Thus

\[ \operatorname{PMI}(1,1) =\log\frac{0.5}{0.6\cdot 0.55} =\log(1.515\ldots)>0. \]

The pair \((1,1)\) occurs more often than expected under independence.

For entry \((1,2)\),

\[ P(W=1,C=2)=\frac{2}{20}=0.1, \]

while

\[ P(W=1)P(C=2)=\frac{12}{20}\cdot\frac{9}{20}=0.27. \]

Therefore

\[ \operatorname{PMI}(1,2)=\log\frac{0.1}{0.27}<0, \]

and the PPMI value is set to zero.

## From symbols to vectors

A token is a discrete object.  If the vocabulary is

$$
V=\{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_d\},
$$

then a token $v_j$ can be represented by the one-hot vector

$$
\delta_j=(0,\ldots,0,1,0,\ldots,0)^T\in R^d,
$$

where the $1$ occurs in position $j$.  This representation is exact but not semantic.  For $i\neq j$,

$$
\delta_i^T\delta_j=0.
$$

Thus every pair of distinct words is equally dissimilar.  The words "cat" and "dog" are no closer than "cat" and "gradient".

A word embedding replaces the one-hot representation with a dense vector:

$$
e:V\longrightarrow R^m,
\qquad
v_j\longmapsto e_j.
$$

Equivalently, the embedding is stored in a matrix

$$
E=\begin{bmatrix}
--- & e_1^T & ---\\
--- & e_2^T & ---\\
& \vdots & \\
--- & e_d^T & ---
\end{bmatrix}
\in R^{d\times m}.
$$

The embedding of token $v_j$ is the $j$th row of $E$:

$$
e_j=E_{j:}\in R^m.
$$

If a token is encoded as a one-hot vector $\delta_j$, then the lookup operation can be written as matrix multiplication:

$$
\delta_j^T E=e_j^T.
$$

::: {.math-card}
**Embedding lookup.**  A word embedding matrix $E\in R^{d\times m}$ converts a one-hot token vector into a dense vector:

$$
\delta_j^TE=e_j^T.
$$

This is why an embedding layer in a neural network is mathematically a trainable matrix lookup.
:::

## Why dense embeddings are useful

One-hot vectors live in a high-dimensional coordinate system where each coordinate is an arbitrary vocabulary index.  Dense embeddings live in a lower-dimensional representation space in which geometry is learned from data.

The central hope is that semantic or syntactic relationships become geometric relationships:

- similar words have small distance or large cosine similarity;
- related categories form clusters;
- some relationships correspond approximately to directions;
- analogy tasks can be expressed using vector arithmetic;
- downstream models can use continuous features rather than sparse symbolic indicators.

This does not mean that embeddings contain "true meaning."  It means that the training objective forces words with similar statistical behavior to receive related vectors.

## The distributional hypothesis

The guiding principle behind many word embedding methods is the **distributional hypothesis**:

> Words that occur in similar contexts tend to have related meanings.

Mathematically, each word $w\in V$ can be described by a context distribution

$$
P(C=c\mid W=w),
$$

where $C$ is a random context word.  If two words $w$ and $w'$ have similar conditional context distributions, then they should have nearby representations.

One way to state this is:

$$
P(C=\cdot\mid W=w)\approx P(C=\cdot\mid W=w')
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
e(w)\approx e(w').
$$

This is a statistical assumption, not a theorem.  It is often useful, but it can fail when words have multiple meanings, when corpora are biased, or when context alone is not enough.

::: {.math-card}
**Distributional semantics.**  A word embedding is a finite-dimensional summary of a word's empirical context distribution.  The geometry of the embedding space reflects statistical regularities in the corpus.
:::

## Co-occurrence matrices

Let a corpus be represented as a token sequence

$$
w_1,w_2,\ldots,w_T,
\qquad w_t\in V.
$$

Choose a context window size $h\geq 1$.  For each position $t$, the context of $w_t$ is the set of nearby positions

$$
\{s:1\leq s\leq T,\ 0<|s-t|\leq h\}.
$$

The word-context co-occurrence matrix is

$$
C\in R_{\geq 0}^{d\times d},
$$

where

$$
C_{ij}=\#\{(t,s):w_t=v_i,\ w_s=v_j,\ 0<|s-t|\leq h\}.
$$

Rows correspond to center words, and columns correspond to context words.  The row

$$
C_{i:}
$$

is a count-based description of the contexts in which word $v_i$ appears.

The simplest possible word representation is to use the row vector $C_{i:}$ itself.  This is usually high-dimensional and sparse.  Embedding methods compress or transform this information into lower-dimensional vectors.

## Empirical probabilities

Let

$$
N=\sum_{i=1}^d\sum_{j=1}^d C_{ij}
$$

be the total number of observed word-context pairs.  The empirical joint distribution is

$$
\widehat P(W=v_i,C=v_j)=\frac{C_{ij}}{N}.
$$

The marginal distributions are

$$
\widehat P(W=v_i)=\frac{\sum_j C_{ij}}{N},
\qquad
\widehat P(C=v_j)=\frac{\sum_i C_{ij}}{N}.
$$

The empirical conditional distribution of contexts given a word is

$$
\widehat P(C=v_j\mid W=v_i)
=
\frac{C_{ij}}{\sum_{k=1}^d C_{ik}}.
$$

This conditional distribution is the probabilistic object that the embedding should summarize.

## PMI and PPMI

A raw co-occurrence count is not enough.  Common words such as "the" or "and" co-occur with many words simply because they are frequent.  Pointwise mutual information corrects for marginal frequency.

The empirical pointwise mutual information of a word-context pair is

$$
\operatorname{PMI}(v_i,v_j)
=
\log\frac{\widehat P(W=v_i,C=v_j)}
{\widehat P(W=v_i)\widehat P(C=v_j)}.
$$

Equivalently, using counts,

$$
\operatorname{PMI}(v_i,v_j)
=
\log\frac{C_{ij}N}{\left(\sum_k C_{ik}\right)\left(\sum_k C_{kj}\right)},
$$

when $C_{ij}>0$.

Positive PMI keeps only positive association:

$$
\operatorname{PPMI}(v_i,v_j)=\max\{\operatorname{PMI}(v_i,v_j),0\}.
$$

A positive PMI value means that the pair occurs more often than expected under independence.  A negative PMI value means that the pair occurs less often than expected under independence.

::: {.math-card}
**PMI compares joint occurrence with independence.**

$$
\operatorname{PMI}(w,c)=\log\frac{P(w,c)}{P(w)P(c)}.
$$

If $W$ and $C$ were independent, then $P(w,c)=P(w)P(c)$ and the PMI would be $0$.
:::

## Matrix factorization viewpoint

Let $M\in R^{d\times d}$ be a transformed co-occurrence matrix, such as a PPMI matrix.  A low-rank embedding seeks matrices

$$
E\in R^{d\times m},
\qquad
F\in R^{d\times m},
$$

such that

$$
M\approx EF^T.
$$

Here $E$ contains word embeddings and $F$ contains context embeddings.  The approximation says

$$
M_{ij}\approx e_i^Tf_j.
$$

Thus the inner product between a word vector and a context vector approximates a transformed co-occurrence statistic.

A classical way to obtain such embeddings is the singular value decomposition.  Suppose

$$
M=U\Sigma V^T.
$$

The rank-$m$ approximation is

$$
M_m=U_m\Sigma_m V_m^T.
$$

A common embedding choice is

$$
E=U_m\Sigma_m^{1/2},
\qquad
F=V_m\Sigma_m^{1/2}.
$$

Then

$$
EF^T=U_m\Sigma_mV_m^T=M_m.
$$

This directly connects word embeddings to the SVD theory from Chapter 7.

## Prediction viewpoint

Matrix factorization is one route to embeddings.  Neural prediction is another.  In the prediction viewpoint, embeddings are learned because they help predict nearby words.

Let $w$ be a center word and $c$ be a context word.  The skip-gram model assigns

$$
P_\theta(c\mid w)=
\frac{\exp(u_c^Tv_w)}
{\sum_{c'\in V}\exp(u_{c'}^Tv_w)},
$$

where

- $v_w\in R^m$ is the input embedding of the center word;
- $u_c\in R^m$ is the output embedding of the context word;
- $\theta$ denotes all embedding parameters.

Given observed word-context pairs $D=\{(w_\ell,c_\ell)\}_{\ell=1}^N$, the negative log-likelihood is

$$
L(\theta)
=-\sum_{\ell=1}^N \log P_\theta(c_\ell\mid w_\ell).
$$

Equivalently,

$$
L(\theta)
=\sum_{\ell=1}^N
\left[
- u_{c_\ell}^Tv_{w_\ell}
+\log\sum_{c'\in V}\exp(u_{c'}^Tv_{w_\ell})
\right].
$$

This is a multiclass cross-entropy objective.

## Gradient of the skip-gram softmax loss

For one observed pair $(w,c)$, define scores

$$
z_k=u_k^Tv_w,
\qquad k=1,\ldots,d,
$$

and probabilities

$$
p_k=\frac{\exp(z_k)}{\sum_{r=1}^d\exp(z_r)}.
$$

The single-pair loss is

$$
\ell(w,c)=-\log p_c.
$$

The gradient with respect to a score is

$$
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial z_k}=p_k-1_{\{k=c\}}.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial v_w}
=\sum_{k=1}^d (p_k-1_{\{k=c\}})u_k,
$$

and

$$
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial u_k}
=(p_k-1_{\{k=c\}})v_w.
$$

This formula shows the learning mechanism:

- the true context vector $u_c$ is pulled closer to $v_w$;
- all other context vectors are pushed away in proportion to their predicted probabilities;
- the update is global because the softmax denominator sums over the whole vocabulary.

## Negative sampling

The full softmax is expensive when $d$ is large.  Negative sampling replaces the multiclass softmax problem by several binary classification problems.

For an observed positive pair $(w,c)$ and negative samples $n_1,\ldots,n_K$, the negative-sampling objective for one pair is often written as

$$
\ell_{\text{NS}}(w,c)
= -\log\sigma(u_c^Tv_w)
-\sum_{r=1}^K\log\sigma(-u_{n_r}^Tv_w),
$$

where

$$
\sigma(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}.
$$

The positive pair is encouraged to have a large inner product, while sampled negative pairs are encouraged to have small inner products.

Negative sampling changes the training objective.  It is not merely a computational trick for the same loss.  It learns vectors by asking whether a word-context pair came from the corpus or from a noise distribution.

## GloVe as weighted least squares

GloVe is another influential embedding method.  It begins with a co-occurrence matrix $C$ and learns word vectors $v_i$, context vectors $u_j$, and biases $a_i,b_j$ by minimizing

$$
J=
\sum_{i=1}^d\sum_{j=1}^d
f(C_{ij})
\left(v_i^Tu_j+a_i+b_j-\log C_{ij}\right)^2.
$$

The weight function $f(C_{ij})$ reduces the influence of rare and extremely frequent pairs.  The model tries to make inner products approximate log co-occurrence counts, up to additive word and context biases.

The mathematical form is a weighted matrix approximation problem.  Unlike ordinary SVD, it does not assign equal weight to every matrix entry.

## Embedding geometry

Once words have vectors, we can analyze them geometrically.  The most common similarity measure is cosine similarity:

$$
\operatorname{cos}(e_i,e_j)
=
\frac{e_i^Te_j}{\lVert e_i\rVert_2\lVert e_j\rVert_2}.
$$

Nearest-neighbor search asks for

$$
\operatorname{NN}(w)=\arg\max_{v\in V,\ v\neq w}\operatorname{cos}(e(w),e(v)).
$$

Analogy tasks use vector arithmetic.  Given words $a,b,c$, the classical query

$$
a:b\approx c:?
$$

is approximated by

$$
\hat d
=
\arg\max_{w\in V\setminus\{a,b,c\}}
\operatorname{cos}\left(e(w),e(b)-e(a)+e(c)\right).
$$

A famous informal example is

$$
e(\text{king})-e(\text{man})+e(\text{woman})\approx e(\text{queen}).
$$

This behavior is not guaranteed by the definition of an embedding.  It emerges only when the training data and objective organize certain relationships approximately as linear directions.

## What embeddings do not solve

Word embeddings are powerful, but they have limitations.

First, a single vector per word cannot represent different meanings of the same word.  The word "bank" may refer to a financial institution or the side of a river.  A static embedding averages across contexts.

Second, embeddings can encode social and historical biases present in the training corpus.  Similarity in the embedding space reflects patterns in data, not moral or factual correctness.

Third, nearest neighbors can be unstable in high dimensions.  Very frequent words, anisotropy, and hubness can distort neighborhood structure.

Fourth, word embeddings alone do not model word order.  They represent tokens, not full contextual syntax.

These limitations motivate contextual embeddings and transformers in later chapters.

```{python}
def ppmi_matrix(C, eps=1e-12):
    total = C.sum()
    row_sum = C.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    col_sum = C.sum(axis=0, keepdims=True)
    expected = row_sum @ col_sum / max(total, eps)
    with np.errstate(divide="ignore", invalid="ignore"):
        pmi = np.log((C * total + eps) / (row_sum @ col_sum + eps))
```

## SVD embeddings from PPMI

We factor the PPMI matrix and keep the first $m$ singular directions.

```{python}
def svd_embeddings(M, m=2):
    U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(M, full_matrices=False)
    E = U[:, :m] * np.sqrt(s[:m])
    F = Vt[:m, :].T * np.sqrt(s[:m])
    return E, F, s
```

## Nearest neighbors

```{python}
def row_normalize(X, eps=1e-12):
    norms = np.linalg.norm(X, axis=1, keepdims=True)
    return X / np.maximum(norms, eps)

def nearest_neighbors(word, E, k=5):
    idx = word_to_id[word]
    En = row_normalize(E)
    scores = En @ En[idx]
    order = np.argsort(-scores)
    rows = []
    for j in order:
        if j != idx:
            rows.append((id_to_word[j], float(scores[j])))
        if len(rows) == k:
            break
    return pd.DataFrame(rows, columns=["word", "cosine_similarity"])

nearest_neighbors("language", E2, k=6)
```

Because the corpus is tiny, the neighbors are not stable.  That is part of the lesson: embeddings need enough data to estimate meaningful context distributions.

## A tiny skip-gram softmax calculation

The next code implements the skip-gram probability for one center word.

```{python}
rng = np.random.default_rng(7243)
d = len(vocab)
m = 4

V_in = rng.normal(0, 0.1, size=(d, m))
U_out = rng.normal(0, 0.1, size=(d, m))

def softmax(z):
    z = z - np.max(z)
    ez = np.exp(z)
    return ez / ez.sum()

def skipgram_probs(center_word):
    i = word_to_id[center_word]
    scores = U_out @ V_in[i]
    probs = softmax(scores)
    return pd.DataFrame({"word": vocab, "probability": probs}).sort_values(
        "probability", ascending=False
    )

skipgram_probs("language").head(8)
```

At initialization, the probabilities are almost uniform because the random inner products are small.  Training changes the vectors so observed context words receive higher probability.

## One negative-sampling update

```{python}
def sigmoid(x):
    return 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-x))

def negative_sampling_step(V_in, U_out, center, positive, negatives, lr=0.05):
    i = word_to_id[center]
    j = word_to_id[positive]
    neg_ids = [word_to_id[w] for w in negatives]

    v = V_in[i].copy()

    # Positive pair gradient for -log sigmoid(u_j^T v)
    score_pos = U_out[j] @ v
    grad_v = (sigmoid(score_pos) - 1.0) * U_out[j]
    grad_u_pos = (sigmoid(score_pos) - 1.0) * v

    # Negative pair gradients for -log sigmoid(-u_n^T v)
    grad_u_negs = []
    for n in neg_ids:
        score_neg = U_out[n] @ v
        grad_v += sigmoid(score_neg) * U_out[n]
        grad_u_negs.append((n, sigmoid(score_neg) * v))

    V_in[i] -= lr * grad_v
    U_out[j] -= lr * grad_u_pos
    for n, grad_u in grad_u_negs:
        U_out[n] -= lr * grad_u

    return V_in, U_out

center = "language"
positive = "models"
negatives = ["algebra", "matrices", "singular"]

before = U_out[word_to_id[positive]] @ V_in[word_to_id[center]]
V_in, U_out = negative_sampling_step(V_in, U_out, center, positive, negatives)
after = U_out[word_to_id[positive]] @ V_in[word_to_id[center]]

print("positive inner product before:", round(float(before), 4))
print("positive inner product after: ", round(float(after), 4))
```

The update tends to increase the inner product of the observed pair and decrease the inner products of sampled negative pairs.

# Worked example: PPMI by hand

Suppose a tiny corpus produces the following co-occurrence counts:

$$
C=
\begin{bmatrix}
10 & 2\\
1 & 7
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The total count is

$$
N=10+2+1+7=20.
$$

For entry $(1,1)$,

$$
P(W=1,C=1)=\frac{10}{20}=0.5,
$$

while

$$
P(W=1)=\frac{12}{20}=0.6,
\qquad
P(C=1)=\frac{11}{20}=0.55.
$$

Thus

$$
\operatorname{PMI}(1,1)
=\log\frac{0.5}{0.6\cdot 0.55}
=\log(1.515\ldots)>0.
$$

The pair $(1,1)$ occurs more often than expected under independence.

For entry $(1,2)$,

$$
P(W=1,C=2)=\frac{2}{20}=0.1,
$$

while

$$
P(W=1)P(C=2)=\frac{12}{20}\cdot\frac{9}{20}=0.27.
$$

Therefore

$$
\operatorname{PMI}(1,2)=\log\frac{0.1}{0.27}<0,
$$

and the PPMI value is set to zero.

# Summary

Word embeddings transform discrete tokens into continuous vectors.  Mathematically, an embedding is a map

$$
e:V\to R^m.
$$

Count-based methods begin with co-occurrence matrices and transform them using PMI, PPMI, and matrix factorization.  Prediction-based methods learn embeddings by optimizing objectives such as skip-gram softmax or negative sampling.  In both cases, the resulting vectors encode statistical regularities from the corpus.

The central message is:

> Word meaning is not inserted into the vectors by hand.  It is induced from patterns of co-occurrence, prediction, and optimization.

This chapter completes the bridge from classical vector-space models to modern neural NLP.  The next chapter studies the geometry of embedding spaces more systematically, including clustering, visualization, anisotropy, and bias.
