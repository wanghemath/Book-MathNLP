29  Topology and Geometry of Language Representations

Metric spaces, persistent homology, and representation diagnostics

Modern NLP represents words, sentences, documents, and prompts as high-dimensional vectors. Once language objects become vectors, a corpus becomes a point cloud, a similarity graph, or a finite metric space. Linear algebra explains directions, projections, and low-rank structure; probability explains uncertainty and likelihood; topology asks a complementary question: what qualitative shape is present in the representation space? This chapter develops the topology and geometry of language representations from a clean mathematical point of view, with emphasis on finite metric spaces, neighborhood graphs, simplicial complexes, persistent homology, and representation diagnostics.

29.1 24.1 Learning goals

After reading this chapter, students should be able to:

  • view a collection of language embeddings as a finite metric space;
  • construct neighborhood graphs and Vietoris–Rips complexes from pairwise distances;
  • compute and interpret Betti numbers in dimensions 0 and 1;
  • explain persistent homology as a multiscale summary of shape;
  • distinguish geometric summaries from topological summaries;
  • understand how clustering, loops, anisotropy, and intrinsic dimension appear in embedding spaces;
  • use persistent features carefully as diagnostics rather than as automatic semantic truths;
  • implement small topological summaries from scratch in Python;
  • use AI tools to critique representation geometry and topology responsibly.
NoteGuiding question

What is the shape of a language representation space?

A linear-algebra answer might examine singular values, principal components, and distances. A statistical answer might estimate class separation or uncertainty. A topological answer asks whether the data form connected components, branching structures, loops, voids, or other multiscale patterns that persist across distance thresholds.

29.2 24.2 From embeddings to point clouds

Let \(T\) be a collection of language objects. Depending on the problem, the objects may be:

  • words in a vocabulary;
  • tokens in one sentence;
  • sentence embeddings from a corpus;
  • document embeddings;
  • contextual token embeddings from one layer of a Transformer;
  • prompts, questions, answers, or retrieved chunks.

An embedding model is a map

\[ f_\theta:T\to R^d. \]

For objects \(t_1,\ldots,t_n\), define

\[ x_i=f_\theta(t_i)\in R^d, \qquad X=\{x_1,\ldots,x_n\}\subset R^d. \]

Thus a finite collection of language objects becomes a finite point cloud. The point cloud may be studied by Euclidean distances,

\[ d_E(x_i,x_j)=\|x_i-x_j\|_2, \]

cosine distances,

\[ d_C(x_i,x_j)=1-\frac{x_i^T x_j}{\|x_i\|_2\|x_j\|_2}, \]

or other task-dependent dissimilarities.

Finite metric-space viewpoint. For topological data analysis, the central object is often not the coordinate matrix \(X\in R^{n\times d}\) itself, but the pairwise distance matrix

\[ D=(d_{ij}), \qquad d_{ij}=d(x_i,x_j). \]

Many topological constructions use only \(D\), so they can be applied to Euclidean embeddings, cosine embeddings, edit-distance spaces, or learned similarity spaces.

29.3 24.3 Geometry versus topology

Geometry studies quantities such as lengths, angles, curvature, projections, and distances. Topology studies qualitative features that are stable under continuous deformation, such as connected components and holes.

For language embeddings, geometry asks questions such as:

  • Which sentence is nearest to a query?
  • Which direction separates two topics?
  • How much variance is explained by the first few principal components?
  • Are embeddings anisotropic?

Topology asks questions such as:

  • How many connected clusters appear at a given scale?
  • Do clusters merge gradually or suddenly?
  • Is there evidence of loop-like structure among semantic groups?
  • Does the same qualitative shape persist across several scales?

A simple example helps. Suppose sentence embeddings lie approximately on a circle because the sentences describe a cyclic process such as morning, afternoon, evening, night, and morning again. PCA may show a two-dimensional plane. Clustering may find local groups. Persistent homology can detect a one-dimensional loop.

29.4 24.4 Neighborhood graphs

A first topological construction is the \(\varepsilon\)-neighborhood graph. Given a finite metric space \((X,d)\) and a scale parameter \(\varepsilon\ge 0\), define a graph

\[ G_\varepsilon=(V,E_\varepsilon), \qquad V=\{1,\ldots,n\}, \]

where

\[ (i,j)\in E_\varepsilon \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad d(x_i,x_j)\le \varepsilon. \]

As \(\varepsilon\) increases, the graph changes:

  • for very small \(\varepsilon\), every point is isolated;
  • at moderate \(\varepsilon\), nearby points form local clusters;
  • at larger \(\varepsilon\), clusters merge;
  • for sufficiently large \(\varepsilon\), the graph becomes connected.

The number of connected components is the zeroth Betti number:

\[ \beta_0(G_\varepsilon)=\text{number of connected components of }G_\varepsilon. \]

For a graph, the number of independent cycles is the graph cycle rank:

\[ \beta_1^{\mathrm{graph}}(G_\varepsilon)=|E_\varepsilon|-|V|+\beta_0(G_\varepsilon). \]

This graph quantity counts cycles before considering whether triangular cycles should be filled in. Persistent homology refines this idea by using simplicial complexes.

29.5 24.5 Simplicial complexes

A simplicial complex is a collection of finite subsets closed under taking subsets. Its elements are called simplices.

  • A 0-simplex is a vertex.
  • A 1-simplex is an edge.
  • A 2-simplex is a filled triangle.
  • A 3-simplex is a filled tetrahedron.

If \(\sigma\) is a simplex and \(\tau\subseteq \sigma\), then \(\tau\) must also be a simplex. This closure condition is important: if a filled triangle is present, then its three edges and three vertices must be present.

For a finite metric space, the most common construction is the Vietoris–Rips complex.

Vietoris–Rips complex. Given \((X,d)\) and \(\varepsilon\ge 0\), the Vietoris–Rips complex \(\operatorname{VR}_\varepsilon(X)\) contains a simplex \(\{i_0,\ldots,i_k\}\) whenever all pairwise distances are at most \(\varepsilon\):

\[ d(x_{i_a},x_{i_b})\le \varepsilon \quad \text{for all }a,b. \]

Equivalently, \(\operatorname{VR}_\varepsilon(X)\) is the clique complex of the \(\varepsilon\)-neighborhood graph.

This construction is useful for language embeddings because it converts similarity information into a multiscale combinatorial object.

29.6 24.6 Betti numbers

Betti numbers count independent topological features. Informally:

\[ \beta_0 = \text{number of connected components}, \]

\[ \beta_1 = \text{number of independent one-dimensional loops}, \]

\[ \beta_2 = \text{number of independent two-dimensional voids}. \]

For a graph without filled triangles, the formula

\[ \beta_1=|E|-|V|+\beta_0 \]

counts independent graph cycles. For a simplicial complex, some graph cycles become boundaries of filled triangles and therefore should not count as holes.

For a 2-dimensional simplicial complex with vertices, edges, and triangles, one can compute

\[ \beta_1 = \dim \ker \partial_1 - \operatorname{rank}\partial_2, \]

where

\[ \partial_1:C_1\to C_0 \]

maps oriented edges to differences of endpoints, and

\[ \partial_2:C_2\to C_1 \]

maps oriented triangles to signed sums of boundary edges.

Since

\[ \dim\ker \partial_1 = |E| - \operatorname{rank}\partial_1, \]

and for a graph

\[ \operatorname{rank}\partial_1 = |V|-\beta_0, \]

we get

\[ \beta_1 = |E|-|V|+\beta_0-\operatorname{rank}\partial_2. \]

This formula shows why filled triangles matter: the rank of \(\partial_2\) subtracts cycles that have become triangle boundaries.

29.7 24.7 Filtrations and persistent homology

A single value of \(\varepsilon\) is arbitrary. Persistent homology studies all scales at once. Since

\[ \varepsilon_1\le \varepsilon_2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \operatorname{VR}_{\varepsilon_1}(X)\subseteq \operatorname{VR}_{\varepsilon_2}(X), \]

we obtain a nested sequence

\[ \operatorname{VR}_{\varepsilon_1}(X) \subseteq \operatorname{VR}_{\varepsilon_2}(X) \subseteq \cdots \subseteq \operatorname{VR}_{\varepsilon_m}(X). \]

This is called a filtration.

As \(\varepsilon\) increases, features appear and disappear. A connected component is born when a point appears and dies when it merges with an older component. A loop is born when edges close a cycle and dies when the loop becomes filled by triangles.

A persistent homology summary records intervals

\[ [b_i,d_i), \]

where \(b_i\) is the birth scale and \(d_i\) is the death scale of a feature. The persistence length is

\[ d_i-b_i. \]

Longer intervals are usually interpreted as more stable multiscale features. Short intervals are often interpreted as local noise, although this interpretation depends on the problem.

WarningInterpretation warning

A persistent loop in an embedding space is not automatically a linguistic fact. It may come from semantic structure, sampling design, normalization, model architecture, dimensionality reduction, or numerical artifacts. Topology gives diagnostics; interpretation still requires domain reasoning.

29.8 24.8 Why topology can help NLP

Topological summaries are useful because they are less tied to a particular coordinate system than raw embedding coordinates. If two embedding spaces are transformed by rotations or small metric perturbations, their large-scale topological summaries may remain similar.

In NLP, topology can help analyze:

  • whether sentence embeddings form topic clusters;
  • whether generated texts cover the same representation shape as human texts;
  • whether contextual embeddings collapse into a narrow cone;
  • whether different layers of a Transformer gradually change representation geometry;
  • whether retrieval chunks form disconnected semantic islands;
  • whether class labels align with connected components or cut across them.

This is especially useful for mathematical NLP because it gives students a way to ask structural questions beyond accuracy.

29.9 24.9 Local dimension and anisotropy

Topology should be combined with geometry. Two important geometric diagnostics are local intrinsic dimension and anisotropy.

Let \(x_i\) be a point and let \(N_k(i)\) be its \(k\) nearest neighbors. Let

\[ Y_i = \{x_j-x_i:j\in N_k(i)\}. \]

The local covariance matrix is

\[ C_i = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{j\in N_k(i)} (x_j-x_i)(x_j-x_i)^T. \]

If the eigenvalues of \(C_i\) are

\[ \lambda_1\ge \lambda_2\ge\cdots\ge\lambda_d\ge 0, \]

then the number of large eigenvalues gives a local dimension heuristic. For example, a curve has approximately one large local eigenvalue, a surface has approximately two, and a full-dimensional cloud has many.

Anisotropy means that vectors concentrate in a narrow cone or a few dominant directions. One diagnostic is the spectrum of the centered covariance matrix

\[ C=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i-\bar x)(x_i-\bar x)^T. \]

A very steep spectrum indicates that the cloud is dominated by a few directions. This can affect nearest-neighbor retrieval and apparent topological structure.

29.10 24.10 Persistent homology and representation layers

For a Transformer, each layer produces a representation matrix

\[ H^{(\ell)}\in R^{T\times d}, \]

for tokens in a sentence, or a collection of sentence embeddings across a dataset. We can treat the rows of \(H^{(\ell)}\) as a point cloud and compare topological summaries across layers.

For each layer \(\ell\), one may compute a persistence summary

\[ \operatorname{PH}_k(H^{(\ell)}), \]

where \(k=0\) studies components and \(k=1\) studies loops. This gives a layerwise diagnostic:

\[ \ell \mapsto \operatorname{PH}_k(H^{(\ell)}). \]

Possible observations include:

  • early layers preserve local lexical neighborhoods;
  • middle layers separate syntactic or semantic roles;
  • later layers may collapse task-relevant examples into decision regions;
  • instruction-tuned models may reshape prompt and answer representations.

These are hypotheses to test, not universal laws.

29.11 24.11 Stability viewpoint

A major reason persistent homology is attractive is stability. Informally, if two point clouds are close as metric spaces, then their persistence diagrams are close. The precise form of stability depends on the construction and the metric used on diagrams.

Let \(D_X\) and \(D_Y\) be persistence diagrams for two point clouds. A common diagram distance is the bottleneck distance

\[ d_B(D_X,D_Y), \]

which matches points in one diagram to points in the other, allowing points to be matched to the diagonal. The diagonal represents zero-persistence features.

The stability principle says, informally,

\[ \text{small perturbation of distances} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{small perturbation of persistence diagrams}. \]

For NLP, this matters because embeddings are noisy and model-dependent. A diagnostic that changes wildly under tiny perturbations is not reliable.

29.12 24.12 Mapper and semantic organization

Another topological method is Mapper. Mapper starts with:

  1. a point cloud \(X\);
  2. a filter function \(g:X\to R^q\);
  3. a cover of the filter range;
  4. a clustering method inside each cover element.

The output is a graph whose nodes are local clusters and whose edges indicate overlap. In NLP, possible filter functions include:

  • first principal component score;
  • sentiment score;
  • document length;
  • perplexity under a language model;
  • retrieval score;
  • topic probability;
  • projection onto a semantic direction.

Mapper can reveal branching or circular organization, but it has tuning choices: cover size, overlap, clustering method, and distance metric.

29.13 24.13 A worked example: six sentence embeddings

Suppose six short sentence embeddings have pairwise distances arranged so that points \(1,2,3\) are close to each other and points \(4,5,6\) are close to each other, with a larger distance between the two groups.

At a small scale \(\varepsilon\), all six points are disconnected:

\[ \beta_0=6, \qquad \beta_1=0. \]

At a moderate scale, each group forms a triangle. If the triangles are filled in the Rips complex, each group is contractible:

\[ \beta_0=2, \qquad \beta_1=0. \]

At a larger scale, the two groups connect by at least one edge:

\[ \beta_0=1. \]

This is a topological version of clustering. The death times of connected components encode inter-cluster distances. In dimension 0, persistent homology is closely related to single-linkage hierarchical clustering.

29.16 24.16 Python example: neighborhood graph and components


The following code builds the \(\varepsilon\)-neighborhood graph and counts connected components.




def epsilon_edges(D, eps):
    n = D.shape[0]
    edges = []
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(i + 1, n):
            if D[i, j] <= eps:
                edges.append((i, j))
    return edges

class UnionFind:
    def __init__(self, n):
        self.parent = list(range(n))

    def find(self, a):
        while self.parent[a] != a:
            self.parent[a] = self.parent[self.parent[a]]
            a = self.parent[a]
        return a

    def union(self, a, b):
        ra, rb = self.find(a), self.find(b)
        if ra != rb:
            self.parent[rb] = ra

    def count_components(self):
        return len({self.find(i) for i in range(len(self.parent))})

def beta0_graph(D, eps):
    n = D.shape[0]
    uf = UnionFind(n)
    edges = epsilon_edges(D, eps)
    for i, j in edges:
        uf.union(i, j)
    return uf.count_components(), edges

for eps in [0.4, 0.8, 1.1, 1.5]:
    b0, edges = beta0_graph(D, eps)
    print(f"epsilon={eps:.1f}, edges={len(edges)}, beta0={b0}")

29.17 24.17 Python example: Betti numbers for a Rips complex up to dimension 2


Now we include filled triangles. The function below computes \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\) for the clique complex up to dimension 2.




def rips_edges_triangles(D, eps):
    n = D.shape[0]
    edges = epsilon_edges(D, eps)
    edge_index = {edge: k for k, edge in enumerate(edges)}
    triangles = []
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(i + 1, n):
            for k in range(j + 1, n):
                if ((i, j) in edge_index and
                    (i, k) in edge_index and
                    (j, k) in edge_index):
                    triangles.append((i, j, k))
    return edges, edge_index, triangles

def rank_matrix(A, tol=1e-10):
    if A.size == 0:
        return 0
    return np.linalg.matrix_rank(A, tol=tol)

def betti_0_1_rips(D, eps):
    n = D.shape[0]
    beta0, edges = beta0_graph(D, eps)
    edges, edge_index, triangles = rips_edges_triangles(D, eps)

    graph_cycle_rank = len(edges) - n + beta0

    # Boundary matrix partial_2: C_2 -> C_1.
    B2 = np.zeros((len(edges), len(triangles)))
    for col, (i, j, k) in enumerate(triangles):
        # oriented boundary: [j,k] - [i,k] + [i,j]
        B2[edge_index[(j, k)], col] = 1
        B2[edge_index[(i, k)], col] = -1
        B2[edge_index[(i, j)], col] = 1

    rank_b2 = rank_matrix(B2)
    beta1 = graph_cycle_rank - rank_b2
    return {
        "epsilon": eps,
        "vertices": n,
        "edges": len(edges),
        "triangles": len(triangles),
        "beta0": beta0,
        "graph_cycle_rank": graph_cycle_rank,
        "rank_boundary_2": rank_b2,
        "beta1": int(round(beta1)),
    }

summary = [betti_0_1_rips(D, eps) for eps in [0.4, 0.8, 1.1, 1.5]]
pd.DataFrame(summary)

29.19 24.19 Python example: local intrinsic dimension


We estimate local dimension by looking at the spectrum of local covariance matrices.




def local_dimension_scores(X, k=3):
    D = np.sqrt(((X[:, None, :] - X[None, :, :]) ** 2).sum(axis=2))
    scores = []
    for i in range(X.shape[0]):
        nn = np.argsort(D[i])[1:k+1]
        Y = X[nn] - X[i]
        C = (Y.T @ Y) / k
        eigvals = np.linalg.eigvalsh(C)[::-1]
        ratio = eigvals[0] / eigvals.sum() if eigvals.sum() > 0 else np.nan
        scores.append({
            "point": labels[i],
            "lambda1": eigvals[0],
            "lambda2": eigvals[1],
            "lambda1_fraction": ratio,
        })
    return pd.DataFrame(scores)

## 24.1 Learning goals

After reading this chapter, students should be able to:

- view a collection of language embeddings as a finite metric space;
- construct neighborhood graphs and Vietoris--Rips complexes from pairwise distances;
- compute and interpret Betti numbers in dimensions 0 and 1;
- explain persistent homology as a multiscale summary of shape;
- distinguish geometric summaries from topological summaries;
- understand how clustering, loops, anisotropy, and intrinsic dimension appear in embedding spaces;
- use persistent features carefully as diagnostics rather than as automatic semantic truths;
- implement small topological summaries from scratch in Python;
- use AI tools to critique representation geometry and topology responsibly.

::: {.callout-note}
## Guiding question
What is the shape of a language representation space?

A linear-algebra answer might examine singular values, principal components, and distances.  A statistical answer might estimate class separation or uncertainty.  A topological answer asks whether the data form connected components, branching structures, loops, voids, or other multiscale patterns that persist across distance thresholds.
:::

## 24.2 From embeddings to point clouds

Let $T$ be a collection of language objects.  Depending on the problem, the objects may be:

- words in a vocabulary;
- tokens in one sentence;
- sentence embeddings from a corpus;
- document embeddings;
- contextual token embeddings from one layer of a Transformer;
- prompts, questions, answers, or retrieved chunks.

An embedding model is a map

$$
f_\theta:T\to R^d.
$$

For objects $t_1,\ldots,t_n$, define

$$
x_i=f_\theta(t_i)\in R^d,
\qquad
X=\{x_1,\ldots,x_n\}\subset R^d.
$$

Thus a finite collection of language objects becomes a finite point cloud.  The point cloud may be studied by Euclidean distances,

$$
d_E(x_i,x_j)=\|x_i-x_j\|_2,
$$

cosine distances,

$$
d_C(x_i,x_j)=1-\frac{x_i^T x_j}{\|x_i\|_2\|x_j\|_2},
$$

or other task-dependent dissimilarities.

::: {.math-card}
**Finite metric-space viewpoint.**  For topological data analysis, the central object is often not the coordinate matrix $X\in R^{n\times d}$ itself, but the pairwise distance matrix

$$
D=(d_{ij}),
\qquad
d_{ij}=d(x_i,x_j).
$$

Many topological constructions use only $D$, so they can be applied to Euclidean embeddings, cosine embeddings, edit-distance spaces, or learned similarity spaces.
:::

## 24.3 Geometry versus topology

Geometry studies quantities such as lengths, angles, curvature, projections, and distances.  Topology studies qualitative features that are stable under continuous deformation, such as connected components and holes.

For language embeddings, geometry asks questions such as:

- Which sentence is nearest to a query?
- Which direction separates two topics?
- How much variance is explained by the first few principal components?
- Are embeddings anisotropic?

Topology asks questions such as:

- How many connected clusters appear at a given scale?
- Do clusters merge gradually or suddenly?
- Is there evidence of loop-like structure among semantic groups?
- Does the same qualitative shape persist across several scales?

A simple example helps.  Suppose sentence embeddings lie approximately on a circle because the sentences describe a cyclic process such as morning, afternoon, evening, night, and morning again.  PCA may show a two-dimensional plane.  Clustering may find local groups.  Persistent homology can detect a one-dimensional loop.

## 24.4 Neighborhood graphs

A first topological construction is the $\varepsilon$-neighborhood graph.  Given a finite metric space $(X,d)$ and a scale parameter $\varepsilon\ge 0$, define a graph

$$
G_\varepsilon=(V,E_\varepsilon),
\qquad
V=\{1,\ldots,n\},
$$

where

$$
(i,j)\in E_\varepsilon
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
d(x_i,x_j)\le \varepsilon.
$$

As $\varepsilon$ increases, the graph changes:

- for very small $\varepsilon$, every point is isolated;
- at moderate $\varepsilon$, nearby points form local clusters;
- at larger $\varepsilon$, clusters merge;
- for sufficiently large $\varepsilon$, the graph becomes connected.

The number of connected components is the zeroth Betti number:

$$
\beta_0(G_\varepsilon)=\text{number of connected components of }G_\varepsilon.
$$

For a graph, the number of independent cycles is the graph cycle rank:

$$
\beta_1^{\mathrm{graph}}(G_\varepsilon)=|E_\varepsilon|-|V|+\beta_0(G_\varepsilon).
$$

This graph quantity counts cycles before considering whether triangular cycles should be filled in.  Persistent homology refines this idea by using simplicial complexes.

## 24.5 Simplicial complexes

A simplicial complex is a collection of finite subsets closed under taking subsets.  Its elements are called simplices.

- A 0-simplex is a vertex.
- A 1-simplex is an edge.
- A 2-simplex is a filled triangle.
- A 3-simplex is a filled tetrahedron.

If $\sigma$ is a simplex and $\tau\subseteq \sigma$, then $\tau$ must also be a simplex.  This closure condition is important: if a filled triangle is present, then its three edges and three vertices must be present.

For a finite metric space, the most common construction is the Vietoris--Rips complex.

::: {.math-card}
**Vietoris--Rips complex.**  Given $(X,d)$ and $\varepsilon\ge 0$, the Vietoris--Rips complex $\operatorname{VR}_\varepsilon(X)$ contains a simplex $\{i_0,\ldots,i_k\}$ whenever all pairwise distances are at most $\varepsilon$:

$$
d(x_{i_a},x_{i_b})\le \varepsilon
\quad
\text{for all }a,b.
$$

Equivalently, $\operatorname{VR}_\varepsilon(X)$ is the clique complex of the $\varepsilon$-neighborhood graph.
:::

This construction is useful for language embeddings because it converts similarity information into a multiscale combinatorial object.

## 24.6 Betti numbers

Betti numbers count independent topological features.  Informally:

$$
\beta_0 = \text{number of connected components},
$$

$$
\beta_1 = \text{number of independent one-dimensional loops},
$$

$$
\beta_2 = \text{number of independent two-dimensional voids}.
$$

For a graph without filled triangles, the formula

$$
\beta_1=|E|-|V|+\beta_0
$$

counts independent graph cycles.  For a simplicial complex, some graph cycles become boundaries of filled triangles and therefore should not count as holes.

For a 2-dimensional simplicial complex with vertices, edges, and triangles, one can compute

$$
\beta_1 = \dim \ker \partial_1 - \operatorname{rank}\partial_2,
$$

where

$$
\partial_1:C_1\to C_0
$$

maps oriented edges to differences of endpoints, and

$$
\partial_2:C_2\to C_1
$$

maps oriented triangles to signed sums of boundary edges.

Since

$$
\dim\ker \partial_1 = |E| - \operatorname{rank}\partial_1,
$$

and for a graph

$$
\operatorname{rank}\partial_1 = |V|-\beta_0,
$$

we get

$$
\beta_1
=
|E|-|V|+\beta_0-\operatorname{rank}\partial_2.
$$

This formula shows why filled triangles matter: the rank of $\partial_2$ subtracts cycles that have become triangle boundaries.

## 24.7 Filtrations and persistent homology

A single value of $\varepsilon$ is arbitrary.  Persistent homology studies all scales at once.  Since

$$
\varepsilon_1\le \varepsilon_2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\operatorname{VR}_{\varepsilon_1}(X)\subseteq \operatorname{VR}_{\varepsilon_2}(X),
$$

we obtain a nested sequence

$$
\operatorname{VR}_{\varepsilon_1}(X)
\subseteq
\operatorname{VR}_{\varepsilon_2}(X)
\subseteq
\cdots
\subseteq
\operatorname{VR}_{\varepsilon_m}(X).
$$

This is called a filtration.

As $\varepsilon$ increases, features appear and disappear.  A connected component is born when a point appears and dies when it merges with an older component.  A loop is born when edges close a cycle and dies when the loop becomes filled by triangles.

A persistent homology summary records intervals

$$
[b_i,d_i),
$$

where $b_i$ is the birth scale and $d_i$ is the death scale of a feature.  The persistence length is

$$
d_i-b_i.
$$

Longer intervals are usually interpreted as more stable multiscale features.  Short intervals are often interpreted as local noise, although this interpretation depends on the problem.

::: {.callout-warning}
## Interpretation warning
A persistent loop in an embedding space is not automatically a linguistic fact.  It may come from semantic structure, sampling design, normalization, model architecture, dimensionality reduction, or numerical artifacts.  Topology gives diagnostics; interpretation still requires domain reasoning.
:::

## 24.8 Why topology can help NLP

Topological summaries are useful because they are less tied to a particular coordinate system than raw embedding coordinates.  If two embedding spaces are transformed by rotations or small metric perturbations, their large-scale topological summaries may remain similar.

In NLP, topology can help analyze:

- whether sentence embeddings form topic clusters;
- whether generated texts cover the same representation shape as human texts;
- whether contextual embeddings collapse into a narrow cone;
- whether different layers of a Transformer gradually change representation geometry;
- whether retrieval chunks form disconnected semantic islands;
- whether class labels align with connected components or cut across them.

This is especially useful for mathematical NLP because it gives students a way to ask structural questions beyond accuracy.

## 24.9 Local dimension and anisotropy

Topology should be combined with geometry.  Two important geometric diagnostics are local intrinsic dimension and anisotropy.

Let $x_i$ be a point and let $N_k(i)$ be its $k$ nearest neighbors.  Let

$$
Y_i = \{x_j-x_i:j\in N_k(i)\}.
$$

The local covariance matrix is

$$
C_i = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{j\in N_k(i)} (x_j-x_i)(x_j-x_i)^T.
$$

If the eigenvalues of $C_i$ are

$$
\lambda_1\ge \lambda_2\ge\cdots\ge\lambda_d\ge 0,
$$

then the number of large eigenvalues gives a local dimension heuristic.  For example, a curve has approximately one large local eigenvalue, a surface has approximately two, and a full-dimensional cloud has many.

Anisotropy means that vectors concentrate in a narrow cone or a few dominant directions.  One diagnostic is the spectrum of the centered covariance matrix

$$
C=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i-\bar x)(x_i-\bar x)^T.
$$

A very steep spectrum indicates that the cloud is dominated by a few directions.  This can affect nearest-neighbor retrieval and apparent topological structure.

## 24.10 Persistent homology and representation layers

For a Transformer, each layer produces a representation matrix

$$
H^{(\ell)}\in R^{T\times d},
$$

for tokens in a sentence, or a collection of sentence embeddings across a dataset.  We can treat the rows of $H^{(\ell)}$ as a point cloud and compare topological summaries across layers.

For each layer $\ell$, one may compute a persistence summary

$$
\operatorname{PH}_k(H^{(\ell)}),
$$

where $k=0$ studies components and $k=1$ studies loops.  This gives a layerwise diagnostic:

$$
\ell \mapsto \operatorname{PH}_k(H^{(\ell)}).
$$

Possible observations include:

- early layers preserve local lexical neighborhoods;
- middle layers separate syntactic or semantic roles;
- later layers may collapse task-relevant examples into decision regions;
- instruction-tuned models may reshape prompt and answer representations.

These are hypotheses to test, not universal laws.

## 24.11 Stability viewpoint

A major reason persistent homology is attractive is stability.  Informally, if two point clouds are close as metric spaces, then their persistence diagrams are close.  The precise form of stability depends on the construction and the metric used on diagrams.

Let $D_X$ and $D_Y$ be persistence diagrams for two point clouds.  A common diagram distance is the bottleneck distance

$$
d_B(D_X,D_Y),
$$

which matches points in one diagram to points in the other, allowing points to be matched to the diagonal.  The diagonal represents zero-persistence features.

The stability principle says, informally,

$$
\text{small perturbation of distances}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\text{small perturbation of persistence diagrams}.
$$

For NLP, this matters because embeddings are noisy and model-dependent.  A diagnostic that changes wildly under tiny perturbations is not reliable.

## 24.12 Mapper and semantic organization

Another topological method is Mapper.  Mapper starts with:

1. a point cloud $X$;
2. a filter function $g:X\to R^q$;
3. a cover of the filter range;
4. a clustering method inside each cover element.

The output is a graph whose nodes are local clusters and whose edges indicate overlap.  In NLP, possible filter functions include:

- first principal component score;
- sentiment score;
- document length;
- perplexity under a language model;
- retrieval score;
- topic probability;
- projection onto a semantic direction.

Mapper can reveal branching or circular organization, but it has tuning choices: cover size, overlap, clustering method, and distance metric.

## 24.13 A worked example: six sentence embeddings

Suppose six short sentence embeddings have pairwise distances arranged so that points $1,2,3$ are close to each other and points $4,5,6$ are close to each other, with a larger distance between the two groups.

At a small scale $\varepsilon$, all six points are disconnected:

$$
\beta_0=6,
\qquad
\beta_1=0.
$$

At a moderate scale, each group forms a triangle.  If the triangles are filled in the Rips complex, each group is contractible:

$$
\beta_0=2,
\qquad
\beta_1=0.
$$

At a larger scale, the two groups connect by at least one edge:

$$
\beta_0=1.
$$

This is a topological version of clustering.  The death times of connected components encode inter-cluster distances.  In dimension 0, persistent homology is closely related to single-linkage hierarchical clustering.

```{=html}
          for (let c = col; c < n; c++) A[r][c] -= factor * A[row][c];
        }
      }
      rank++;
      row++;
    }
    return rank;
  }

  function betaNumbers(points, eps) {
    const n = points.length;
    const et = edgesAndTriangles(points, eps);
    const c = components(n, et.edges);
    const graphCycles = et.edges.length - n + c;
    const B2 = Array.from({length: et.edges.length}, function() { return Array(et.triangles.length).fill(0); });
    et.triangles.forEach(function(tri, col) {
      const i = tri[0], j = tri[1], k = tri[2];

      B2[et.edgeIndex[i + "," + k]][col] = -1;
      B2[et.edgeIndex[i + "," + j]][col] = 1;
    });
    const r2 = matrixRank(B2, 1e-9);
    const beta1 = Math.max(0, graphCycles - r2);
    return {beta0: c, beta1: beta1, graphCycles: graphCycles, rankB2: r2, edges: et.edges, triangles: et.triangles};
  }
```

## 24.16 Python example: neighborhood graph and components

The following code builds the $\varepsilon$-neighborhood graph and counts connected components.

```{python}
def epsilon_edges(D, eps):
    n = D.shape[0]
    edges = []
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(i + 1, n):
            if D[i, j] <= eps:
                edges.append((i, j))
    return edges

class UnionFind:
    def __init__(self, n):
        self.parent = list(range(n))

    def find(self, a):
        while self.parent[a] != a:
            self.parent[a] = self.parent[self.parent[a]]
            a = self.parent[a]
        return a

    def union(self, a, b):
        ra, rb = self.find(a), self.find(b)
        if ra != rb:
            self.parent[rb] = ra

    def count_components(self):
        return len({self.find(i) for i in range(len(self.parent))})

def beta0_graph(D, eps):
    n = D.shape[0]
    uf = UnionFind(n)
    edges = epsilon_edges(D, eps)
    for i, j in edges:
        uf.union(i, j)
    return uf.count_components(), edges

for eps in [0.4, 0.8, 1.1, 1.5]:
    b0, edges = beta0_graph(D, eps)
    print(f"epsilon={eps:.1f}, edges={len(edges)}, beta0={b0}")
```

## 24.17 Python example: Betti numbers for a Rips complex up to dimension 2

Now we include filled triangles.  The function below computes $\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$ for the clique complex up to dimension 2.

```{python}
def rips_edges_triangles(D, eps):
    n = D.shape[0]
    edges = epsilon_edges(D, eps)
    edge_index = {edge: k for k, edge in enumerate(edges)}
    triangles = []
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(i + 1, n):
            for k in range(j + 1, n):
                if ((i, j) in edge_index and
                    (i, k) in edge_index and
                    (j, k) in edge_index):
                    triangles.append((i, j, k))
    return edges, edge_index, triangles

def rank_matrix(A, tol=1e-10):
    if A.size == 0:
        return 0
    return np.linalg.matrix_rank(A, tol=tol)

def betti_0_1_rips(D, eps):
    n = D.shape[0]
    beta0, edges = beta0_graph(D, eps)
    edges, edge_index, triangles = rips_edges_triangles(D, eps)

    graph_cycle_rank = len(edges) - n + beta0

    # Boundary matrix partial_2: C_2 -> C_1.
    B2 = np.zeros((len(edges), len(triangles)))
    for col, (i, j, k) in enumerate(triangles):
        # oriented boundary: [j,k] - [i,k] + [i,j]
        B2[edge_index[(j, k)], col] = 1
        B2[edge_index[(i, k)], col] = -1
        B2[edge_index[(i, j)], col] = 1

    rank_b2 = rank_matrix(B2)
    beta1 = graph_cycle_rank - rank_b2
    return {
        "epsilon": eps,
        "vertices": n,
        "edges": len(edges),
        "triangles": len(triangles),
        "beta0": beta0,
        "graph_cycle_rank": graph_cycle_rank,
        "rank_boundary_2": rank_b2,
        "beta1": int(round(beta1)),
    }

summary = [betti_0_1_rips(D, eps) for eps in [0.4, 0.8, 1.1, 1.5]]
pd.DataFrame(summary)
```

The graph cycle rank can be positive even when $\beta_1=0$, because filled triangles may kill cycles.

## 24.19 Python example: local intrinsic dimension

We estimate local dimension by looking at the spectrum of local covariance matrices.

```{python}
def local_dimension_scores(X, k=3):
    D = np.sqrt(((X[:, None, :] - X[None, :, :]) ** 2).sum(axis=2))
    scores = []
    for i in range(X.shape[0]):
        nn = np.argsort(D[i])[1:k+1]
        Y = X[nn] - X[i]
        C = (Y.T @ Y) / k
        eigvals = np.linalg.eigvalsh(C)[::-1]
        ratio = eigvals[0] / eigvals.sum() if eigvals.sum() > 0 else np.nan
        scores.append({
            "point": labels[i],
            "lambda1": eigvals[0],
            "lambda2": eigvals[1],
            "lambda1_fraction": ratio,
        })
    return pd.DataFrame(scores)

local_dimension_scores(X, k=3)
```

A point with most local variance in one direction has a curve-like neighborhood.  A point with substantial variance in two directions has a more surface-like neighborhood.

## 24.23 Common mistakes

1. **Confusing a graph cycle with a topological loop.**  A triangle in a graph is a cycle, but in a Rips complex it is usually filled, so it does not contribute to $\beta_1$.

2. **Using dimensionality reduction as evidence of topology.**  A loop in a two-dimensional visualization may be introduced or destroyed by projection.  Compute topology from the original metric when possible.

3. **Ignoring normalization.**  Euclidean distance and cosine distance can give very different filtrations.

4. **Overinterpreting short bars.**  Low-persistence features may be noise or sampling artifacts.

5. **Forgetting sample-size effects.**  Persistent homology depends on how densely the underlying shape is sampled.

6. **Treating persistent homology as a black box.**  Students should understand vertices, edges, triangles, boundary maps, and Betti numbers before using a library.

## 24.27 Summary

This chapter introduced a topological viewpoint on NLP representation spaces.  The central idea is simple: embeddings turn language objects into points, distances turn point clouds into finite metric spaces, and filtrations turn metric spaces into multiscale topological summaries.  Betti numbers and persistent homology provide tools for studying connected components, loops, and other qualitative structures.  These tools are most valuable when used together with linear algebra, probability, and domain knowledge.  In mathematical NLP, topology is not a replacement for modeling or evaluation; it is a structural lens for asking better questions about representation spaces.
