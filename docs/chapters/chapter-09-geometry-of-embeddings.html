14  Geometry of Embedding Spaces

Similarity, dimension reduction, clustering, and high-dimensional caution

Word embeddings turn tokens into points in a vector space. Once language objects become vectors, NLP inherits the language of geometry: distances, angles, projections, subspaces, neighborhoods, clusters, and directions. This chapter develops the geometry of embedding spaces carefully, with emphasis on the mathematical assumptions behind similarity search and visualization.

14.1 Learning goals

After studying this chapter, you should be able to:

  • Treat a collection of word or sentence embeddings as a point cloud \(X \in R^{n \times m}\).
  • Compare inner-product similarity, cosine similarity, and Euclidean distance.
  • Explain why nearest-neighbor lists depend on normalization and metric choice.
  • Derive PCA from the singular value decomposition of a centered embedding matrix.
  • Interpret low-dimensional embedding visualizations responsibly.
  • State the \(k\)-means objective and understand clustering as an optimization problem.
  • Describe anisotropy, hubness, and distance concentration in high-dimensional spaces.
  • Use linear directions to study interpretable attributes and possible bias in embeddings.

14.2 9.1 Why geometry matters in NLP

In earlier chapters, a word embedding was introduced as a map

\[ E:V \longrightarrow R^m, \]

where \(V\) is a vocabulary and \(m\) is the embedding dimension. For a token \(w \in V\), the vector \(E(w)\) is often written as \(e_w \in R^m\).

If \(V = \{w_1,\dots,w_n\}\), the embedding model produces a matrix

\[ X= \begin{bmatrix} - & e_{w_1}^T & -\\ - & e_{w_2}^T & -\\ & \vdots & \\ - & e_{w_n}^T & - \end{bmatrix} \in R^{n\times m}. \]

The rows of \(X\) form a finite point cloud in \(R^m\). Many NLP operations can then be written geometrically.

NLP question Geometric formulation
Which words are similar to king? Find nearest neighbors of \(e_{king}\).
Which documents match a query? Find vectors with large inner product or cosine similarity to the query vector.
Are topics separated? Look for clusters or low-dimensional structure.
What direction represents an attribute? Estimate a vector direction \(b\) and project embeddings onto \(b\).
Can we visualize embeddings? Project \(R^m\) to \(R^2\) using PCA, SVD, or another map.

The important point is that geometry is not merely a visualization tool. It is part of the model. A statement such as “these words are close” is incomplete until we specify the representation, the metric, and any preprocessing such as centering or normalization.

Core viewpoint. An embedding space is a finite metric or inner-product structure induced by a representation map. The geometry we observe is not intrinsic to language alone; it depends on the construction of \(E\), the training data, the loss function, and the similarity measure.

14.3 9.2 Similarity measures

Let \(x,y \in R^m\) be two embedding vectors.

14.3.1 Inner product

The inner product is

\[ \langle x,y\rangle = x^T y = \sum_{j=1}^m x_j y_j. \]

Large inner products favor vectors that point in similar directions and also have large norms. Therefore inner-product retrieval is sensitive to vector length.

14.3.2 Euclidean distance

The Euclidean distance is

\[ d_2(x,y)=\|x-y\|_2= \left(\sum_{j=1}^m (x_j-y_j)^2\right)^{1/2}. \]

Small Euclidean distance means that the vectors are close as points in \(R^m\). It uses both direction and length.

14.3.3 Cosine similarity

The cosine similarity is

\[ \cos(x,y)=\frac{x^T y}{\|x\|_2\|y\|_2}, \qquad x\ne 0,\ y\ne 0. \]

Cosine similarity depends only on the angle between \(x\) and \(y\). It is invariant under positive rescaling:

\[ \cos(cx,dy)=\cos(x,y), \qquad c>0,\ d>0. \]

This is useful when vector length is less meaningful than direction. In many word and document embedding applications, cosine similarity is preferred for nearest-neighbor search.

14.3.4 Relationship after normalization

Define the normalized vectors

\[ \hat{x}=\frac{x}{\|x\|_2}, \qquad \hat{y}=\frac{y}{\|y\|_2}. \]

Then

\[ \|\hat{x}-\hat{y}\|_2^2 = \|\hat{x}\|_2^2+ \|\hat{y}\|_2^2-2\hat{x}^T\hat{y} =2-2\cos(x,y). \]

Thus, for normalized embeddings, minimizing Euclidean distance is equivalent to maximizing cosine similarity.

Metric choice matters. The nearest neighbors of a word can change when we switch from inner product to cosine similarity or Euclidean distance. This is not a numerical accident. It reflects different mathematical assumptions about whether vector length carries semantic information.

14.4 9.3 Nearest neighbors as an optimization problem

Given embeddings \(e_1,\dots,e_n \in R^m\) and a query vector \(q \in R^m\), nearest-neighbor search solves

\[ \operatorname{NN}_s(q) = \arg\max_{1\le i\le n} s(q,e_i), \]

where \(s\) is a similarity function such as inner product or cosine similarity. Equivalently, using a distance \(d\),

\[ \operatorname{NN}_d(q) = \arg\min_{1\le i\le n} d(q,e_i). \]

For top-\(k\) retrieval, we return the \(k\) largest scores or the \(k\) smallest distances.

In matrix form, if \(X\in R^{n\times m}\) stores the candidate embeddings as rows, inner-product scores are

\[ s = Xq \in R^n. \]

For cosine similarity, define a row-normalized matrix \(\widehat X\) and normalized query \(\hat q\). Then

\[ s = \widehat X \hat q. \]

This simple matrix-vector multiplication is one reason embedding retrieval is computationally attractive.

14.7 9.6 Python example: nearest neighbors from scratch

The following example implements nearest-neighbor search without a machine learning library. This is useful because it makes the metric choice explicit.

import numpy as np

words = ["cat", "dog", "lion", "car", "truck", "bus", "king", "queen"]
X = np.array([
    [1.00,  0.90],
    [1.10,  0.80],
    [1.45,  1.10],
    [-1.00, -0.80],
    [-1.25, -0.90],
    [-0.90, -1.15],
    [0.20,  1.85],
    [0.05,  1.70],
])

def cosine_scores(X, q):
    X_norm = X / np.linalg.norm(X, axis=1, keepdims=True)
    q_norm = q / np.linalg.norm(q)
    return X_norm @ q_norm

def euclidean_distances(X, q):
    return np.linalg.norm(X - q, axis=1)

def nearest_neighbors(query_word, metric="cosine", k=3):
    idx = words.index(query_word)
    q = X[idx]

    if metric == "cosine":
        values = cosine_scores(X, q)
        order = np.argsort(-values)
    elif metric == "inner":
        values = X @ q
        order = np.argsort(-values)
    elif metric == "euclidean":
        values = euclidean_distances(X, q)
        order = np.argsort(values)
    else:
        raise ValueError("metric must be cosine, inner, or euclidean")

    result = []
    for j in order:
        if j != idx:
            result.append((words[j], float(values[j])))
        if len(result) == k:
            break
    return result

print("cosine:", nearest_neighbors("cat", metric="cosine"))
print("inner product:", nearest_neighbors("cat", metric="inner"))
print("euclidean:", nearest_neighbors("cat", metric="euclidean"))

14.8 9.7 Centering, normalization, and anisotropy

Embedding spaces often have strong global directions. For example, many vectors may share a large common component. This can make most pairwise cosine similarities positive and reduce the contrast between meaningful neighbors and unrelated words.

Let the rows of \(X\in R^{n\times m}\) be embeddings. The sample mean vector is

\[ \bar{x}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i. \]

The centered embedding matrix is

\[ X_c = X - {\bf 1}\bar{x}^T, \]

where \({\bf 1}\in R^n\) is the vector of all ones. Centering removes the global mean direction.

Row normalization gives

\[ \tilde{x}_i=\frac{x_i}{\|x_i\|_2}. \]

Depending on the task, one may use raw vectors, centered vectors, normalized vectors, or centered-and-normalized vectors. These choices change the geometry.

Anisotropy. A point cloud is anisotropic when its variance is much larger in some directions than in others. In embedding spaces, anisotropy can cause a few dominant directions to affect many similarity computations.

A simple diagnostic is to inspect the singular values of the centered matrix. If the first few singular values dominate, then much of the variation lies in a low-dimensional subspace.

14.9 9.8 PCA for embedding spaces

PCA is a standard method for projecting high-dimensional embeddings to a lower-dimensional space. It should be understood as a variance-preserving linear projection, not as a perfect map of semantic meaning.

Let \(X\in R^{n\times m}\) be the embedding matrix, and let

\[ X_c = X - {\bf 1}\bar{x}^T \]

be the centered matrix. The sample covariance matrix is

\[ S=\frac{1}{n-1}X_c^T X_c \in R^{m\times m}. \]

PCA finds orthonormal directions \(v_1,\dots,v_r\) solving

\[ v_1 = \arg\max_{\|v\|_2=1} v^T S v, \]

and, for \(j>1\),

\[ v_j = \arg\max_{\|v\|_2=1,\ v\perp v_1,\dots,v_{j-1}} v^T S v. \]

The solutions are eigenvectors of \(S\):

\[ S v_j = \lambda_j v_j, \qquad \lambda_1\ge \lambda_2\ge \cdots \ge 0. \]

Equivalently, use the singular value decomposition

\[ X_c = U\Sigma V^T. \]

The columns of \(V\) are the principal directions, and the low-dimensional PCA coordinates are

\[ Z_k = X_c V_k \in R^{n\times k}, \]

where \(V_k=[v_1,\dots,v_k]\).

The fraction of variance explained by the first \(k\) components is

\[ \frac{\lambda_1+\cdots+\lambda_k}{\lambda_1+\cdots+\lambda_m} = \frac{\sigma_1^2+\cdots+\sigma_k^2}{\sigma_1^2+\cdots+\sigma_r^2}. \]

14.9.1 PCA is useful but limited

PCA preserves large-variance linear structure. It does not necessarily preserve all nearest-neighbor relationships, clusters, or nonlinear geometry. A two-dimensional PCA plot is a useful diagnostic, but it should not be treated as the embedding space itself.

14.11 9.10 Clustering embeddings

Clustering asks whether embedding vectors can be partitioned into groups with similar geometry. A common model is \(k\)-means clustering.

Given embeddings \(x_1,\dots,x_n\in R^m\), the \(k\)-means objective is

\[ \min_{C_1,\dots,C_k,\ \mu_1,\dots,\mu_k} \sum_{r=1}^k \sum_{i\in C_r} \|x_i-\mu_r\|_2^2, \]

where \(C_1,\dots,C_k\) partition \(\{1, \dots,n\}\) and \(\mu_r\in R^m\) is the centroid of cluster \(r\).

For fixed clusters, the optimal centroid is the sample mean:

\[ \mu_r = \frac{1}{|C_r|}\sum_{i\in C_r} x_i. \]

The standard \(k\)-means algorithm alternates between two steps:

  1. Assign each point to the nearest centroid.
  2. Replace each centroid by the mean of the points assigned to it.

This decreases the objective at every iteration, but it can converge to a local minimum.

14.11.1 Spherical clustering

If cosine similarity is the desired geometry, it is common to normalize vectors before clustering. Applying \(k\)-means to normalized vectors is related to spherical clustering, where direction matters more than length.

14.12 9.11 Python example: small \(k\)-means implementation

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(7243)

def kmeans(X, k, n_iter=10):
    # Choose initial centroids from the data.
    initial = rng.choice(X.shape[0], size=k, replace=False)
    centroids = X[initial].copy()

    for _ in range(n_iter):
        distances = np.linalg.norm(X[:, None, :] - centroids[None, :, :], axis=2)
        labels = np.argmin(distances, axis=1)

        new_centroids = centroids.copy()
        for r in range(k):
            if np.any(labels == r):
                new_centroids[r] = X[labels == r].mean(axis=0)
        centroids = new_centroids

    return labels, centroids

labels, centroids = kmeans(X, k=3, n_iter=20)

for r in range(3):
    cluster_words = [w for w, lab in zip(words, labels) if lab == r]
    print(f"cluster {r}:", cluster_words)
cluster 0: ['cat', 'dog', 'lion', 'king', 'queen']
cluster 1: ['car', 'truck']
cluster 2: ['bus']

This example is intentionally small. The goal is not to obtain a perfect clustering, but to see the mathematical objective behind the algorithm.

14.13 9.12 High-dimensional caution: distance concentration

Embedding spaces used in NLP are often high-dimensional. High-dimensional geometry can differ sharply from low-dimensional intuition.

Suppose \(x,y\in R^m\) have independent standard normal coordinates. Then

\[ \|x-y\|_2^2 = \sum_{j=1}^m (x_j-y_j)^2. \]

Since \(x_j-y_j\sim N(0,2)\), the expected squared distance is

\[ E\|x-y\|_2^2 = 2m. \]

As \(m\) grows, distances between random points become increasingly concentrated around their mean. This can make nearest-neighbor structure unstable unless the embedding space has meaningful low-dimensional or clustered structure.

Another related issue is hubness: some points appear as nearest neighbors of many other points. In embedding spaces, hubs may arise because of frequency effects, anisotropy, or dominant common directions.

14.14 9.13 Python example: distance concentration

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(9)

def distance_summary(dim, n_pairs=5000):
    A = rng.normal(size=(n_pairs, dim))
    B = rng.normal(size=(n_pairs, dim))
    distances = np.linalg.norm(A - B, axis=1)
    return distances.mean(), distances.std(), distances.std() / distances.mean()

for dim in [2, 10, 50, 100, 300, 768]:
    mean_d, std_d, cv = distance_summary(dim)
    print(f"dim={dim:>3d}: mean={mean_d:.3f}, std={std_d:.3f}, coefficient of variation={cv:.3f}")

14.15 9.14 Semantic directions and analogy geometry

Embedding spaces often encode attributes as approximate directions. For example, the analogy pattern

\[ e_{king}-e_{man}+e_{woman}\approx e_{queen} \]

suggests that the difference vector

\[ b = e_{woman}-e_{man} \]

may represent a gender-related direction in that embedding space.

More generally, if we have paired examples \((a_i,b_i)\) representing an attribute change, we can estimate a direction

\[ d = \frac{1}{r}\sum_{i=1}^r (e_{b_i}-e_{a_i}). \]

Then the scalar projection of a word vector \(x\) onto this direction is

\[ \operatorname{score}_d(x)=\frac{x^T d}{\|d\|_2}. \]

This score measures how far \(x\) lies along the direction \(d\). Such directions can be useful for interpretation, but they must be used carefully: a direction learned from a small set of examples may reflect social bias, corpus artifacts, or confounding variables.

Bias as geometry. If an embedding space encodes an undesirable association as a direction or subspace, then downstream models that use the embeddings may inherit that association. Geometric analysis can reveal such structure, but it does not automatically solve the ethical or statistical problem.

14.16 9.15 Python example: a toy attribute direction

The example below constructs a toy singular-to-plural direction and projects each word onto it. This avoids making a social claim and lets us focus on the linear algebra.

import numpy as np

    [1.05, 0.20, 0.90],   # cats
    [0.90, 0.30, 0.05],   # dog
    [0.95, 0.30, 0.95],   # dogs

    [-0.75, 0.10, 0.85],  # cars
    [0.10, -0.90, 0.10],  # book

    [-0.15, -0.70, 0.90], # cities
])

14.17 9.16 Debiasing as projection: a mathematical sketch

A simple geometric debiasing operation removes the component of a vector along a direction \(b\ne 0\).

The projection of \(x\) onto the span of \(b\) is

\[ \operatorname{proj}_b(x)=\frac{x^T b}{b^T b}b. \]

The component orthogonal to \(b\) is

\[ x_{\perp}=x-\operatorname{proj}_b(x). \]

This operation guarantees

\[ x_{\perp}^T b = 0. \]

However, this does not guarantee that all bias has been removed. Bias may live in a higher-dimensional subspace, in nonlinear structure, or in downstream training data. Projection is a useful mathematical tool, but it is not a complete social or statistical solution.

14.18 9.17 Static versus contextual embedding geometry

The previous chapter focused mostly on static embeddings, where each word type has one vector:

\[ E:V\to R^m. \]

Modern transformer models produce contextual embeddings. A token’s vector depends on the full input sequence:

\[ H_t = F_t(w_1,\dots,w_T) \in R^m. \]

Thus the same word can have different vectors in different contexts. For example, bank in “river bank” and bank in “savings bank” may have different contextual embeddings.

The geometry of contextual embeddings is therefore a geometry of token occurrences rather than only word types. The data matrix may contain many rows for the same word in different contexts:

\[ X = \begin{bmatrix} - & H_{t_1}^{(1)T} & -\\ - & H_{t_2}^{(2)T} & -\\ & \vdots & \\ - & H_{t_n}^{(n)T} & - \end{bmatrix}. \]

This shift is important. Static embedding geometry asks: “Where is the word type?” Contextual embedding geometry asks: “Where is this occurrence of the word in this sentence?”

14.19 9.18 What can go wrong in embedding visualization?

Low-dimensional plots are useful, but they can be misleading. Common issues include:

  • Projection distortion: points close in \(R^2\) may not be close in \(R^m\).
  • Lost variance: a two-dimensional PCA plot may explain only a small fraction of total variance.
  • Metric mismatch: visualization may use Euclidean distance while retrieval uses cosine similarity.
  • Crowding: many points in high dimension must be packed into a small two-dimensional region.
  • Overinterpretation: visible clusters may be artifacts of projection, preprocessing, or selected examples.

A responsible visualization should report the preprocessing, projection method, metric, and explained variance when applicable.

14.21 9.20 Worked calculation

Let

\[ x=(2,1), \qquad y=(4,2), \qquad z=(1,2). \]

Then

\[ \cos(x,y)=\frac{2\cdot 4+1\cdot 2}{\sqrt{2^2+1^2}\sqrt{4^2+2^2}} =\frac{10}{\sqrt{5}\sqrt{20}} =1. \]

So \(x\) and \(y\) have exactly the same direction. Their Euclidean distance is

\[ \|x-y\|_2=\sqrt{(2-4)^2+(1-2)^2}=\sqrt{5}. \]

For \(z\),

\[ \cos(x,z)=\frac{2\cdot 1+1\cdot 2}{\sqrt{5}\sqrt{5}}=\frac{4}{5}=0.8, \]

while

\[ \|x-z\|_2=\sqrt{(2-1)^2+(1-2)^2}=\sqrt{2}. \]

Thus \(y\) is more similar to \(x\) by cosine similarity, but \(z\) is closer to \(x\) by Euclidean distance. This illustrates why metric choice changes nearest-neighbor rankings.

## 9.1 Why geometry matters in NLP

In earlier chapters, a word embedding was introduced as a map

$$
E:V \longrightarrow R^m,
$$

where $V$ is a vocabulary and $m$ is the embedding dimension.  For a token $w \in V$, the vector $E(w)$ is often written as $e_w \in R^m$.

If $V = \{w_1,\dots,w_n\}$, the embedding model produces a matrix

$$
X=
\begin{bmatrix}
- & e_{w_1}^T & -\\
- & e_{w_2}^T & -\\
& \vdots & \\
- & e_{w_n}^T & -
\end{bmatrix}
\in R^{n\times m}.
$$

The rows of $X$ form a finite point cloud in $R^m$.  Many NLP operations can then be written geometrically.

| NLP question | Geometric formulation |
|---|---|
| Which words are similar to `king`? | Find nearest neighbors of $e_{king}$. |
| Which documents match a query? | Find vectors with large inner product or cosine similarity to the query vector. |
| Are topics separated? | Look for clusters or low-dimensional structure. |
| What direction represents an attribute? | Estimate a vector direction $b$ and project embeddings onto $b$. |
| Can we visualize embeddings? | Project $R^m$ to $R^2$ using PCA, SVD, or another map. |

The important point is that geometry is not merely a visualization tool.  It is part of the model.  A statement such as “these words are close” is incomplete until we specify the representation, the metric, and any preprocessing such as centering or normalization.

::: {.math-card}
**Core viewpoint.**  An embedding space is a finite metric or inner-product structure induced by a representation map.  The geometry we observe is not intrinsic to language alone; it depends on the construction of $E$, the training data, the loss function, and the similarity measure.
:::

## 9.2 Similarity measures

Let $x,y \in R^m$ be two embedding vectors.

### Inner product

The inner product is

$$
\langle x,y\rangle = x^T y = \sum_{j=1}^m x_j y_j.
$$

Large inner products favor vectors that point in similar directions and also have large norms.  Therefore inner-product retrieval is sensitive to vector length.

### Euclidean distance

The Euclidean distance is

$$
d_2(x,y)=\|x-y\|_2=
\left(\sum_{j=1}^m (x_j-y_j)^2\right)^{1/2}.
$$

Small Euclidean distance means that the vectors are close as points in $R^m$.  It uses both direction and length.

### Cosine similarity

The cosine similarity is

$$
\cos(x,y)=\frac{x^T y}{\|x\|_2\|y\|_2},
\qquad x\ne 0,\ y\ne 0.
$$

Cosine similarity depends only on the angle between $x$ and $y$.  It is invariant under positive rescaling:

$$
\cos(cx,dy)=\cos(x,y),
\qquad c>0,\ d>0.
$$

This is useful when vector length is less meaningful than direction.  In many word and document embedding applications, cosine similarity is preferred for nearest-neighbor search.

### Relationship after normalization

Define the normalized vectors

$$
\hat{x}=\frac{x}{\|x\|_2},
\qquad
\hat{y}=\frac{y}{\|y\|_2}.
$$

Then

$$
\|\hat{x}-\hat{y}\|_2^2
=
\|\hat{x}\|_2^2+
\|\hat{y}\|_2^2-2\hat{x}^T\hat{y}
=2-2\cos(x,y).
$$

Thus, for normalized embeddings, minimizing Euclidean distance is equivalent to maximizing cosine similarity.

::: {.math-card}
**Metric choice matters.**  The nearest neighbors of a word can change when we switch from inner product to cosine similarity or Euclidean distance.  This is not a numerical accident.  It reflects different mathematical assumptions about whether vector length carries semantic information.
:::

## 9.3 Nearest neighbors as an optimization problem

Given embeddings $e_1,\dots,e_n \in R^m$ and a query vector $q \in R^m$, nearest-neighbor search solves

$$
\operatorname{NN}_s(q)
=
\arg\max_{1\le i\le n} s(q,e_i),
$$

where $s$ is a similarity function such as inner product or cosine similarity.  Equivalently, using a distance $d$,

$$
\operatorname{NN}_d(q)
=
\arg\min_{1\le i\le n} d(q,e_i).
$$

For top-$k$ retrieval, we return the $k$ largest scores or the $k$ smallest distances.

In matrix form, if $X\in R^{n\times m}$ stores the candidate embeddings as rows, inner-product scores are

$$
s = Xq \in R^n.
$$

For cosine similarity, define a row-normalized matrix $\widehat X$ and normalized query $\hat q$.  Then

$$
s = \widehat X \hat q.
$$

This simple matrix-vector multiplication is one reason embedding retrieval is computationally attractive.

## 9.6 Python example: nearest neighbors from scratch

The following example implements nearest-neighbor search without a machine learning library.  This is useful because it makes the metric choice explicit.

```{python}
#| label: py-ch09-nearest-neighbors
import numpy as np

words = ["cat", "dog", "lion", "car", "truck", "bus", "king", "queen"]
X = np.array([
    [1.00,  0.90],
    [1.10,  0.80],
    [1.45,  1.10],
    [-1.00, -0.80],
    [-1.25, -0.90],
    [-0.90, -1.15],
    [0.20,  1.85],
    [0.05,  1.70],
])

def cosine_scores(X, q):
    X_norm = X / np.linalg.norm(X, axis=1, keepdims=True)
    q_norm = q / np.linalg.norm(q)
    return X_norm @ q_norm

def euclidean_distances(X, q):
    return np.linalg.norm(X - q, axis=1)

def nearest_neighbors(query_word, metric="cosine", k=3):
    idx = words.index(query_word)
    q = X[idx]

    if metric == "cosine":
        values = cosine_scores(X, q)
        order = np.argsort(-values)
    elif metric == "inner":
        values = X @ q
        order = np.argsort(-values)
    elif metric == "euclidean":
        values = euclidean_distances(X, q)
        order = np.argsort(values)
    else:
        raise ValueError("metric must be cosine, inner, or euclidean")

    result = []
    for j in order:
        if j != idx:
            result.append((words[j], float(values[j])))
        if len(result) == k:
            break
    return result

print("cosine:", nearest_neighbors("cat", metric="cosine"))
print("inner product:", nearest_neighbors("cat", metric="inner"))
print("euclidean:", nearest_neighbors("cat", metric="euclidean"))
```

The same vectors can produce different neighbor rankings.  In a real embedding model, such differences can affect search results, clustering results, and qualitative interpretations.

## 9.7 Centering, normalization, and anisotropy

Embedding spaces often have strong global directions.  For example, many vectors may share a large common component.  This can make most pairwise cosine similarities positive and reduce the contrast between meaningful neighbors and unrelated words.

Let the rows of $X\in R^{n\times m}$ be embeddings.  The sample mean vector is

$$
\bar{x}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i.
$$

The centered embedding matrix is

$$
X_c = X - {\bf 1}\bar{x}^T,
$$

where ${\bf 1}\in R^n$ is the vector of all ones.  Centering removes the global mean direction.

Row normalization gives

$$
\tilde{x}_i=\frac{x_i}{\|x_i\|_2}.
$$

Depending on the task, one may use raw vectors, centered vectors, normalized vectors, or centered-and-normalized vectors.  These choices change the geometry.

::: {.math-card}
**Anisotropy.**  A point cloud is anisotropic when its variance is much larger in some directions than in others.  In embedding spaces, anisotropy can cause a few dominant directions to affect many similarity computations.
:::

A simple diagnostic is to inspect the singular values of the centered matrix.  If the first few singular values dominate, then much of the variation lies in a low-dimensional subspace.

## 9.8 PCA for embedding spaces

PCA is a standard method for projecting high-dimensional embeddings to a lower-dimensional space.  It should be understood as a variance-preserving linear projection, not as a perfect map of semantic meaning.

Let $X\in R^{n\times m}$ be the embedding matrix, and let

$$
X_c = X - {\bf 1}\bar{x}^T
$$

be the centered matrix.  The sample covariance matrix is

$$
S=\frac{1}{n-1}X_c^T X_c \in R^{m\times m}.
$$

PCA finds orthonormal directions $v_1,\dots,v_r$ solving

$$
v_1 = \arg\max_{\|v\|_2=1} v^T S v,
$$

and, for $j>1$,

$$
v_j = \arg\max_{\|v\|_2=1,\ v\perp v_1,\dots,v_{j-1}} v^T S v.
$$

The solutions are eigenvectors of $S$:

$$
S v_j = \lambda_j v_j,
\qquad
\lambda_1\ge \lambda_2\ge \cdots \ge 0.
$$

Equivalently, use the singular value decomposition

$$
X_c = U\Sigma V^T.
$$

The columns of $V$ are the principal directions, and the low-dimensional PCA coordinates are

$$
Z_k = X_c V_k \in R^{n\times k},
$$

where $V_k=[v_1,\dots,v_k]$.

The fraction of variance explained by the first $k$ components is

$$
\frac{\lambda_1+\cdots+\lambda_k}{\lambda_1+\cdots+\lambda_m}
=
\frac{\sigma_1^2+\cdots+\sigma_k^2}{\sigma_1^2+\cdots+\sigma_r^2}.
$$

### PCA is useful but limited

PCA preserves large-variance linear structure.  It does not necessarily preserve all nearest-neighbor relationships, clusters, or nonlinear geometry.  A two-dimensional PCA plot is a useful diagnostic, but it should not be treated as the embedding space itself.

## 9.9 Python example: PCA by SVD

```{python}
#| label: py-ch09-pca-svd
import numpy as np

# Center the toy embedding matrix.
X_centered = X - X.mean(axis=0, keepdims=True)

# Singular value decomposition.
U, singular_values, Vt = np.linalg.svd(X_centered, full_matrices=False)

# Two-dimensional PCA coordinates.
Z = X_centered @ Vt[:2].T

print("Singular values:", np.round(singular_values, 3))
print("PCA coordinates:")
for word, z in zip(words, Z):
    print(f"{word:>6s}: ({z[0]: .3f}, {z[1]: .3f})")
```

For this toy example, the original vectors are already two-dimensional.  In a real embedding model, $m$ might be 100, 300, 768, or larger.  PCA gives a low-dimensional view of this higher-dimensional structure.

## 9.10 Clustering embeddings

Clustering asks whether embedding vectors can be partitioned into groups with similar geometry.  A common model is $k$-means clustering.

Given embeddings $x_1,\dots,x_n\in R^m$, the $k$-means objective is

$$
\min_{C_1,\dots,C_k,\ \mu_1,\dots,\mu_k}
\sum_{r=1}^k \sum_{i\in C_r} \|x_i-\mu_r\|_2^2,
$$

where $C_1,\dots,C_k$ partition $\{1,
\dots,n\}$ and $\mu_r\in R^m$ is the centroid of cluster $r$.

For fixed clusters, the optimal centroid is the sample mean:

$$
\mu_r = \frac{1}{|C_r|}\sum_{i\in C_r} x_i.
$$

The standard $k$-means algorithm alternates between two steps:

1. Assign each point to the nearest centroid.
2. Replace each centroid by the mean of the points assigned to it.

This decreases the objective at every iteration, but it can converge to a local minimum.

### Spherical clustering

If cosine similarity is the desired geometry, it is common to normalize vectors before clustering.  Applying $k$-means to normalized vectors is related to spherical clustering, where direction matters more than length.

## 9.11 Python example: small $k$-means implementation

```{python}
#| label: py-ch09-kmeans-from-scratch
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(7243)

def kmeans(X, k, n_iter=10):
    # Choose initial centroids from the data.
    initial = rng.choice(X.shape[0], size=k, replace=False)
    centroids = X[initial].copy()

    for _ in range(n_iter):
        distances = np.linalg.norm(X[:, None, :] - centroids[None, :, :], axis=2)
        labels = np.argmin(distances, axis=1)

        new_centroids = centroids.copy()
        for r in range(k):
            if np.any(labels == r):
                new_centroids[r] = X[labels == r].mean(axis=0)
        centroids = new_centroids

    return labels, centroids

labels, centroids = kmeans(X, k=3, n_iter=20)

for r in range(3):
    cluster_words = [w for w, lab in zip(words, labels) if lab == r]
    print(f"cluster {r}:", cluster_words)
```

This example is intentionally small.  The goal is not to obtain a perfect clustering, but to see the mathematical objective behind the algorithm.

## 9.12 High-dimensional caution: distance concentration

Embedding spaces used in NLP are often high-dimensional.  High-dimensional geometry can differ sharply from low-dimensional intuition.

Suppose $x,y\in R^m$ have independent standard normal coordinates.  Then

$$
\|x-y\|_2^2 = \sum_{j=1}^m (x_j-y_j)^2.
$$

Since $x_j-y_j\sim N(0,2)$, the expected squared distance is

$$
E\|x-y\|_2^2 = 2m.
$$

As $m$ grows, distances between random points become increasingly concentrated around their mean.  This can make nearest-neighbor structure unstable unless the embedding space has meaningful low-dimensional or clustered structure.

Another related issue is **hubness**: some points appear as nearest neighbors of many other points.  In embedding spaces, hubs may arise because of frequency effects, anisotropy, or dominant common directions.

## 9.13 Python example: distance concentration

```{python}
#| label: py-ch09-distance-concentration
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(9)

def distance_summary(dim, n_pairs=5000):
    A = rng.normal(size=(n_pairs, dim))
    B = rng.normal(size=(n_pairs, dim))
    distances = np.linalg.norm(A - B, axis=1)
    return distances.mean(), distances.std(), distances.std() / distances.mean()

for dim in [2, 10, 50, 100, 300, 768]:
    mean_d, std_d, cv = distance_summary(dim)
    print(f"dim={dim:>3d}: mean={mean_d:.3f}, std={std_d:.3f}, coefficient of variation={cv:.3f}")
```

The coefficient of variation decreases as dimension increases.  This does not mean all high-dimensional embeddings are useless.  It means that meaningful structure must come from learned correlations, training objectives, and data geometry rather than from random high-dimensional distance alone.

## 9.14 Semantic directions and analogy geometry

Embedding spaces often encode attributes as approximate directions.  For example, the analogy pattern

$$
e_{king}-e_{man}+e_{woman}\approx e_{queen}
$$

suggests that the difference vector

$$
b = e_{woman}-e_{man}
$$

may represent a gender-related direction in that embedding space.

More generally, if we have paired examples $(a_i,b_i)$ representing an attribute change, we can estimate a direction

$$
d = \frac{1}{r}\sum_{i=1}^r (e_{b_i}-e_{a_i}).
$$

Then the scalar projection of a word vector $x$ onto this direction is

$$
\operatorname{score}_d(x)=\frac{x^T d}{\|d\|_2}.
$$

This score measures how far $x$ lies along the direction $d$.  Such directions can be useful for interpretation, but they must be used carefully: a direction learned from a small set of examples may reflect social bias, corpus artifacts, or confounding variables.

::: {.math-card}
**Bias as geometry.**  If an embedding space encodes an undesirable association as a direction or subspace, then downstream models that use the embeddings may inherit that association.  Geometric analysis can reveal such structure, but it does not automatically solve the ethical or statistical problem.
:::

## 9.15 Python example: a toy attribute direction

The example below constructs a toy **singular-to-plural** direction and projects each word onto it.  This avoids making a social claim and lets us focus on the linear algebra.

```{python}
#| label: py-ch09-attribute-direction
import numpy as np

morph_words = ["cat", "cats", "dog", "dogs", "car", "cars", "book", "books", "city", "cities"]
Y = np.array([
    [1.00, 0.20, 0.00],   # cat
    [1.05, 0.20, 0.90],   # cats
    [0.90, 0.30, 0.05],   # dog
    [0.95, 0.30, 0.95],   # dogs
    [-0.80, 0.10, 0.00],  # car
    [-0.75, 0.10, 0.85],  # cars
    [0.10, -0.90, 0.10],  # book
    [0.15, -0.90, 0.95],  # books
    [-0.20, -0.70, 0.05], # city
    [-0.15, -0.70, 0.90], # cities
])

pairs = [("cat", "cats"), ("dog", "dogs"), ("car", "cars"), ("book", "books")]
plural_direction = np.zeros(Y.shape[1])
for singular, plural in pairs:
    plural_direction += Y[morph_words.index(plural)] - Y[morph_words.index(singular)]
plural_direction /= len(pairs)
plural_direction /= np.linalg.norm(plural_direction)

scores = Y @ plural_direction
order = np.argsort(-scores)

print("Projection onto the toy singular-to-plural direction:")
for j in order:
    print(f"{morph_words[j]:>6s}: {scores[j]: .3f}")
```

In real applications, one should estimate directions from multiple pairs, test sensitivity to the choice of pairs, and evaluate whether the direction is stable across corpora and model families.

## 9.16 Debiasing as projection: a mathematical sketch

A simple geometric debiasing operation removes the component of a vector along a direction $b\ne 0$.

The projection of $x$ onto the span of $b$ is

$$
\operatorname{proj}_b(x)=\frac{x^T b}{b^T b}b.
$$

The component orthogonal to $b$ is

$$
x_{\perp}=x-\operatorname{proj}_b(x).
$$

This operation guarantees

$$
x_{\perp}^T b = 0.
$$

However, this does not guarantee that all bias has been removed.  Bias may live in a higher-dimensional subspace, in nonlinear structure, or in downstream training data.  Projection is a useful mathematical tool, but it is not a complete social or statistical solution.

## 9.17 Static versus contextual embedding geometry

The previous chapter focused mostly on static embeddings, where each word type has one vector:

$$
E:V\to R^m.
$$

Modern transformer models produce contextual embeddings.  A token's vector depends on the full input sequence:

$$
H_t = F_t(w_1,\dots,w_T) \in R^m.
$$

Thus the same word can have different vectors in different contexts.  For example, `bank` in “river bank” and `bank` in “savings bank” may have different contextual embeddings.

The geometry of contextual embeddings is therefore a geometry of token occurrences rather than only word types.  The data matrix may contain many rows for the same word in different contexts:

$$
X =
\begin{bmatrix}
- & H_{t_1}^{(1)T} & -\\
- & H_{t_2}^{(2)T} & -\\
& \vdots & \\
- & H_{t_n}^{(n)T} & -
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

This shift is important.  Static embedding geometry asks: “Where is the word type?”  Contextual embedding geometry asks: “Where is this occurrence of the word in this sentence?”

## 9.18 What can go wrong in embedding visualization?

Low-dimensional plots are useful, but they can be misleading.  Common issues include:

- **Projection distortion:** points close in $R^2$ may not be close in $R^m$.
- **Lost variance:** a two-dimensional PCA plot may explain only a small fraction of total variance.
- **Metric mismatch:** visualization may use Euclidean distance while retrieval uses cosine similarity.
- **Crowding:** many points in high dimension must be packed into a small two-dimensional region.
- **Overinterpretation:** visible clusters may be artifacts of projection, preprocessing, or selected examples.

A responsible visualization should report the preprocessing, projection method, metric, and explained variance when applicable.

## 9.20 Worked calculation

Let

$$
x=(2,1),
\qquad
 y=(4,2),
\qquad
 z=(1,2).
$$

Then

$$
\cos(x,y)=\frac{2\cdot 4+1\cdot 2}{\sqrt{2^2+1^2}\sqrt{4^2+2^2}}
=\frac{10}{\sqrt{5}\sqrt{20}}
=1.
$$

So $x$ and $y$ have exactly the same direction.  Their Euclidean distance is

$$
\|x-y\|_2=\sqrt{(2-4)^2+(1-2)^2}=\sqrt{5}.
$$

For $z$,

$$
\cos(x,z)=\frac{2\cdot 1+1\cdot 2}{\sqrt{5}\sqrt{5}}=\frac{4}{5}=0.8,
$$

while

$$
\|x-z\|_2=\sqrt{(2-1)^2+(1-2)^2}=\sqrt{2}.
$$

Thus $y$ is more similar to $x$ by cosine similarity, but $z$ is closer to $x$ by Euclidean distance.  This illustrates why metric choice changes nearest-neighbor rankings.

## 9.24 What to remember

- Embedding geometry depends on the representation, preprocessing, and metric.
- Cosine similarity compares directions; Euclidean distance compares positions; inner products also reward large norms.
- PCA is a linear variance-preserving projection, not a perfect picture of semantic space.
- Clustering embeddings means optimizing a geometric objective, not discovering guaranteed natural categories.
- High-dimensional spaces can exhibit distance concentration, hubness, and anisotropy.
- Linear directions can be useful for interpretation, but they can also reveal or amplify bias.
- Contextual embeddings move from word-type geometry to token-occurrence geometry.
